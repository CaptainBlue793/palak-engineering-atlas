Design Video Streaming

YouTube receives hundreds of hours of video every minute. Netflix alone is more than 10% of all internet traffic coming down to users. A video system is really two pipelines. One is a heavy background pipeline that turns a single upload into dozens of optimized files. The other is a delivery path that streams petabytes a day through CDNs, adjusting quality to each viewer's network second by second.

  • Estimate video storage and bandwidth, and see why CDNs are essential
  • Design uploads that can resume, and convert video with many machines at once
  • Understand codecs, quality versions, segments and playlists (HLS/DASH)
  • Build adaptive quality streaming, and compare the algorithms that choose the quality
  • Serve the world with CDNs and caches placed inside internet providers
  • Handle view counts, content protection (DRM), live streaming and rarely watched videos

STEP 1 / 6

Requirements

🛠️ Functional

✓Creators upload videos (up to ~10 GB)
✓Viewers watch on phones, TVs and browsers, on any kind of connection
✓Video metadata: title, description, thumbnails, view counts
✓Resume where you left off; seek anywhere
✓Out of scope: recommendations, comments, monetization

📏 Non-functional

✓Playback starts in < 2 s. The most important quality measure is how often video pauses to reload (rebuffering)
✓Far more reads than writes: a video is uploaded once and watched millions of times
✓Uploads are processed within minutes, and never lost
✓Works worldwide and stays cheap: bandwidth is the biggest bill

1 billion hours × 3,600 s × 2.5 Mbps ÷ 8 ≈ 1.1 exabytes a day. Every thousand people watching at once need about 2.5 Gbps (1,000 × 2.5 Mbps), and at peak there are tens of millions watching at once.

The other numbers, step by step:

  • Uploads per day: 500 hours/minute × 1,440 minutes/day = 720K hours/day.
  • Bytes per stored hour: each video is kept in several renditions (Step 3). Averaged over all uploads (most are not 4K), their bitrates add up to about 9 Mbps. 9 Mbps × 3,600 s ÷ 8 bits per byte ≈ 4 GB per hour of video.
  • New storage per day: 720K h × 4 GB ≈ 2.9 PB, so ~3 PB. Keep 3 copies (or use erasure coding, Chapter 9, at ~1.5×) and add the originals, and you're adding several petabytes every day, over an exabyte a year. That is why rarely watched files move to cheap storage.
  • Viewers at once: 1B hours watched ÷ 24 hours ≈ 42M people watching at any moment on average, more at peak. 42M × 2.5 Mbps ≈ 100 Tbps on average.

No single data centre can push that much data. Delivery has to come from caches inside or right next to internet providers. And every 1% better compression saves millions of dollars.

STEP 3 / 6

Video basics you need

🎞️

Codecs & containers

A codec is the method used to compress the frames. H.264 works everywhere. VP9 and HEVC are ~40% smaller. AV1 is ~30% smaller again, but slow to encode. A container (MP4, fMP4, WebM) is the file format that wraps the audio, video and details together.

🪜

Rendition ladder

Each video is encoded several times, at different sizes and data rates: 240p at 0.4 Mbps, 1080p at 6 Mbps, 4K at 16 Mbps, and so on. These versions are called renditions. With per-title encoding you tune the set for each video. A flat cartoon needs far fewer bits than a concert full of confetti.

🧩

Segments

Each rendition is cut into 2–6 second segments. The cuts land on keyframes (full frames that don't depend on earlier ones), so each segment can play on its own. The player can switch quality at any segment boundary, and jumping to a point in the video only needs one segment.

📜

Manifests: HLS & DASH

A manifest is a playlist file (.m3u8 for HLS, .mpd for DASH). It lists the renditions and the URL of every segment. The player just downloads ordinary HTTP files. That's exactly why normal CDNs can cache video.

# master.m3u8 — HLS multivariant playlist
#EXT-X-STREAM-INF:BANDWIDTH=400000,RESOLUTION=426x240,CODECS="avc1.42e01e,mp4a.40.2"
240p/playlist.m3u8
#EXT-X-STREAM-INF:BANDWIDTH=3000000,RESOLUTION=1280x720
720p/playlist.m3u8
#EXT-X-STREAM-INF:BANDWIDTH=6000000,RESOLUTION=1920x1080
1080p/playlist.m3u8

# 720p/playlist.m3u8 — media playlist
#EXT-X-TARGETDURATION:4
#EXTINF:4.0,  seg_00001.m4s
#EXTINF:4.0,  seg_00002.m4s   # … one small file per 4 seconds

A sized example. A 2-hour movie cut into 4-second segments has 7,200 ÷ 4 = 1,800 segments per rendition. The 720p rendition at 3 Mbps makes each segment 3 × 4 ÷ 8 = 1.5 MB. So playing the movie means ~1,800 small HTTP downloads of about 1.5 MB each, and any two viewers of the same movie ask for exactly the same files. That sameness is what makes caching work.

Choosing the segment length. Shorter segments (2 s) let the player change quality sooner and cut delay for live video. But each segment must start with a keyframe, which is large, so compression gets a bit worse, and there are twice as many requests. Longer segments (6 s) compress better and mean fewer requests, but the player reacts more slowly to a network drop. 4 seconds is a common middle ground for on-demand video.

Choosing codecs. Why not encode everything in AV1, the smallest? Encoding cost is paid once per video, but bandwidth savings are paid back on every view. A video watched 10M hours at 2.5 Mbps sends ~11 PB; 30% smaller saves ~3 PB, easily worth hours of extra computing. A video watched 50 times saves almost nothing. So every video gets H.264 (it plays on every device), and the expensive newer codecs are added only once a video becomes popular.

STEP 4 / 6

The upload & processing pipeline

From raw upload to playable videoStep-through
  1. The app asks to start an upload. It gets a video ID and temporary upload links for each part of the file (Chapter 9), and sends the parts straight to storage. If the connection drops, it carries on from the last part that was confirmed. That matters a lot for multi-GB files sent from phones.
  2. The parts are joined back into the original file in the raw bucket, and the status becomes UPLOADED. We keep the original forever, so we can re-encode later with better codecs (AV1 today, something better tomorrow) from the best possible source.
  3. An ObjectCreated event starts a workflow (Temporal, Step Functions, or an in-house scheduler). The steps form a DAG: a plan where each task runs once its inputs are ready. First it inspects the file (length, resolution, frame rate, audio tracks), then splits it into chunks that start on keyframes.
  4. Every combination of chunk and rendition is its own task, run on a big pool of machines (CPUs, GPUs, or custom chips like YouTube's Argos VCUs). A one-hour video becomes thousands of small tasks that finish in minutes instead of hours. If one task fails, only that task is retried.
  5. The encoded chunks are joined back together and packaged into segments plus HLS/DASH playlists. Where required, they're encrypted for DRM (Widevine, FairPlay, PlayReady).
  6. Side branches of the workflow run ML classifiers that check the content rules. They also fingerprint the audio and video and compare it with material owned by rights holders (Content ID). The results can block, restrict or monetize the video, just before or shortly after it goes live.
  7. The segments land in the origin storage behind the CDN, and the status becomes READY. The lower-quality versions usually go live first, so people can watch quickly while 4K and AV1 finish later.

The arithmetic behind the simulator. A 20-minute video in 1-minute chunks with 5 renditions is 20 × 5 = 100 tasks. At ~2 minutes each, one machine working through them in turn needs 200 minutes. With 16 workers, the tasks run in ⌈100 ÷ 16⌉ = 7 rounds of 2 minutes, 14 minutes, plus about a minute to split the file and stitch the results. Add workers and the wait keeps dropping until there are more workers than tasks.

Why a workflow engine, and what it must survive

A simple queue of tasks could run the transcodes. The workflow engine earns its place because the steps depend on each other (packaging waits for every chunk), and because someone must track which of thousands of tasks are done. Here are the failures it has to handle:

  • A worker dies mid-task. Each task is handed out with a time limit (a lease). If no “done” arrives in time, the task is given to another worker. Outputs are written to a fixed name like video123/720p/chunk_0042, so running a task twice just overwrites the same file.
  • The same upload event arrives twice. Storage events can be delivered more than once. The workflow is keyed by video ID, so starting it a second time is a no-op.
  • A corrupt or unsupported file. Retrying won't help. After a few failures, mark the video FAILED, stop, and tell the creator what went wrong instead of retrying forever.
  • Abandoned uploads. A creator closes the app halfway through a 10 GB upload (1,000 parts of 10 MB). A storage lifecycle rule deletes unfinished multipart uploads after a few days, so orphaned parts don't pile up on the bill.
STEP 5 / 6

Playback: adaptive bitrate streaming

A viewer's network keeps changing: Wi-Fi to 4G, a train tunnel, a sibling starting a download. So the player keeps a buffer: video already downloaded but not yet played. Before each segment, an ABR algorithm (adaptive bitrate) picks which quality to fetch next.

Pick too high and the buffer empties, so playback stops to reload: that's rebuffering, the spinner. Pick too low and the picture is blurrier than it needed to be.

Why picking too high empties the buffer — a worked example. Your network gives 4 Mbps and the buffer holds 10 seconds. The player asks for 1080p at 6 Mbps. One 4-second segment is 6 × 4 = 24 megabits, which takes 24 ÷ 4 = 6 seconds to download. While downloading, 6 seconds of video play but only 4 arrive, so the buffer shrinks by 2 seconds per segment. After five segments it is empty and the spinner appears.

The two basic ABR strategies decide differently:

  • Throughput-based: measure how fast recent segments downloaded and pick the highest rendition below a safety margin, such as 80% of that. With 4 Mbps measured, 80% is 3.2 Mbps, so it picks 720p at 3 Mbps. The weak spot: measurements are noisy, so quality jumps around.
  • Buffer-based: ignore the speed estimate and look at the buffer. For example, below 10 s buffered pick the lowest quality; above 30 s pick the highest; in between, scale up in steps. A full buffer means downloads are keeping up, so it's safe to aim higher.

Starting fast. To begin playing within 2 seconds, players usually start at a low rendition. A 4-second 240p segment at 0.4 Mbps is only ~200 KB, which arrives almost instantly, then quality climbs as the buffer fills.

Delivering exabytes: CDNs inside ISPs

🏢

Embedded caches

Netflix's Open Connect and Google's Global Cache put cache servers packed with storage inside internet providers' networks, for free. The traffic never crosses the expensive long-distance internet backbone. The provider saves money, and the viewer gets video faster.

🌙

Proactive pre-positioning

Viewing is fairly predictable: you know which titles will be popular tomorrow. So during quiet hours, the caches are filled with the files they'll need. At peak time, almost every byte then comes from the cache.

🧭

Steering

When you press play, a control service in the cloud checks you're allowed to watch. It picks the best nearby caches that are healthy and actually have the file, and returns their URLs. If one fails, the player switches to the next.

🧊

The long tail

Most of YouTube's catalogue is hardly ever watched. Keep the popular renditions at the edge. Serve rarely watched videos from regional caches or from the origin. Move old, unwatched renditions to cheaper, slower storage. You can even delete them and re-encode from the original when someone asks.

Why the hit ratio decides the bill. Take the ~100 Tbps average from Step 2. If ISP caches serve 95% of the bytes, the origin and backbone carry 5 Tbps. If the hit ratio slips to 90%, that doubles to 10 Tbps. A few points of hit ratio is the difference between one origin fleet and two.

Edge case: the premiere. When a hit series releases at midnight, millions press play in the same minute. If the caches are empty, every one of them misses and runs to the origin at once (a cache stampede, Chapter 6). Two defences: fill the caches before the release (pre-positioning), and let each cache merge simultaneous misses for the same segment into one origin fetch (request coalescing), handing the result to everyone waiting.

STEP 6 / 6

Deep dives

Why not just run UPDATE videos SET views = views + 1? A viral video can get 100K views a second, and every one of those updates would queue for the lock on the same row. Hundreds of thousands of view events a second must not touch the video table directly. Players send regular “still watching” events to Kafka. A stream processor drops repeats (same user, same video, short time window), filters out bots, and totals views per video per minute (Chapter 18, Chapter 40). The public counter is refreshed from those totals every so often, and may lag or freeze on purpose while spam checks run. For unique viewers, use HyperLogLog (Chapter 20), a compact way to estimate how many distinct people watched.

Live video can't be processed ahead of time. The chain is: receive the stream (RTMP/SRT/WebRTC) → transcode in real time → package into segments → CDN. The player keeps fetching a playlist that only holds the latest few segments. Plain HLS runs 15–30 s behind real time. LL-HLS/LL-DASH, which send partial segments as they're produced, get to 2–5 s. When people need to interact (auctions, co-streaming), WebRTC gets under a second, but costs more per viewer.

Players send (user, video, position) now and then. Keep only the newest value per user and video in a wide-column or key-value store, where the last write simply wins. Losing a few seconds of progress is fine. Have the player save every 10–30 seconds and on pause, instead of once a second per viewer.

Private or paid content uses signed CDN links that expire quickly (or cookies, Chapter 9), so a copied link stops working. Big studios also require DRM: the segments are encrypted (CENC), and the player gets the decryption keys from a license server, but only after the backend checks that the user paid, how many devices they use, and which region they're in.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Transcoding a whole file on one machine. Split it into chunks. A two-hour video should take minutes, not hours.
  • Serving video from the origin servers. The bill is bandwidth, not computing. CDN and provider caches are essential.
  • Ignoring the long tail. Most videos are rarely watched. Keeping every version on fast storage wastes a huge amount of space.
🎤
Interview drill

Design: A creator uploads a 4K, 3-hour video. What happens before anyone can watch it?

How a strong answer sounds
The file goes straight to object storage, in parts that can resume after a dropped connection, and the original is kept forever. An ObjectCreated event starts a workflow. It inspects the file, splits it into chunks that begin on keyframes, and spreads one transcode task per chunk and rendition across a pool of machines. Then it packages the segments and playlists (HLS/DASH), makes thumbnails and captions, and runs moderation and copyright matching in parallel branches. The lower qualities publish first, so the video is watchable within minutes while 4K and AV1 finish.

Debug: Users in one country report constant buffering, everyone else is fine.

How a strong answer sounds
First separate delivery from encoding. Check the CDN's cache hit ratio, which edge locations or provider caches serve that country, and whether requests are falling back to a far-away origin. Then look at the player's quality choices: if bandwidth there really is low, is the set of renditions missing a low-quality one? Finally, check segment and playlist sizes, and whether the player starts at too high a quality. The fix is usually loading the content into that region's caches in advance, not changing the encoder.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • A video is written once and read millions of times. So tune processing for throughput, and delivery for cost and speed.
  • Upload in parts that can resume after a dropped connection. Keep the original so you can re-encode later.
  • Transcode as many small tasks, one per chunk and rendition, spread over a large pool of machines.
  • Package each rendition into short segments with HLS/DASH playlists, so ordinary HTTP CDNs can serve them.
  • ABR algorithms pick each segment's quality from measured bandwidth and how full the buffer is, to avoid pauses.
  • Bandwidth is the main cost. Caches inside internet providers, filled during quiet hours, deliver most of the bytes.
  • Count views by totalling events in a stream. Store watch progress with “last write wins”. Protect content with signed URLs and DRM.

Check your understanding

Why split videos into 2–6 second segments aligned on keyframes?

Each segment can be decoded on its own. That allows quality switching, fast seeking, and caching in a CDN.

A 2-hour 4K upload takes 10 hours to transcode on one machine. What's the most effective fix?

Once it's split into chunks, all the pieces can be transcoded at the same time. The time you wait drops roughly in step with the number of machines.

A buffer-based ABR algorithm sees the buffer drop to 3 seconds. What does it do?

When the buffer is nearly empty, the buffer-based algorithm plays it safe. Quality rises again as the buffer refills.

Why do Netflix and YouTube place cache servers inside ISP networks?

At an exabyte a day, serving from inside the viewer's own internet provider is the only affordable option.

How should view counts be updated for a viral video with 50,000 views per second?

Updating one row per view makes that row a hot spot. Totalling events in the background absorbs the load and filters out bots.
