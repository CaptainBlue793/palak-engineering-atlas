Design Video Streaming

YouTube receives hundreds of hours of video every minute; Netflix accounts for a double-digit share of all downstream internet traffic. Video systems are dominated by two pipelines: a heavy, asynchronous processing pipeline that turns one upload into dozens of optimized files, and a delivery path that streams petabytes a day through CDNs while adapting quality to each viewer's network second by second.

  • Estimate video storage and bandwidth — and see why CDNs are mandatory
  • Design resumable uploads and a parallel transcoding DAG
  • Understand codecs, renditions, segments and manifests (HLS/DASH)
  • Implement adaptive bitrate streaming and compare ABR algorithms
  • Serve globally with CDNs and ISP-embedded caches
  • Handle view counts, DRM, live streaming and cold content
STEP 1 / 6

Requirements

🛠️ Functional

✓Creators upload videos (up to ~10 GB)
✓Viewers watch on phones, TVs and browsers, on any network
✓Video metadata: title, description, thumbnails, view counts
✓Resume where you left off; seek anywhere
✓Out of scope: recommendations, comments, monetization

📏 Non-functional

✓Playback starts in < 2 s; rebuffering is the #1 quality metric
✓Extreme read:write ratio — a video is uploaded once, watched millions of times
✓Uploads processed within minutes; highly durable (never lose an upload)
✓Global, and cost-efficient: bandwidth is the biggest bill

1 billion hours × 3,600 s × 2.5 Mbps ÷ 8 ≈ 1.1 exabytes a day — an average of ~100 Gbps… per thousand concurrent streams, and tens of millions of concurrent streams at peak. No origin data centre can push that. Delivery must happen from caches inside or next to ISPs, and every percent of compression efficiency is worth millions.

STEP 3 / 6

Video basics you need

🎞️

Codecs & containers

A codec compresses frames (H.264 — universal; VP9/HEVC — ~40% smaller; AV1 — ~30% smaller again, but slow to encode). A container (MP4, fMP4, WebM) packages audio + video + metadata.

🪜

Rendition ladder

Each video is encoded at several resolution/bitrate pairs: 240p @ 0.4 Mbps … 1080p @ 6 Mbps … 4K @ 16 Mbps. Per-title encoding tunes the ladder per video — a cartoon needs far fewer bits than a confetti-filled concert.

🧩

Segments

Each rendition is cut into 2–6 second segments aligned on keyframes (GOP boundaries), so the player can switch quality at any segment boundary and seeking only needs one segment.

📜

Manifests: HLS & DASH

A manifest (.m3u8 for HLS, .mpd for DASH) lists the renditions and their segment URLs. The player fetches plain HTTP files — which is exactly why ordinary CDNs can cache video.

STEP 4 / 6

The upload & processing pipeline

From raw upload to playable videoStep-through
  1. The app requests an upload session, gets a video ID and presigned multipart URLs (Chapter 9), and uploads parts directly to storage. A dropped connection resumes from the last acknowledged part — essential for multi-GB files from phones.
  2. Parts are assembled into the original file in the raw bucket; metadata status becomes UPLOADED. We keep the original so future codecs (AV1 today, something better tomorrow) can be re-encoded from the best source.
  3. An ObjectCreated event starts a workflow (Temporal, Step Functions, or an in-house DAG scheduler). It probes the file (duration, resolution, frame rate, audio tracks) and splits it into keyframe-aligned chunks.
  4. Each chunk × rendition is an independent task on a large worker pool (CPU, GPU, or custom ASICs like YouTube's Argos VCUs). A one-hour video becomes thousands of small tasks that finish in minutes instead of hours. Failed tasks retry individually.
  5. Encoded chunks are stitched and packaged into segments + HLS/DASH manifests, encrypted for DRM where required (Widevine, FairPlay, PlayReady).
  6. Side branches of the DAG run ML policy classifiers and audio/video fingerprint matching against rights-holder references (Content ID). Results can block, restrict or monetize the video before or shortly after publishing.
  7. Segments land in origin storage behind the CDN, and status flips to READY. Lower renditions often publish first so the video is watchable quickly while 4K/AV1 finish later.

STEP 5 / 6

Playback: adaptive bitrate streaming

The viewer's network changes constantly — Wi-Fi to 4G, a train tunnel, a sibling starting a download. The player keeps a buffer of downloaded-but-not-yet-played video and, before each segment, an ABR algorithm picks the rendition to fetch next. Pick too high and the buffer drains → rebuffering (the spinner). Pick too low and the picture is needlessly blurry.

Delivering exabytes: CDNs inside ISPs

🏢

Embedded caches

Netflix Open Connect and Google Global Cache place storage-dense cache servers inside ISP networks for free. Traffic never crosses the expensive internet backbone; the ISP saves transit cost, the viewer gets lower latency.

🌙

Proactive pre-positioning

Viewing is predictable: tomorrow's popular titles are known. During off-peak hours, caches are filled with the files they'll need — so at peak time almost every byte is a cache hit.

🧭

Steering

On play, a control-plane service (running in the cloud) authorizes the user, picks the best nearby caches that actually hold the file and are healthy, and returns URLs. The player fails over between them.

🧊

The long tail

Most of YouTube's catalogue is rarely watched. Keep popular renditions at the edge; serve cold videos from regional caches or origin; move old, unwatched renditions to colder storage — or even delete them and re-transcode from the original on demand.

STEP 6 / 6

Deep dives

Hundreds of thousands of view events per second must not hit the video metadata table directly. Players emit heartbeat events to Kafka; a stream processor dedupes (same user, same video, short window), filters bots, and aggregates counts per video per minute (Chapter 18, Chapter 40). The public counter is updated periodically from those aggregates — and can deliberately lag or freeze while spam checks run. Unique viewer counts use HyperLogLog (Chapter 20).

Live video can't be transcoded ahead of time: ingest (RTMP/SRT/WebRTC) → real-time transcoder → segment packager → CDN, with the player fetching a sliding-window playlist. Standard HLS has 15–30 s latency; LL-HLS/LL-DASH with partial segments and chunked transfer get to 2–5 s; interactive use cases (auctions, co-streaming) use WebRTC for sub-second latency at higher cost per viewer.

Players periodically send (user, video, position). Store only the latest per user-video in a wide-column/KV store with last-write-wins — losing a few seconds of progress is fine. Batch writes client-side (every 10–30 s and on pause) to avoid a write per second per viewer.

Private or paid content uses signed, short-lived CDN URLs or cookies (Chapter 9) so links can't be shared. Premium studios require DRM: segments are encrypted (CENC), and the player obtains decryption keys from a license server after the backend verifies entitlement, device limits and region.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Transcoding a whole file on one machine. Chunk it; a two-hour video should take minutes, not hours.
  • Serving video from the origin. Bandwidth, not compute, is the bill — CDN and ISP caches are mandatory.
  • Ignoring the long tail. Most of the catalogue is rarely watched; keeping every rendition hot wastes enormous storage.
🎤
Interview drill

Design: A creator uploads a 4K, 3-hour video. What happens before anyone can watch it?

How a strong answer sounds
Resumable multipart upload straight to object storage; the original is kept forever. An ObjectCreated event starts a workflow: probe the file, split into keyframe-aligned chunks, fan out chunk × rendition transcode tasks across a worker pool, then package segments and manifests (HLS/DASH), generate thumbnails and captions, and run moderation and copyright matching in parallel branches. Lower renditions publish first so the video is watchable within minutes while 4K/AV1 finish.

Debug: Users in one country report constant buffering, everyone else is fine.

How a strong answer sounds
Separate delivery from encoding: check CDN cache hit ratio and which POP/ISP caches serve that country, and whether requests are falling back to a distant origin. Then look at the ABR side — if available bandwidth there is genuinely low, is the ladder missing a low rendition? Finally check manifest/segment sizes and whether the player's startup bitrate is too optimistic. The fix is usually pre-positioning content in that region's caches, not touching the encoder.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Video is write-once, read-millions: optimize processing for throughput and delivery for cost and latency.
  • Upload with resumable multipart transfers; keep the original for future re-encoding.
  • Transcode as a DAG of small chunk × rendition tasks across a large worker pool.
  • Package renditions into short segments with HLS/DASH manifests so plain HTTP CDNs can serve them.
  • ABR algorithms choose each segment's quality from throughput and buffer level to avoid rebuffering.
  • Bandwidth dominates cost: ISP-embedded caches and off-peak pre-positioning deliver most bytes.
  • Count views via streaming aggregation, store watch progress with LWW, protect content with signed URLs and DRM.

Check your understanding

Why split videos into 2–6 second segments aligned on keyframes?

Independently decodable segments enable adaptive switching, fast seeking, and CDN caching.

A 2-hour 4K upload takes 10 hours to transcode on one machine. What's the most effective fix?

Chunking makes transcoding embarrassingly parallel; wall-clock time drops roughly with the number of workers.

A buffer-based ABR algorithm sees the buffer drop to 3 seconds. What does it do?

In the “reservoir” zone, BBA plays it safe; quality rises again as the buffer fills.

Why do Netflix and YouTube place cache servers inside ISP networks?

At exabyte-per-day scale, serving from inside the viewer's ISP is the only economically viable option.

How should view counts be updated for a viral video with 50,000 views per second?

Per-view row updates create a massive hot row. Async aggregation absorbs the load and filters bots.
