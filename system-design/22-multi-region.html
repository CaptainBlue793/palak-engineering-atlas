Multi-Region & Disaster Recovery

Availability zones protect you from a data centre fire. But entire cloud regions do go down — through bad deploys to shared control planes, power and cooling events, network cuts, or a mistyped command. And your users live on every continent. Going multi-region is the hardest scaling step there is: it collides with the speed of light and the CAP theorem at the same time.

  • Define RPO and RTO and pick a DR strategy by business need
  • Design backups that actually restore
  • Route users globally with GeoDNS, anycast and health-checked failover
  • Understand why cross-region synchronous replication is slow
  • Handle multi-region writes: home regions, conflict resolution and CRDTs
  • Contain failures with cells, static stability and chaos engineering

Why (and when) to go multi-region

🌍

Latency

Sydney → Virginia is ~200 ms round trip before your code runs. Serving from nearby regions makes interactive apps feel instant.

🛡️

Availability & DR

Survive a full region outage. Required when downtime costs millions per hour or regulators demand continuity plans.

⚖️

Data residency

GDPR, data sovereignty laws and enterprise contracts can require EU data to stay in the EU.

💸
It costs more than servers

Multi-region roughly doubles infrastructure, adds cross-region data transfer fees, forces hard consistency decisions, and multiplies operational complexity. Most companies should first nail multi-AZ high availability plus tested backups in another region, and go active-active only where the business case is clear.

RPO & RTO: the two numbers that drive everything

⏪ RPO — Recovery Point Objective

How much data can we afford to lose? Measured back in time from the disaster. RPO = 1 hour means the last hour of writes may be gone. Driven by replication/backup frequency.

⏩ RTO — Recovery Time Objective

How long can we be down? Measured forward from the disaster until service is restored. Driven by how much standby infrastructure exists and how automated failover is.

Backups that actually work

3️⃣

The 3-2-1 rule

3 copies of data, on 2 different media/services, 1 off-site (another region or account). Add “1 immutable/air-gapped” for ransomware: an attacker with admin credentials must not be able to delete backups (object lock, separate account).

⏱️

Point-in-time recovery

Nightly snapshots + continuously archived transaction logs (WAL) let you restore to any second — e.g. to 14:31:59, just before someone ran DELETE FROM orders without a WHERE clause. Replication won't save you there: it replicates the delete instantly.

🧪

Test restores, regularly

A backup you've never restored is a hope, not a backup. Automate periodic restores into a scratch environment and verify row counts and checksums. Measure the real restore time — that's your true RTO.

📚

Back up more than databases

Infrastructure code, secrets/KMS key policies, DNS config, object storage (versioning + cross-region replication), queues' durable state, and runbooks you can reach when your wiki is down.

Automated regional failoverStep-through
  1. All writes go to us-east-1. The database replicates asynchronously to us-west-2, typically lagging by under a second. The global LB health-checks both regions every few seconds from many vantage points.
  2. us-east-1 starts failing health checks. Crucially, checks come from multiple locations and require several consecutive failures — you don't want a single flaky probe triggering a region evacuation.
  3. Before promoting, make sure the old primary can't accept writes if it comes back (revoke its credentials, flip a replicated “write-enabled” flag). Otherwise you get split-brain: two regions both accepting conflicting writes.
  4. The us-west-2 replica is promoted to leader. Any writes not yet replicated are lost — this is your RPO for async replication. Scale the standby app tier up to full capacity.
  5. The global LB routes 100% to us-west-2. Clients retry failed requests with idempotency keys (Chapter 3), so in-flight operations complete safely.
  6. When east recovers, don't flip back automatically. Rebuild it as a replica of west, reconcile any orphaned writes from the failover window, and switch back in a planned window. Automatic flapping between regions causes more damage than the outage.

Data across regions: the speed of light problem

Light in fibre travels at ~200,000 km/s, and cables don't run in straight lines. Physics sets a floor on cross-region latency that no engineering can remove — and synchronous replication pays that round trip on every write.

Real numbers are higher once you add routing hops, TLS and consensus rounds. This is why most systems replicate asynchronously across regions and synchronously only across nearby AZs — and why Spanner-style globally consistent databases place quorums carefully and accept tens of milliseconds per write.

Who accepts writes?

One region takes all writes; others serve reads from async replicas and stand by for failover. Remote users pay write latency; reads can be local but slightly stale (read-your-writes issues from Chapter 8 apply across regions).

+
  • No write conflicts — one leader
  • Simplest data model
−
  • Writes from far away are slow
  • Failover loses async-unreplicated writes (RPO > 0)

Geo-partitioning: each user or tenant is assigned a home region, and their data lives and is written there. An EU user's writes go to Frankfurt, a US user's to Virginia. A global directory maps user → region.

+
  • Local-latency writes for most users
  • Natural fit for data residency
  • No conflicts: each record has one writer region
−
  • Cross-user interactions across regions (a US user messaging an EU user) need care
  • Travelling users hit a far region; moving a user's home is a migration

Used by Slack/Salesforce-style multi-tenant SaaS, CockroachDB's REGIONAL BY ROW, and many banks.

Every region accepts writes to any data and replicates to the others (multi-leader, Chapter 8). Best latency and availability — but two regions can modify the same record concurrently during normal operation, not just during failures. Conflicts are guaranteed and must be resolved automatically.

+
  • Local writes everywhere
  • A region outage is barely noticed
−
  • Needs conflict resolution: last-write-wins loses data; CRDTs or app-level merges are harder
  • Global uniqueness constraints (usernames, inventory) become very hard

DynamoDB global tables (LWW), Cassandra multi-DC, Figma/Notion-style collaborative editing (CRDTs/OT), CockroachDB/Spanner (consensus instead of conflicts, with latency cost).

Resolving conflicts: last-write-wins vs CRDTs

A CRDT (Conflict-free Replicated Data Type) is a data structure designed so that replicas can be updated independently and always merge to the same result, no matter the order — merges are commutative, associative and idempotent. Try a partitioned shopping cart:

➕

G-Counter / PN-Counter

Each replica counts its own increments; merge takes the per-replica max and sums. Likes, views, votes — no lost increments.

🏷️

OR-Set

Every add gets a unique tag; a remove deletes only the tags it observed. Concurrent add + remove → add wins. Carts, tags, memberships.

📝

Sequence CRDTs

RGA, Yjs, Automerge: collaborative text and lists where concurrent inserts interleave deterministically. Power local-first apps and multiplayer editors.

Limiting blast radius

🧫

Cell-based architecture

Split the whole stack into independent cells — each a complete copy (app, DB, cache, queue) serving a subset of customers, routed by a thin, highly reliable cell router. A bad deploy or poison-pill request takes down one cell (say 5% of customers), not everyone. Deploy cell by cell. Used by AWS, Slack, DoorDash.

🧊

Static stability

The data plane should keep working when the control plane is down. Pre-provision failover capacity instead of relying on launching instances during a disaster (when everyone else is launching too). Cache config and credentials locally. Don't make failover depend on a service in the failed region.

🔀

Shuffle sharding

Give each customer a random combination of, say, 2 of 8 workers. A poisonous customer takes out their 2 workers, but almost no other customer shares that exact pair, so nearly everyone keeps at least one healthy worker.

🧯

Avoid global single points

Watch for hidden global dependencies: one auth service, one DNS provider, one feature-flag service, one container registry region. Several major multi-region outages were caused by exactly these.

Chaos engineering & game days

You don't know your system survives a region failure until you've failed a region. Chaos engineering runs controlled experiments in production (or production-like) environments to find weaknesses before real incidents do.

Define steady state

Pick business-level SLIs: checkout success rate, p99 latency.

Hypothesize

“If we terminate 30% of pods in one AZ, checkout success stays above 99.9%.”

Inject a fault, small blast radius

Kill instances, add latency, drop packets, fail a dependency, block a region — starting with a small percentage of traffic, with an abort button. Tools: Chaos Monkey, Gremlin, AWS FIS, Chaos Mesh, LitmusChaos.

Observe and learn

Did alerts fire? Did failover work? Did the runbook make sense? Fix gaps, then widen the experiment.

Game days

Scheduled exercises where teams practise a full regional evacuation together. Measure the real RTO — it's usually worse than the plan says the first time.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Going multi-region before mastering multi-AZ. It doubles cost and complexity, and most outages are not regional.
  • Untested failover. A runbook nobody has executed is a hypothesis. The first real attempt always reveals a missing dependency.
  • Automatic failback. Flapping between regions causes more damage than the original outage — fail back deliberately.
🎤
Interview drill

Design: A European customer demands their data never leaves the EU, while your stack is in us-east-1. What changes?

How a strong answer sounds
Geo-partitioning: the user record carries a home region, and a global directory maps user → region. EU users' data is written and stored only in EU infrastructure, with EU-resident backups and EU-only replicas. Cross-user features (messaging a US user, global search) need explicit rules: either metadata-only replication, or processing in the region that owns the data. Also check the periphery — logs, metrics, backups, support tooling and third-party processors all leak data if you forget them.

Operate: us-east-1 is degraded but not down. Do you fail over?

How a strong answer sounds
Grey failures are the hardest case. Decide from user-facing SLIs, not infrastructure noise: is error rate or latency past the threshold for long enough, from multiple vantage points? Failover isn't free — you'll lose async-unreplicated writes and hit cold caches in the new region. I'd have a pre-agreed rule (“x minutes above y% errors”), fence writes in the old region before promoting, and communicate on the status page as step one, not step five.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Go multi-region for latency, regional-outage survival or residency — after mastering multi-AZ and cross-region backups.
  • RPO is how much data you can lose; RTO is how long you can be down. Pick DR strategies per system from backup & restore up to active-active.
  • Follow 3-2-1 with immutable copies, use point-in-time recovery, and test restores regularly.
  • Route with anycast/global load balancers for fast failover; DNS failover is bounded by TTLs.
  • Cross-region round trips cost tens to hundreds of ms, so replication across regions is usually async (RPO > 0).
  • Fence the old primary before promoting a standby to avoid split-brain; fail back deliberately.
  • Active-active needs conflict resolution: last-write-wins loses data; CRDTs merge deterministically. Home regions avoid conflicts.
  • Cells, shuffle sharding and static stability limit blast radius; chaos experiments prove it works.

Check your understanding

The business says: “we can tolerate 4 hours of downtime but must never lose more than 5 minutes of orders.” What are the RTO and RPO?

RTO is tolerable downtime; RPO is tolerable data loss measured in time.

An engineer accidentally deletes all rows in the orders table. You have synchronous replicas in 3 AZs and async replicas in 2 regions. How do you recover?

Replicas faithfully replicate the delete. Only backups with point-in-time recovery can go back before the mistake.

Why do most systems replicate asynchronously between distant regions?

Physics sets the latency floor. Async keeps writes fast at the cost of an RPO greater than zero.

During failover, the old primary region comes back online and resumes accepting writes while the new primary is also accepting writes. What is this called and how do you prevent it?

Two leaders create divergent histories. Fencing (revoking write ability, tokens, STONITH) ensures only one leader can write.

A global “likes” counter is incremented concurrently in 3 active regions. Which approach never loses increments?

LWW keeps only one region's total. A G-Counter merges per-replica counts with max, then sums — every increment is kept.
