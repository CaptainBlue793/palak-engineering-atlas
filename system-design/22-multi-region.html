Multi-Region & Disaster Recovery

Availability zones (separate data centres in one area) protect you from a single building burning down. But entire cloud regions do go down too: a bad deploy to shared control software, a power or cooling failure, a cut cable, or one mistyped command. And your users live on every continent. Running in several regions is the hardest scaling step there is. It runs into the speed of light and the CAP theorem at the same time.

  • Define RPO and RTO and pick a DR strategy by business need
  • Design backups that actually restore

  • Contain failures with cells, static stability and chaos engineering

Why (and when) to go multi-region

First, the vocabulary. A region is a geographic area where a cloud provider runs data centres, such as us-east-1 in Virginia or eu-central-1 in Frankfurt. Each region contains several availability zones (AZs): separate buildings with their own power and cooling, a few kilometres to tens of kilometres apart, joined by fast private links. Spreading across AZs protects you from one building failing and costs only a millisecond or two. Spreading across regions protects you from a whole area failing, but regions are hundreds or thousands of kilometres apart, and that distance is what makes everything in this chapter hard.

🌍

Latency

A round trip from Sydney to Virginia takes about 200 ms, before your code does anything at all. Serving users from a nearby region makes an app feel instant.

🛡️

Availability & DR

Survive a whole region going down. You need this when an hour of downtime costs millions, or when regulators require a disaster recovery (DR) plan.

⚖️

Data residency

GDPR, national data laws and enterprise contracts can all require that EU data stays inside the EU.

💸
It costs more than servers

Running in several regions roughly doubles your infrastructure. It adds fees for moving data between regions, forces hard decisions about consistency, and makes day-to-day operations much harder. Most companies should first get two things right: high availability across several availability zones, and backups in another region that they have actually tested restoring. Only go active-active where the business case is clear.

A quick way to decide: count the cost of a regional outage. Say you earn $2M a day online, and a major region goes down for about 6 hours once every couple of years. That is roughly $500K of lost revenue every two years, around $250K a year. If a second active region costs $1M a year in servers, transfer fees and engineering time, it does not pay for itself on outages alone. A tested cross-region backup plus a warm standby might. The numbers change completely for a payment network or a hospital system, which is why the answer is decided per system.

RPO & RTO: the two numbers that drive everything

⏪ RPO — Recovery Point Objective

How much data can we afford to lose? You measure it backwards from the moment of the disaster. RPO = 1 hour means the last hour of writes may be gone. It depends on how often you replicate or back up.

⏩ RTO — Recovery Time Objective

How long can we be down? You measure it forwards from the disaster until the service works again. It depends on how much standby infrastructure you keep running, and how automatic the switchover is.

A timeline makes the difference clear. Say backups run every hour, the last one finished at 13:00, the region fails at 13:40, and service is back at 15:10. Everything written between 13:00 and 13:40 is gone: that 40 minutes is the data you actually lost, and the worst case is a full hour, so this setup can only promise an RPO of 1 hour. The 90 minutes from 13:40 to 15:10 is your RTO. The two numbers are independent. Replicating every second shrinks RPO but does nothing for RTO if it still takes 90 minutes to bring servers up and switch traffic.

The four strategies in the simulator, from cheapest to most expensive:

  • Backup & restore: only backups exist in the second region. On disaster you build everything from scratch. Cheapest, but RTO is hours.
  • Pilot light: the database is kept replicated in the second region, but app servers are off. Turn them on during a disaster. RTO in tens of minutes.
  • Warm standby: a small but complete copy runs all the time, and is scaled up during failover. RTO in minutes.
  • Active-active: both regions serve real traffic all the time. Losing one just shifts its users to the other. RTO near zero, cost and complexity highest.

Backups that actually work

3️⃣

The 3-2-1 rule

Keep 3 copies of the data, on 2 different kinds of storage or service, with 1 of them off-site (another region or account). Against ransomware, add one copy that cannot be changed or deleted at all. Someone who steals your admin credentials must not be able to wipe your backups. Use object lock and a separate account.

⏱️

Point-in-time recovery

Take nightly snapshots and continuously archive the database's write-ahead log (WAL), which records every change. Together they let you restore to any second. For example, to 14:31:59, just before someone ran DELETE FROM orders and forgot the WHERE clause. Replication can't save you there, because it copies the delete instantly.

🧪

Test restores, regularly

A backup you've never restored is a hope, not a backup. Automatically restore into a throwaway environment now and then, and check row counts and checksums. Time how long the restore really takes. That is your true RTO.

For scale: restoring 5 TB at 500 MB/s takes about 3 hours before the database even starts replaying its log. If your stated RTO is 1 hour, that plan cannot meet it, and only a real test tells you so.

📚

Back up more than databases

Also back up your infrastructure code, secrets and encryption key policies, DNS settings, and object storage (turn on versioning and cross-region replication). Include queues' stored state, and runbooks you can still reach when your wiki is down.

Routing users across regions

Running in two regions only helps if users actually reach the healthy one. Routing does two jobs: in normal times, send each user to a nearby region; during a failure, move users away from the broken one quickly. The options below differ mostly in how fast that second job happens.

Why DNS failover is slow: when a user's device looks up your name, the answer is cached for its TTL, say 60 seconds. Change the record at 12:00 because Virginia is down, and a user who looked it up at 11:59:30 keeps going to Virginia until 12:00:30. Some resolvers and apps keep answers far longer than the TTL. Anycast and global load balancers avoid this because the IP address never changes; only what sits behind it does.

Automated regional failoverStep-through
  1. All writes go to us-east-1. The database copies them to us-west-2 asynchronously, usually less than a second behind. The global load balancer health-checks both regions every few seconds, from many places around the world.
  2. us-east-1 starts failing health checks. This matters: the checks come from several different locations and must fail several times in a row. You don't want one flaky probe to evacuate a whole region.
  3. Before promoting the standby, make sure the old primary can't accept writes if it wakes up again. Revoke its credentials, or flip a shared “writes allowed” flag. This is called fencing. Without it you get split-brain: two regions both accepting writes that conflict.
  4. The us-west-2 copy is promoted to leader. Any writes that hadn't been copied over yet are lost. That gap is your RPO with async replication. Now scale the standby app servers up to full capacity.
  5. The global load balancer sends 100% of traffic to us-west-2. Clients retry failed requests using idempotency keys (Chapter 3), so retried operations don't happen twice.
  6. When east recovers, don't switch back automatically. Rebuild it as a copy of west, sort out any writes stranded during the failover, and switch back in a planned window. Regions flipping back and forth on their own do more damage than the original outage.

Data across regions: the speed of light problem

Light in a fibre cable travels at about 200,000 km/s, and cables don't run in straight lines. So physics sets a minimum delay between regions that no amount of engineering can remove. Synchronous replication waits for the far region to confirm, so it pays that round trip on every write.

Real numbers are higher, once you add routing hops, TLS handshakes and rounds of agreement between replicas. That's why most systems replicate asynchronously between regions, and synchronously only between nearby availability zones. Globally consistent databases like Spanner place their voting replicas carefully and simply accept tens of milliseconds per write.

Worked example: Virginia to Oregon is about 4,000 km in a straight line, so about 6,000 km of fibre. At 200,000 km/s, one way takes 30 ms, and a round trip 60 ms. A single connection doing writes one after another, each waiting for that confirmation, can finish at most 1,000 / 60 ≈ 16 writes per second. Within one region, AZs are about 1 ms apart, so the same connection could do hundreds.

Who accepts writes?

One region accepts all writes. The others serve reads from async copies and wait to take over if needed. Users far from the write region wait longer for writes. Their reads can be local, but slightly out of date. The read-your-writes problems from Chapter 8 apply here too, across regions.

Pick it when writes are a small share of traffic, or when a single source of truth matters more than write latency. Most systems start here.

+
  • No write conflicts, because there is only one leader
  • Simplest data model
−
  • Writes from far away are slow
  • Failover loses writes that hadn't been copied yet (RPO > 0)

This is called geo-partitioning. Each user (or customer company) is given a home region, and their data lives and is written there. An EU user's writes go to Frankfurt, a US user's to Virginia. A small global directory remembers which user belongs to which region.

Pick it when most data naturally belongs to one user or one customer company, and when data residency rules apply.

+
  • Fast local writes for most users
  • Fits data residency rules naturally
  • No conflicts, because each record is written in only one region
−
  • Interactions between users in different regions, like a US user messaging an EU user, need careful design
  • Travelling users hit a far-away region, and moving someone's home region is a migration

Used by Slack and Salesforce-style SaaS products, by CockroachDB's REGIONAL BY ROW, and by many banks.

Every region accepts writes to any data, then copies them to the others. This is multi-leader replication (Chapter 8). You get the best latency and availability. But two regions can change the same record at the same time during normal operation, not just during a failure. Conflicts will happen, and something has to resolve them automatically.

Pick it only for data that can merge safely (counters, carts, presence, documents built on CRDTs), or when you use a database that coordinates writes for you and you accept its latency.

+
  • Fast local writes everywhere
  • A region outage is barely noticed
−
  • You need a way to resolve conflicts. Last-write-wins quietly loses data, while CRDTs or app-level merging are more work
  • Rules that must hold globally, like unique usernames or stock counts, become very hard

DynamoDB global tables (last-write-wins), Cassandra across data centres, Figma and Notion-style collaborative editing (CRDTs or OT), and CockroachDB/Spanner, which use agreement between replicas to avoid conflicts, at the cost of latency.

Resolving conflicts: last-write-wins vs CRDTs

Last-write-wins (LWW) is the simplest rule: every write carries a timestamp, and when two copies disagree, the one with the later timestamp is kept whole. It is easy and always converges, but whatever the other region wrote is silently thrown away. Clocks on different machines also drift, so “later” may not even be later in real time. LWW is fine for a user's profile picture, where losing one of two near-simultaneous changes is harmless. It is wrong for a cart, a counter or a list where both changes matter.

A CRDT (Conflict-free Replicated Data Type) is a data structure built so that copies can be changed independently and still always merge to the same result, whatever order the changes arrive in. That works because merging is commutative (order doesn't matter), associative (grouping doesn't matter) and idempotent (merging twice changes nothing). ➕

G-Counter / PN-Counter

Each replica counts only its own increments. To merge, take the highest count seen for each replica, then add them up. Good for likes, views and votes, with no lost increments.

Example: US has counted 7 likes, EU 5. Each replica stores both numbers, {US: 7, EU: 5}. EU gets 2 more likes, so it has {US: 7, EU: 7}, while the US copy still shows {US: 7, EU: 5}. Merging takes the larger value per region, {US: 7, EU: 7}, total 14. Merging again changes nothing. A PN-Counter keeps a second G-Counter for decrements and subtracts it.

🏷️

OR-Set

Every add gets a unique tag. A remove only deletes the tags it has actually seen. So if an add and a remove happen at the same time, the add wins. Good for carts, tags and memberships.

📝

Sequence CRDTs

RGA, Yjs and Automerge handle shared text and lists. When two people insert at the same spot at once, every replica ends up with the same order. These power offline-first apps and multiplayer editors.

Limiting blast radius

Blast radius means how many users one failure can hurt. Multi-region design protects you from a region failing, but most real outages are not caused by a building losing power. They come from a bad deploy, a bad config change, or one customer's strange request that crashes every server it touches. Those spread to every region you copy them to. The patterns below make sure one failure stays in one small part of the system.

🧫

Cell-based architecture

Split the whole stack into independent cells. Each cell is a full copy (app, database, cache, queue) serving one slice of your customers. A thin, very reliable router sends each customer to their cell. Then a bad deploy or a request that crashes the service takes down one cell, say 5% of customers, instead of everyone. You also deploy cell by cell. Used by AWS, Slack and DoorDash.

🧊

Static stability

The part that serves traffic (the data plane) should keep working even when the part that makes changes (the control plane) is down. So have your failover capacity already running, instead of hoping to launch new machines mid-disaster, when everyone else is launching too. Keep a local copy of config and credentials. And never let failover depend on a service living in the region that just failed.

🔀

Shuffle sharding

Give each customer a random pair of workers, say 2 out of 8. A customer whose requests crash workers takes out their own 2. But almost no other customer has that exact pair, so nearly everyone still has at least one healthy worker.

Why: 8 workers can be paired in 8 × 7 / 2 = 28 different ways. Only 1 in 28 customers shares the bad customer's exact pair and loses everything. Customers sharing one of the two workers still have their other worker. Compare that with putting everyone on the same 2 workers, where one bad customer takes everyone down.

🧯

Avoid global single points

Look for hidden things that every region depends on: one login service, one DNS provider, one feature-flag service, one region holding the container registry. Several major multi-region outages were caused by exactly these.

Chaos engineering & game days

You don't know your system survives a region failure until you've actually failed one. Chaos engineering means breaking things on purpose, in a controlled way, in production or a production-like environment. The point is to find weaknesses before a real incident does.

It sounds reckless, but the alternative is worse. Failover paths are code and procedures that run rarely, so they rot: a certificate expires, a new service is added without a standby copy, a runbook points to a dashboard that no longer exists. A planned experiment at 2 pm on a Tuesday, with everyone watching and an abort button, finds these problems. A real outage at 3 am finds them too, much more expensively.

Define steady state

Pick a couple of measures that reflect the business, such as checkout success rate and p99 latency. These are your SLIs (service level indicators).

Hypothesize

Write down what you expect: “if we kill 30% of the pods in one availability zone, checkout success stays above 99.9%.”

Inject a fault, small blast radius

Kill instances, add delay, drop packets, break a dependency, or block a region. Start with a small share of traffic, and keep an abort button. Tools: Chaos Monkey, Gremlin, AWS FIS, Chaos Mesh, LitmusChaos.

Observe and learn

Did the alerts fire? Did failover work? Did the runbook actually make sense? Fix what you found, then run a bigger experiment.

Game days

Planned exercises where teams practise evacuating a whole region together. Measure the real RTO. The first time, it is usually much worse than the plan claims.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Going multi-region before getting multi-AZ right. It doubles cost and complexity, and most outages aren't whole-region ones.
  • Untested failover. A runbook nobody has run is just a guess. The first real attempt always turns up a forgotten dependency.
  • Automatic failback. Flipping back and forth between regions does more damage than the original outage. Fail back on purpose, at a planned time.
  • A standby too small for real traffic. A warm standby sized at 20% will fall over the moment it receives 100% of users. Either keep it big enough, or make sure scaling it up does not depend on the failed region.
  • Using last-write-wins for data where both writes matter. Carts, counters and balances silently lose updates. Pick a CRDT, a home region, or a single leader for that data.
🎤
Interview drill

Design: A European customer demands their data never leaves the EU, while your stack is in us-east-1. What changes?

How a strong answer sounds
Use geo-partitioning. Each user record carries a home region, and a global directory maps user to region. EU users' data is written and stored only on EU infrastructure, with EU backups and EU-only replicas. Features that span users, such as messaging a US user or global search, need explicit rules: either copy metadata only, or do the processing in the region that owns the data. Then check the edges. Logs, metrics, backups, support tools and third-party providers all leak data out of the region if you forget them.

Operate: us-east-1 is degraded but not down. Do you fail over?

How a strong answer sounds
Partial, “grey” failures are the hardest case. Decide from what users experience, not from noisy infrastructure metrics: has the error rate or latency been past the threshold for long enough, seen from several locations? Failover isn't free. You lose writes that weren't copied over yet, and the new region starts with cold caches. So agree the rule in advance (“x minutes above y% errors”), fence writes in the old region before promoting the new one, and update the status page as step one, not step five.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Go multi-region for latency, to survive a region outage, or for data residency rules. Do it after you've got multi-AZ and cross-region backups right.
  • RPO is how much data you can lose. RTO is how long you can be down. Pick a DR strategy per system, from backup and restore up to active-active.
  • Follow 3-2-1, keep copies that can't be deleted, use point-in-time recovery, and test restores regularly.
  • Anycast and global load balancers fail over in seconds. DNS failover is limited by how long answers stay cached.
  • A round trip between regions costs tens to hundreds of milliseconds, so cross-region replication is usually async, which means RPO > 0.
  • Fence the old primary before promoting a standby, or you get split-brain. Fail back on purpose, not automatically.
  • Active-active needs a conflict resolution plan. Last-write-wins loses data; CRDTs always merge to the same answer. Home regions avoid conflicts entirely.
  • Cells, shuffle sharding and static stability limit how far a failure spreads. Chaos experiments prove it actually works.

Check your understanding

The business says: “we can tolerate 4 hours of downtime but must never lose more than 5 minutes of orders.” What are the RTO and RPO?

RTO is how much downtime you can accept. RPO is how much data loss you can accept, measured as a length of time.

An engineer accidentally deletes all rows in the orders table. You have synchronous replicas in 3 AZs and async replicas in 2 regions. How do you recover?

Replicas copy the delete just as faithfully as any other change. Only backups with point-in-time recovery can go back to before the mistake.

Why do most systems replicate asynchronously between distant regions?

Physics sets a minimum delay you can't engineer away. Async replication keeps writes fast, at the cost of an RPO greater than zero.

During failover, the old primary region comes back online and resumes accepting writes while the new primary is also accepting writes. What is this called and how do you prevent it?

Two leaders means two versions of history that no longer match. Fencing makes sure only one of them can write, by revoking its access, using fencing tokens, or powering the old node off (STONITH).

A global “likes” counter is incremented concurrently in 3 active regions. Which approach never loses increments?

Last-write-wins keeps only one region's total and throws the others away. A G-Counter takes the highest count from each region, then adds them up, so every increment survives.
