Reliability & Observability

At scale, something is always broken — a disk, a node, a network link, a dependency. Reliable systems aren't ones where nothing fails; they're ones designed so failures stay small, get noticed fast, and heal quickly.

  • Calculate availability and the cost of each “nine”
  • Use redundancy to raise availability
  • Retry safely with backoff & jitter
  • Stop cascading failures with circuit breakers
  • Debug with logs, metrics & distributed traces
  • Set SLOs and manage error budgets

Failure is normal

A hard drive might last ~1 million hours on average. Sounds immortal. But with 100,000 drives, you should expect about 2–3 drive failures every day. Google-scale fleets see machines, racks and even whole data centers fail routinely. So we design for it:

👯

Redundancy

No single point of failure: N+1 instances, replicas, multiple zones.

⏱️

Timeouts

Never wait forever. A slow dependency is worse than a dead one.

🧯

Isolation

Circuit breakers and bulkheads stop failures spreading.

🔭

Observability

You can't fix what you can't see.

Availability & the nines

Availability = uptime ÷ total time. It's usually quoted in “nines”. Each extra nine cuts allowed downtime by 10× — and typically costs far more than 10× the engineering effort. Click one:

Series vs parallel

When a request must pass through components in series, availabilities multiply — and the total is worse than the weakest link. When components are redundant (in parallel), the system fails only if all copies fail: 1 − (1 − A)ⁿ.

Timeouts, retries & the thundering herd

Retries turn transient failures into successes. But naive retries are dangerous: when a service struggles, every client retrying immediately multiplies the load exactly when the service can least handle it — often keeping it down long after the original problem is gone.

Circuit breakers

If a dependency is clearly broken, stop calling it for a while. A circuit breaker wraps remote calls and fails fast when the failure rate is high — freeing threads, protecting the struggling service, and letting you serve a fallback. It periodically lets a few trial requests through to check for recovery.

More resilience patterns

🚢

Bulkheads

Why ships have compartments
Give each dependency its own pool of threads/connections. If the slow recommendations service exhausts its pool of 20, checkout's separate pool is untouched. One leak doesn't sink the ship.
🪂

Graceful degradation

Serve something rather than nothing
Recommendations down? Show bestsellers from cache. Personalised feed slow? Show a generic one. Core flows (browse, checkout) keep working while “nice-to-haves” switch off.
🚮

Load shedding

Drop work on purpose
When overloaded, reject low-priority requests early (with 503) so high-priority ones succeed. Better to serve 80% of users well than 100% badly. Prioritise by endpoint, user tier or request age.
🐒

Chaos engineering

Break things on purpose
Netflix's Chaos Monkey randomly kills production instances so teams must build for failure. Regular game days test failover, alerting and runbooks before a real outage does.

Observability: logs, metrics & traces

Monitoring tells you that something is wrong. Observability lets you ask why — including questions you didn't anticipate. It rests on three pillars:

📜

Logs

Timestamped records of discrete events. Use structured JSON with request IDs so you can search and correlate. Great detail, expensive at volume.

📈

Metrics

Numeric time-series: request rate, error %, p99 latency, CPU. Cheap to store and ideal for dashboards and alerts. Prometheus, Datadog.

🧵

Traces

Follow one request across every service as a tree of timed spans. Essential in microservices. OpenTelemetry, Jaeger.

SLIs, SLOs & error budgets

📏

SLI — indicator

What you measure: “% of checkout requests that succeed in under 400ms”.

🎯

SLO — objective

Your internal target: “99.9% over a rolling 30 days”.

📜

SLA — agreement

A contract with customers, with penalties. Always looser than your SLO.

The error budget is 100% − SLO: the unreliability you're allowed. It turns reliability into a shared, data-driven decision: budget left → ship features faster; budget burned → freeze risky launches and fix reliability.

Safe deployments round it out

Most outages are caused by changes. Reduce blast radius with canary releases (1% → 10% → 100% while watching golden signals), blue-green deploys (instant switch back), feature flags (turn off without deploying), and automated rollback when SLOs degrade.

When it breaks: incident response

Every system in this course will have outages. What separates strong engineering organisations isn't avoiding incidents — it's how quickly they detect, mitigate and learn from them. Availability is really a race between MTBF (how often it breaks) and MTTR (how fast you recover), and MTTR is the one you control day to day.

Detect minutes matter

Alert on symptoms users feel (error rate, latency, failed checkouts) rather than causes (CPU at 90%). Every page should be actionable and link to a runbook; everything else is a dashboard or a ticket. Alert fatigue is an outage waiting to happen.

Declare & coordinate

One incident commander runs the response (they coordinate, they don't debug), someone owns communications, others investigate. A shared channel and a live timeline beat ten people guessing in parallel. Declaring early is cheap; declaring late is expensive.

Mitigate before diagnosing

Stop the bleeding first: roll back the recent deploy, flip the feature flag off, fail over the region, shed load, scale up. Understanding the root cause can wait until users are served again. “What changed in the last hour?” resolves most incidents.

Communicate honestly

Status page updates on a fixed cadence, even when the update is “still investigating”. Say what's affected and what customers should do. Silence costs more trust than the outage.

Blameless postmortem within days

Timeline, impact (users, revenue, error budget), contributing factors, what went well, and concrete action items with owners and dates. Blameless means asking why the system let a human mistake cause an outage — people don't cause outages, systems that are easy to break do.

Follow through

Track action items like any other work, and pause feature work when the error budget is exhausted (Chapter 16's SLOs). A postmortem nobody implements is theatre.

📕

Runbooks

One page per alert: what it means, how to confirm, the three most likely causes, exact commands to mitigate, and who to escalate to. Written when calm, used at 3 a.m.

🔁

Sustainable on-call

Follow-the-sun or humane rotations, a hard cap on pages per shift, handover notes, and time budgeted to fix what woke you up. If a rotation is noisy, that's a reliability bug, not a people problem.

🧪

Practise

Game days and chaos experiments (Chapter 22), plus regular restore drills. The worst time to discover your failover runbook is wrong is during a real failover.

🎤
In an interview

After designing a system, spend 30 seconds on operations: “I'd alert on checkout success rate and p99 latency against the SLO, keep the deploy rollback to one command, put a feature flag on the new fan-out path, and run a game day for region failover.” Very few candidates do this, and it's exactly what senior roles are judged on.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Retrying without backoff, jitter or a budget. Retries are the most common way a small incident becomes a total outage.
  • Alerting on causes instead of symptoms. Pages for “CPU > 90%” train people to ignore pages; alert on what users experience.
  • Chasing 100% availability. Without an error budget you can't decide when to slow down and when to ship.
🎤
Interview drill

Design: Service A calls B, which calls C. C gets slow. Stop it from taking down all three.

How a strong answer sounds
Timeouts everywhere, set well below the caller's own deadline and propagated as a deadline so work isn't done for a request that's already given up. Circuit breakers in A and B so calls fail fast once C is clearly unhealthy. Bulkheads — separate connection/thread pools per dependency — so C's slowness can't consume all of A's capacity. Retries with backoff, jitter and a budget (say ≤10% of traffic). And a fallback: cached data, a degraded response, or a queued async path.

Operate: You have a 99.9% monthly SLO and you've burned 80% of the error budget by day 10. What now?

How a strong answer sounds
Slow down risky changes: freeze non-essential deploys, prioritize reliability work over features, and look at what consumed the budget — usually one or two recurring failure modes. The budget is the conversation-ender in a “ship it now” argument: it's an agreed number, not an opinion. If the budget is consistently exhausted, either the SLO is wrong or the system needs investment; both are decisions, not accidents.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Each extra nine is 10× less downtime; serial dependencies multiply unavailability, redundancy multiplies availability.
  • Always set timeouts. Retry only idempotent operations, with exponential backoff and jitter.
  • Circuit breakers fail fast when a dependency is unhealthy and probe for recovery.
  • Bulkheads, graceful degradation and load shedding keep failures contained.
  • Logs, metrics and traces together explain what's happening; traces are essential for microservices.
  • Alert on user-facing symptoms (golden signals / SLOs) and use error budgets to balance speed and reliability.

Check your understanding

A request passes through 3 services in series, each 99.9% available. Roughly what's the end-to-end availability?

0.999³ ≈ 0.997. Serial dependencies compound unavailability — ~26 hours of downtime a year instead of ~9.

After an outage, a service keeps crashing as soon as it restarts, even though the bug is fixed. Most likely cause?

Synchronized retries create a thundering herd bigger than normal traffic. Backoff + jitter (and circuit breakers) spread the load.

What does an OPEN circuit breaker do?

“Open” like an open electrical circuit: no current flows. After the cooldown it goes half-open to test recovery.

Users report slow checkouts. Your trace shows payment-svc 580ms, containing fraud-check 550ms, containing ml-model 530ms. Where do you look first?

Parent spans include child time. The deepest span consuming most of the time — ml-model — is the root cause.

Your SLO is 99.9% and you've used 90% of this month's error budget in week one. What should the team do?

That's the point of error budgets: they turn reliability vs velocity into an objective, pre-agreed decision.
