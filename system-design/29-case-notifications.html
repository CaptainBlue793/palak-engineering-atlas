Design a Notification System

“Your code is 482913.” “Your order has shipped.” “50% off this weekend!” Every large product sends billions of push notifications, emails and text messages (SMS). Sending one message is easy. The hard parts are getting the important ones out in seconds, even while a marketing blast to 200 million users is going out. You must also respect every user's settings and never send the same message twice. And you must cope with outside providers (the companies that actually deliver SMS, email and push), which fail all the time.

  • Decide what a notification platform must do across push, email, SMS and in-app
  • Design a background pipeline where urgent messages never wait behind bulk ones
  • Apply user settings, quiet hours, frequency caps and duplicate checks
  • Handle unreliable providers with retries, circuit breakers and a backup provider
  • Send campaigns to hundreds of millions of users
  • Track delivery, opens and clicks

STEP 1 / 6

Requirements

A notification system is a platform: many internal teams (orders, security, marketing, social) send through it, and none of them wants to learn how Apple's push service or an SMS gateway works. So the requirements are partly about users (“don't wake me at 3am”) and partly about the teams calling you (“never make my checkout wait on you”). Keep both groups in mind as you read.

🛠️ Functional

✓Channels: mobile push (APNs, FCM), email, SMS, in-app inbox
✓Internal services send through one API: one-off messages (login codes, receipts) and bulk campaigns
✓Templates translated per language and filled in per user
✓User settings per category and channel, unsubscribe, quiet hours
✓Scheduled sends and delivery/open/click tracking

📏 Non-functional

✓Critical messages (OTP login codes, security alerts) delivered in < 5 s, even during campaigns
✓At-least-once inside the system (a message may be processed twice), but the user never sees a duplicate
✓Always accepts requests — services sending notifications must never be stuck waiting on us
✓Keeps working when providers go down or limit our sending rate
✓Follows the law: opt-outs honoured, CAN-SPAM/GDPR rules, SMS consent

Two numbers shape the design:

  • Campaign bursts are several times the normal rate. So accepting requests must be separated from delivering them, with queues in between.
  • SMS is expensive, and your email reputation is fragile (spam filters judge you by past behaviour). Duplicates and spam aren't just annoying. They cost real money and make future emails more likely to land in spam.

The working: 2B notifications ÷ 86,400 s ≈ 23K/s on average. A campaign of 200M in one hour is 200M ÷ 3,600 ≈ 55K/s — more than double the normal rate, on top of it. Channel mix: 5% of 2B is 100M SMS a day; at ~$0.008 each that's about $800K a day, or roughly $290M a year. At that price, a bug that sends every SMS twice costs about $800K a day. Duplicates are not a cosmetic issue.

What decides the number of workers? Mostly how long the provider takes to answer, not our own CPU. If a push call takes ~50 ms, one worker making one call at a time sends 20 per second. To send 55K/s you'd need ~2,750 calls in flight at once — which is why workers send many requests concurrently and use batch APIs where providers offer them (many email and push providers accept batches).

Why does the API return 202 Accepted and not 200 OK? 202 means “I have your request and will do it”, not “it's done”. That's the honest answer: the push or email may go out a second or an hour later (for a scheduled send). The caller gets a notification_id it can use to look up the status later.

Why does the caller choose the idempotency key, like order-7321-shipped? Because only the caller knows which requests mean “the same event”. If the Order service times out and retries, it sends the same key, and we can tell it's a repeat. If we generated the key ourselves, every retry would look new.

Why so many stores? Each piece of data is read differently. Preferences are read for every notification, so they sit behind a cache. Device tokens are looked up by user and change often. The log is written billions of times and only kept 90 days, which suits Cassandra with a TTL. Dedupe keys only need to live for a day, which suits Redis with an expiry.

STEP 4 / 6

High-level design

The design has one big idea: accepting a notification and delivering it are separate steps, joined by queues. Accepting must always be fast and always work. Delivering depends on outside providers that are slow, rate-limited and sometimes down. Putting Kafka between them means a problem on the delivery side never flows back to the services calling us — messages simply wait in the queue until they can go out.

Life of a notificationStep-through
  1. The Order service calls POST /v1/notifications. The API checks the request is valid. Then it checks the idempotency key (a unique ID for this request) in Redis, using SET NX with a 24h TTL. If the caller retries, the second request does nothing. The API writes the request to Kafka and returns 202 Accepted within milliseconds. Callers never wait on email providers.
  2. Router workers read from the intake topic. For each notification they load the user's preferences (did they opt out of marketing email?), quiet hours in their timezone, frequency caps (e.g. max 3 promos a day) and device tokens. Then they fill in the template in the user's language.
  3. Each message that passes the checks goes to a queue for its channel and priority. So a slow email provider can't hold up push. And a campaign in the low-priority queue can't delay a login code in the critical queue. Think of separate lanes at a toll booth.
  4. Workers take messages and call the providers. They stay under each provider's rate limits using token buckets (Chapter 15). Temporary failures are retried, waiting longer each time (exponential backoff) plus a random delay (jitter). Permanent failures, like an invalid token or an email address that doesn't exist (a hard bounce), are recorded and not retried.
  5. Providers report deliveries, bounces and unsubscribes by calling us back (webhooks). Opens and clicks come from tracking pixels and redirect links. These events flow into Kafka → analytics. They also update our data: an invalid push token is deleted, and a bounced email address is blocked from future sends.
STEP 5 / 6

Deep dive: isolation, priorities & provider failures

This is where notification systems succeed or fail. Try it: a marketing campaign floods the system while users are trying to log in with SMS codes. Then the main SMS provider goes down.

Here's the problem in numbers. Say the SMS workers can send 1,000 messages a second, and a campaign drops 600,000 promo SMS into a single shared queue. A login code that arrives just after them sits behind 600,000 messages: 600,000 ÷ 1,000 = 600 seconds, or 10 minutes. The code expires after 5. With a separate critical queue and, say, 100 workers reserved for it, the code is picked up almost at once, while the campaign still uses the other 900.

🧪 With one shared queue, login codes wait behind thousands of promos, and users can't log in. Priority queues with reserved workers keep codes fast, and the campaign still finishes. During a provider outage, retries alone just pile up. A circuit breaker switches to the backup provider within seconds.
🚦

Priority isolation

Use separate queues per priority and channel. Give critical traffic its own workers, so nothing else can take them over. Low-priority workers grow and shrink with the load. Rate-limit campaigns where they start, so they flow out steadily instead of all at once.

🔌

Provider abstraction & failover

Put every provider behind the same interface. Track each provider's error rate and speed. When failures pile up, a circuit breaker “trips”, like the one in your fuse box, and traffic goes to a backup provider (Chapter 16). Set up sender IDs, templates and consent on both providers in advance.

🧯

Retries without spam

Retry temporary errors with backoff + jitter, but give each message a max age. A login code valid for 5 minutes should be dropped after that, not delivered late. Messages that keep failing (“poison” messages) go to a dead-letter queue, a side queue where people can inspect them.

🪪

Duplicates at every layer

Duplicates can sneak in at three points:

  • Callers retry → check the idempotency key when the request comes in.
  • Kafka delivers a message twice → check (notification_id, channel) before calling the provider.
  • A provider times out but actually sent it → use the provider's own idempotency keys where it offers them. Accept a rare duplicate push, but avoid duplicate SMS, which you pay for.

How does the circuit breaker decide? It watches recent calls to a provider. A typical rule: if more than half of the last 20 calls failed or timed out, open the circuit — stop calling that provider and send to the backup. After, say, 30 seconds, go half-open: let a few test messages through. If they succeed, close the circuit and go back to normal; if not, stay open for another period. The point is to stop wasting time and money on a provider that is clearly down, while still noticing when it comes back.

When should you fail over and when should you just wait? Fail over for critical messages, where a delay hurts (login codes, fraud alerts). For low-priority messages like promos, it's often better to let them wait in the queue until the main provider recovers — the backup may cost more, and a promo an hour late is fine.

Deep dive: preferences, quiet hours & caps

Respecting user choices is required by law. It's also how you avoid getting your app uninstalled. Every notification goes through the same checks before it is queued. Play with this user's settings:

The checks run in a fixed order, and each can stop the message:

  1. Is the category allowed at all? Security alerts are usually always sent — users can't opt out of “someone logged into your account”. Promos need the user's opt-in.
  2. Is this channel allowed for this category? A user may want order updates by push but not by email.
  3. Is it quiet hours in the user's timezone? If yes, a non-urgent message is delayed until quiet hours end, not dropped. Critical ones go through.
  4. Is a frequency cap reached? If the user already got 3 promos today, a fourth is dropped (or merged into tomorrow's digest).

Example: user 42 is in Berlin, it's 23:30 local time, and quiet hours are 22:00–08:00. “Ana liked your photo” is delayed to 08:00. “New login from Chrome” is sent now. A promo is delayed too — and if they already hit today's cap, it's dropped. Note that “local time” uses the user's stored timezone, not the server's: a server in Virginia at 17:30 would wrongly think it's a fine hour for Berlin.

Deep dive: campaigns to 200 million users

A campaign is different from normal traffic: one request turns into hundreds of millions of messages. Doing that in one go would flood the queues, the providers and your own website. The steps below turn one huge job into many small, controlled ones.

Resolve the segment offline

“Users in Germany who bought something in the last 90 days” is a query for the data warehouse, not the live production database. A batch job (Chapter 18) writes the matching user IDs to object storage, in chunks (shards) of, say, 100K.

Fan out in controlled batches

A campaign coordinator queues one task per chunk. Workers turn each chunk into individual low-priority notifications at a limited rate. That protects the providers, your own services, and the backend that users will hit when they tap the notification.

Deliver in local time

Group users by timezone and schedule each group for 10:00 local time. The campaign then rolls around the globe instead of waking up half the world.

Checkpoint & pause

Save progress for each chunk. Then you can pause a campaign (“wrong price in the template!”), resume it or cancel it, without re-sending to people who already got it.

The arithmetic for chunks: 200M users in chunks of 100K is 2,000 chunks. If the coordinator releases chunks so that the campaign sends at 20K/s, each chunk takes 5 seconds and the whole campaign takes 200M ÷ 20K = 10,000 s ≈ 2.8 hours — spread out, not a spike. If one chunk's worker crashes halfway, only that chunk is retried, and the per-message dedupe check (notification_id, channel) stops people in the finished half from getting it twice.

📈
The thundering herd you created

Send a push to 50 million people, and many of them open the app in the same minute. Warn the teams behind the landing page. Add capacity ahead of time (Chapter 21), cache the campaign content at the CDN, and spread delivery out. More than one company has taken down its own site with a notification.

STEP 6 / 6

Wrap-up: bottlenecks & extensions

🗑️

Token hygiene

APNs/FCM (Apple's and Google's push services) answer “unregistered” when the app was uninstalled. Delete those tokens right away. Otherwise you waste sending capacity and may get slowed down by the provider.

📬

Email reputation

Raise volume slowly on new sending IPs (“warming up”). Prove emails are really from you with SPF/DKIM/DMARC. Stop sending to addresses that bounce. Use separate sending domains for one-off and marketing emails.

🔔

In-app inbox

Store a notification list per user (like a feed, Chapter 27) with read/unread state. Send live updates over WebSockets (Chapter 28).

🧠

Smart delivery

Let ML pick the best send time and channel. Combine several minor notifications into one digest. Give each user an overall daily budget.

📊

Observability

Track these per channel, provider and priority: how far behind each queue is, send time, error codes, delivery rate, and how many users opt out after a campaign.

🔒

Security

Only approved internal services can send (using mTLS plus a quota per service). Never put secrets in push messages, because they can show on lock screens.

Edge cases each component must handle

  • User has no devices, or all tokens are dead. Push can't reach them. Fall back to the next allowed channel for that category (for example email), or record “undeliverable” — don't retry forever.
  • User unsubscribes while a campaign is running. Preferences are checked when each message is routed, not when the campaign starts, so later chunks respect the change. Keep the preferences cache TTL short (a minute or so) so an opt-out takes effect quickly — some laws require it within days, users expect it now.
  • Template renders wrongly for one language. A missing translation should fall back to a default language, not send {{first_name}} to millions of people. Render a sample in every language before a campaign starts.
  • Provider says “accepted” but never reports delivery. Keep the status as “sent”, not “delivered”. Don't resend: an unknown outcome is not a failure, and resending risks a duplicate.
  • Kafka or Redis is down at intake. The API can't safely accept, so it returns an error (503) and the caller retries later with the same idempotency key. Returning 202 without storing the request would lose it silently.
⚠️
Common mistakes
  • One queue for everything. A marketing blast then delays login codes. Separate queues by priority and channel.
  • Retrying expired messages. A login code that arrives after it expired is worse than none. Give messages a max age.
  • Never cleaning up device tokens. Dead tokens waste sending capacity, and APNs/FCM may slow you down for it.

Interview drill

Design: Send a 200M-user campaign without breaking OTP delivery or your own website.

How a strong answer sounds
Find the target users in the data warehouse, away from live traffic, and write the user IDs to object storage in chunks. A campaign coordinator queues the chunks at a limited rate into the low-priority queues, which have their own workers. Critical traffic uses separate topics with reserved capacity, so it never waits behind the campaign. Group recipients by timezone so they get it at a sensible local hour. Save progress per chunk so you can pause and resume. And warn the teams whose pages will get the traffic spike.

Failure: Your SMS provider starts returning 503s. Walk through what happens.

How a strong answer sounds
Workers see errors and retry with exponential backoff and jitter. After enough failures, a circuit breaker opens, so you stop hammering a dead provider. The router then switches to a backup provider, with sender IDs, templates and consent set up in advance. Messages past their max age are dropped rather than delivered late. The rest go out once the breaker “half-opens” (lets a test request through) and it succeeds. Meanwhile an alert fires on the provider's error rate, not just on queue length.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Accept requests right away with an idempotency key, then process them in the background through Kafka.
  • Apply user settings, quiet hours and frequency caps in one central place before queueing.
  • Separate queues by channel and priority, with their own workers, so campaigns can never delay critical messages.
  • Assume providers will fail: rate limit, retry with backoff and a max age, and use a circuit breaker to switch to a backup.
  • Check for duplicates when requests arrive and again before calling providers. Drop expired messages rather than sending them late.
  • Send campaigns in rate-limited batches by timezone, saving progress as you go. And prepare for the traffic they cause.
  • Use the providers' delivery callbacks to remove dead tokens, stop mailing bounced addresses, and measure engagement.

Check your understanding

During a big marketing blast, users report login codes arriving 10 minutes late. What's the root design flaw?

If everything shares one queue, login codes wait behind millions of promos. Separate queues with reserved workers fix it.

The Order service times out calling the Notify API and retries. How do you prevent two “order shipped” emails?

The caller sends the same key on every retry. The second request with that key gets a normal “OK”, but no new notification is created.

The SMS provider is down for 15 minutes. What should happen to OTP messages?

Retrying hard against a dead provider wastes time and may deliver useless expired codes. Switch to the backup, and respect each message's expiry time.

Why resolve a 200M-user campaign segment in the data warehouse instead of the production database?

Finding the target users means scanning huge amounts of data. That's analytics (OLAP) work, and it belongs away from the database serving live traffic (Chapter 18).

APNs responds that a device token is unregistered. Best action?

The app was uninstalled, or the token was replaced. Sending to it wastes capacity and can get you slowed down.
