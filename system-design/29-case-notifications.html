Design a Notification System

“Your code is 482913.” “Your order has shipped.” “50% off this weekend!” Every large product sends billions of push notifications, emails and SMS messages. The hard parts aren't sending one message — they're making sure the important ones arrive in seconds while a marketing blast to 200 million users is in flight, respecting every user's preferences, never sending duplicates, and surviving third-party providers that fail constantly.

  • Scope a multi-channel notification platform
  • Design an asynchronous pipeline with priority isolation
  • Apply preferences, quiet hours, frequency caps and dedupe
  • Handle unreliable providers with retries, circuit breakers and failover
  • Fan out campaigns to hundreds of millions of users
  • Track delivery, opens and clicks
STEP 1 / 6

Requirements

🛠️ Functional

✓Channels: mobile push (APNs, FCM), email, SMS, in-app inbox
✓Internal services send via one API: transactional (OTP, receipts) and bulk campaigns
✓Templates with localization and personalization
✓User preferences per category and channel, unsubscribe, quiet hours
✓Scheduled sends and delivery/open/click tracking

📏 Non-functional

✓Critical messages (OTP, security) delivered in < 5 s, even during campaigns
✓At-least-once internally, but no user-visible duplicates
✓Highly available intake — sending services must never block on us
✓Resilient to provider outages and rate limits
✓Compliance: opt-out honoured, CAN-SPAM/GDPR, SMS consent

STEP 4 / 6

High-level design

Life of a notificationStep-through
  1. The Order service calls POST /v1/notifications. The API validates the payload, checks the idempotency key in Redis (SET NX with a 24h TTL — a retry from the caller becomes a no-op), writes the request to Kafka and returns 202 Accepted in milliseconds. Callers never wait on email providers.
  2. Router workers consume the intake topic. For each notification they load the user's preferences (opted out of marketing email?), quiet hours in their timezone, frequency caps (max 3 promos/day), and device tokens. They render the localized template.
  3. Each deliverable message goes to a per-channel, per-priority queue. A slow email provider can't back up push, and a campaign in the low-priority queue can't delay an OTP in the critical queue.
  4. Workers pull messages and call providers, respecting each provider's rate limits with token buckets (Chapter 15). Transient failures are retried with exponential backoff + jitter; permanent failures (invalid token, hard bounce) are recorded and not retried.
  5. Providers report delivery, bounces and unsubscribes via webhooks; opens and clicks come from tracking pixels and redirect links. Events flow into Kafka → analytics, and feed back into state: an invalid push token is deleted, a hard-bounced email address is suppressed.

🧪 With one shared queue, OTPs wait behind thousands of promos and users can't log in. Priority queues with reserved capacity keep OTPs fast while the campaign still completes. During a provider outage, retries alone just pile up; a circuit breaker fails over to the backup provider within seconds.
🚦

Priority isolation

Separate topics/queues per priority and channel, with dedicated worker pools for critical traffic (they can't be starved) and low-priority workers that scale elastically. Rate-limit campaigns at the source so they drain steadily instead of all at once.

🔌

Provider abstraction & failover

Wrap each provider behind a common interface. Track error rate and latency per provider; a circuit breaker opens on failures and routes to a secondary (Chapter 16). Keep sender IDs, templates and consent valid on both in advance.

🧯

Retries without spam

Retry transient errors with backoff + jitter and a max age: an OTP older than its 5-minute validity should be dropped, not delivered late. Poison messages go to a dead-letter queue for inspection.

🪪

Duplicates at every layer

Callers retry → idempotency key at intake. Kafka redelivers → dedupe on (notification_id, channel) before calling the provider. A provider times out after actually sending → provider-level idempotency keys where supported; accept rare duplicates for push, never for SMS charges you can avoid.

Deep dive: campaigns to 200 million users

Resolve the segment offline

“Users in DE who bought in the last 90 days” is a query over the data warehouse, not the production DB. A batch job (Chapter 18) writes the resulting user IDs to object storage in shards of, say, 100K.

Fan out in controlled batches

A campaign orchestrator enqueues one task per shard. Workers expand shards into individual low-priority notifications at a throttled rate — protecting providers, your own services, and the backend that users will hit when they tap the notification.

Deliver in local time

Bucket users by timezone and schedule each bucket for 10:00 local, so the campaign rolls around the globe rather than waking half the world.

Checkpoint & pause

Record progress per shard so a campaign can be paused (“wrong price in the template!”), resumed or cancelled without re-sending to people who already got it.

📈
The thundering herd you created

Sending a push to 50 million people means many of them open the app within the same minute. Warn the teams behind the landing page, pre-scale (Chapter 21), cache the campaign content at the CDN, and stagger delivery. More than one company has taken down its own site with a notification.

STEP 6 / 6

Wrap-up: bottlenecks & extensions

🗑️

Token hygiene

APNs/FCM return “unregistered” for uninstalled apps. Delete those tokens immediately, or you waste throughput and may get throttled.

📬

Email reputation

Warm up new sending IPs, authenticate with SPF/DKIM/DMARC, suppress bounces, separate transactional and marketing sending domains.

🔔

In-app inbox

Store per-user notification lists (like a feed, Chapter 27) with read/unread state and push real-time updates over WebSockets (Chapter 28).

🧠

Smart delivery

ML-chosen send times and channels, digesting several low-value notifications into one, and global per-user budgets.

📊

Observability

Per channel/provider/priority: queue lag, send latency, error codes, delivery rate, opt-out rate after campaigns.

🔒

Security

Only authorized internal services can send (mTLS + per-service quotas); never put secrets in push payloads, which can show on lock screens.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • One queue for everything. A marketing blast then delays login codes — isolate by priority and channel.
  • Retrying expired messages. An OTP delivered after its validity window is worse than none; give messages a max age.
  • Never cleaning device tokens. Dead tokens waste throughput and can get you throttled by APNs/FCM.

Interview drill

Design: Send a 200M-user campaign without breaking OTP delivery or your own website.

How a strong answer sounds
Resolve the segment offline in the warehouse, write user-ID shards to object storage, and have a campaign orchestrator enqueue shard tasks at a throttled rate into the low-priority queues, which have their own workers. Critical traffic uses separate topics with reserved capacity, so it can never queue behind the campaign. Bucket recipients by timezone to deliver at a sensible local hour, checkpoint per shard so it can pause/resume, and warn the teams whose pages will receive the resulting traffic spike.

Failure: Your SMS provider starts returning 503s. Walk through what happens.

How a strong answer sounds
Workers see errors, retry with exponential backoff and jitter, and a circuit breaker opens after a failure threshold so you stop hammering a dead provider. The router then fails over to a secondary provider (pre-registered sender IDs, templates and consent). Messages past their max age are dropped rather than delivered late, and everything else drains once the breaker half-opens and succeeds. Meanwhile an alert fires on provider error rate, not just on queue depth.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Accept requests synchronously with an idempotency key, then process asynchronously through Kafka.
  • Apply preferences, quiet hours and frequency caps centrally before queueing.
  • Isolate by channel and priority with dedicated workers so campaigns can never delay critical messages.
  • Treat providers as unreliable: rate limit, retry with backoff and max age, circuit-break and fail over.
  • Dedupe at intake and before provider calls; drop expired messages rather than sending them late.
  • Run campaigns as throttled, checkpointed batch fan-outs by timezone — and prepare for the traffic they cause.
  • Close the loop with delivery webhooks: remove dead tokens, suppress bounces, measure engagement.

Check your understanding

During a big marketing blast, users report login codes arriving 10 minutes late. What's the root design flaw?

Without priority isolation, OTPs wait behind millions of promos. Separate queues and reserved capacity fix it.

The Order service times out calling the Notify API and retries. How do you prevent two “order shipped” emails?

The caller supplies a stable key; the second request with the same key is acknowledged without creating a new notification.

The SMS provider is down for 15 minutes. What should happen to OTP messages?

Aggressive retries against a dead provider waste time and may deliver useless expired codes. Fail over, and respect message TTLs.

Why resolve a 200M-user campaign segment in the data warehouse instead of the production database?

Segment queries scan huge amounts of data — an OLAP workload that belongs off the transactional database (Chapter 18).

APNs responds that a device token is unregistered. Best action?

The app was uninstalled or the token rotated. Continuing to send wastes capacity and can lead to throttling.
