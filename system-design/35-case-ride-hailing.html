Design Ride Hailing

Uber, Lyft, Grab, Bolt: you press a button and a stranger's car arrives in four minutes. Behind that button is a live matching market. Millions of cars send their location every few seconds. Riders can appear anywhere. A matching engine must pair them within seconds, and it must never give the same driver two trips.

  • Take in a huge, nonstop flood of location updates without overloading a database
  • Index moving cars by location so you can quickly find the nearby ones
  • Compare two matching strategies: greedy (match instantly) and batched (match in groups)
  • Guarantee each driver is given exactly one trip at a time
  • Model a trip's life as a state machine (a fixed set of steps it moves through) driven by events
  • Work out surge pricing and ETAs (estimated time of arrival)

Working the numbers through:

  • Location updates: 1M drivers each pinging every 4 s is 1M ÷ 4 = 250K updates/s. At ~100 bytes each, 250K × 100 B = 25 MB/s, modest network traffic.
  • If stored as rows: 250K/s × 86,400 s ≈ 21.6B rows a day, most of them never read again.
  • Ride requests: say ~40M requests a day including price checks. 40M ÷ 86,400 ≈ 460/s on average, but demand is very bursty (Friday nights, rain, concerts), so plan for ~10× that: ~5K/s. That is 50× less than location traffic, which tells you where the write pressure is.
  • Live state: 1M drivers × ~100 bytes ≈ 100 MB, small enough for one machine's memory.

The key insight: current driver locations are tiny and disposable. Each one is out of date in a few seconds, and the next ping replaces it. A million drivers' latest positions (~100 MB) fit in the memory of a single machine. You still copy and split that data across machines (replicate and shard), but only so it survives a crash — not because it's too big.

Writing every ping to a durable database would mean 250K writes per second of data nobody ever looks up. So keep the live state in memory. Send the history to analytics storage in the background (asynchronously), where it doesn't slow down matching.

STEP 3 / 6

High-level design

From driver ping to matched tripStep-through
  1. Each driver app keeps a WebSocket (a connection that stays open both ways) to a gateway. Every ~4 s it sends {driver_id, lat, lng, heading, status}. It sends less often when the car is parked, and bundles updates when the signal is bad. The gateway updates the in-memory geo index: “driver 812 is now in cell 9q8yy”.
  2. The same ping is also published to Kafka (a durable event log). It feeds analytics, ETA model training, driver pay checks and rebuilding what happened after an incident. None of these are on the critical path — matching never waits for them.
  3. The Trip API creates a trip in the REQUESTED state and asks the matcher for a driver. The app sends an idempotency key (a unique request ID), so if it retries, the same request is not created twice.
  4. The matcher asks the geo index for available drivers in the rider's cell and its neighbouring cells (Chapter 10). If it finds too few, it widens the search.
  5. A driver 200 m away across a river may be 10 minutes out. So the routing service scores candidates by driving time on real roads with live traffic. It also weighs driver rating, vehicle type and how often the driver accepts offers.
  6. The best driver is reserved in one atomic step: a compare-and-set that flips the status AVAILABLE → OFFERED only if it is still AVAILABLE. Now no other match can grab them. A push offer goes out with a 15-second timeout. If the driver declines or doesn't answer, the reservation is released and the next candidate gets the offer.
  7. The trip moves to ACCEPTED and both apps subscribe to live updates. The driver's location now streams to the rider until drop-off. At drop-off, the fare is calculated and payment starts (Chapter 37).

Sizing the “nearby” search

Why search the neighbouring cells too? A rider standing near the edge of a cell may have the closest driver just across the border, in the next cell. Searching the cell plus its 8 neighbours covers every direction. A sized example with geohash precision 6, where a cell is about 1.2 km × 0.6 km:

  • 9 cells cover roughly 3.6 km × 1.8 km, about 6.5 km².
  • A city with 20K online drivers spread over 1,000 km² has ~20 drivers per km², so ~130 drivers in those 9 cells.
  • Filter to the ones that are available and have the right vehicle type: maybe 40.
  • Take the closest ~10 by straight-line distance and ask the routing service for real ETAs only for those. Calling it for all 130 would be slow and wasteful.

The index also has to handle two moving-object edge cases. When a car crosses from one cell into another, the update must remove it from the old cell and add it to the new one, or it will appear in both. And when a driver's phone dies, pings simply stop. Give each entry a short expiry (say 20–30 s without a ping), so the matcher never offers a trip to a “ghost” driver who is no longer there.

STEP 4 / 6

Deep dive: matching

Greedy matching answers each request right away with the nearest car. Batched matching waits a couple of seconds and collects several riders and drivers. Then it solves the whole group at once. That gives shorter pickups overall, because it avoids “nearest-first” mistakes — like giving away a car that was the only close option for the next rider.

Think of a taxi rank dispatcher who looks at the whole queue before sending cars, instead of shouting “next!” each time. Uber found batching cuts both rider wait times and the miles drivers spend empty.

A two-rider example shows the mistake greedy makes. Driver D1 is 2 minutes from both riders. Driver D2 is 3 minutes from rider R1 but 10 minutes from R2. R1 asks first.

R1 getsR2 getsTotal pickup time
Greedy (nearest first)D1 (2 min)D2 (10 min)12 min
Batched (best for the group)D2 (3 min)D1 (2 min)5 min

R1 waits one extra minute; R2 waits eight fewer. When to pick which: batching only helps when several riders and drivers are competing in the same area at once. At 3 a.m. in a quiet suburb there is rarely anyone to batch with, so greedy is as good and responds a couple of seconds faster. Busy downtowns at rush hour are where batching pays off.

Deep dive: never double-assign a driver

Most of this system is fine being eventually consistent (data may be briefly out of date, but catches up). A location that is one second old does no harm. But driver assignment is a uniqueness rule: one driver, one trip. Two riders getting the same car is a serious failure. Here are the options, from least to most coordination:

🔄

Conditional update (best)

Reserve with a compare-and-set (change the value only if it still has the value you expect): UPDATE drivers SET status='OFFERED', trip_id=:t WHERE id=:d AND status='AVAILABLE'. If zero rows change, someone else got there first. It's one atomic operation, and no locks are held while waiting on the network.

🔐

Lease + fencing

If the reservation spans several services, take a short lease (a lock that expires on its own) with a fencing token (Chapter 17) — an ever-increasing number that lets services reject old holders. That way a matcher that froze and woke up late can't bring back an out-of-date assignment.

🧵

Single-writer per region

Send all matching for a city (or geo cell) through one matcher instance or partition — like one consumer reading one Kafka partition. Decisions for a region happen one at a time, so races can't happen at all, and it stays fast.

How the conditional update settles a race, step by step. Matchers A and B both read driver 812 as AVAILABLE, a millisecond apart. Both send the UPDATE … WHERE status='AVAILABLE'. The database runs them one after the other on that row. A's update finds the status still AVAILABLE, changes it, and reports “1 row updated”. B's update now finds OFFERED, so its WHERE doesn't match and it reports “0 rows”. B simply moves on to its next candidate. No lock was held while either matcher waited on the network.

Edge case: a late acceptance. The offer times out after 15 s, the reservation is released, and driver 812 is offered to someone else. Then the first driver's “accept” arrives at 15.2 s over a slow network. Acceptance must also be a conditional update, such as WHERE id=812 AND status='OFFERED' AND trip_id=:t, so the stale accept matches zero rows and the driver's app is told the offer expired.

Deep dive: the trip state machine

Every trip is a small state machine: it moves through a fixed list of states in a strict order. The moves (transitions) are triggered by events from two phones on unreliable networks. Messages can arrive twice or in the wrong place. So every transition must be idempotent and validated:

  • Idempotent (safe to repeat): if the trip is “already in this state”, return success and do nothing.
  • Validated: moves that break the rules, like cancelling a trip that has already started, are rejected.

The happy path is REQUESTED → OFFERED → ACCEPTED → ARRIVED → IN_PROGRESS → COMPLETED, with CANCELLED reachable from the early states. Why be this strict? Consider the driver's phone in a tunnel: it taps “start trip”, gets no reply, and retries three times. Without idempotency, the server might start the fare clock three times or log three events. With it, the second and third “start trip” see the trip is already IN_PROGRESS and just return success. And if a rider's cancel arrives after the trip began, validation rejects it instead of releasing a driver who has a passenger in the car.

Each transition writes to the trip store and to an outbox (Chapter 17) in the same transaction. The outbox is a table of “things that happened” that gets published as events. Other services — pricing, payments, notifications, analytics — react to those events reliably, and the matcher never has to call them and wait.
STEP 5 / 6

Deep dive: surge pricing & ETAs

📈

Surge

Split the city into hexagons (H3, a standard hexagon grid for the globe — Chapter 10). A streaming job counts open requests and free drivers in each hex over a short time window (Chapter 18). When riders outnumber drivers, a price multiplier rises. That cools demand and pulls drivers toward the area. Smooth the multiplier across neighbouring hexes and over time so prices don't jump around, and cap it during emergencies.

An illustrative rule: a hex with 30 open requests and 10 free drivers has demand ÷ supply = 3. A simple table might map a ratio of 1 to 1.0×, 2 to 1.5× and 3 to 2.0×. Real systems tune this curve with experiments.

🛣️

ETA & routing

Roads are stored as a graph, with each road segment weighted by live traffic. A precomputation trick (contraction hierarchies) finds shortest paths in microseconds. ML models then correct the raw route times using past pickup data. ETAs are needed to rank candidates, show arrival times and set prices — so this service is read far more often than it is updated.

📍

Map matching

Raw GPS is noisy — tall buildings and tunnels throw it off. A hidden Markov model (a statistical model that picks the most likely path) snaps the points onto real roads. So the rider sees the car on the road, not in a building. Distance-based fares are also computed from the snapped path, not from GPS jitter.

📶

Flaky networks

When offline, phones save location updates and send them later in batches, with their original timestamps. Apps must cope with duplicate and out-of-order events. Key actions (accept, start trip, end trip) carry idempotency keys and are retried until the server confirms them.

STEP 6 / 6

Wrap-up: scaling and isolation

🏙️

Shard by city

Rides are always local: a rider in Berlin never matches a driver in Cairo. So run a separate, independent stack (matcher, geo index, trip store) for each city or metro area. This is a natural cell architecture (Chapter 22). It limits the blast radius — how much breaks when one part fails — and lets you roll out changes city by city. The edge: trips that cross a boundary, like a ride from the city to an airport in the next metro area. Match by the pickup location, and let the trip stay owned by that city's stack until it ends, wherever the car goes.

🧠

Hot-partition care

Airports and stadiums pack a lot of demand into one cell, creating a “hot” partition. Use smaller cells where things are crowded (like a quadtree, which splits busy squares into smaller ones). Also shard matchers by cell, not only by city.

🛟

Degrade gracefully

If the ETA service is down, fall back to straight-line distance. If surge calculation fails, use 1.0×. If a geo index shard is lost, rebuild it in seconds from the next round of driver pings — it's disposable state.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Writing every GPS ping to the main database. It's disposable state. Keep current locations in memory and send the history somewhere else.
  • Matching on straight-line distance. A driver across a river is not really nearby. Rank by driving-time ETA.
  • Assigning drivers without an atomic reservation. Two matchers will pick the same car within milliseconds of each other.
🎤
Interview drill

Design: How do you find “available drivers near me” in 20 ms, for a city with 100K drivers?

How a strong answer sounds
Keep live positions in an in-memory geo index, sharded by city and keyed by geohash/H3 cell. A query reads the rider's cell plus its neighbours, and widens if there are too few candidates. That's just a handful of hash lookups. Filter by status and vehicle type in memory. Then rank only the top few candidates by routing ETA — the expensive call — instead of scoring everyone. If a shard dies, the next round of pings rebuilds it, so it doesn't need to be durable.

Reason: Where in this system do you actually need strong consistency, and where not?

How a strong answer sounds
Driver assignment needs it. It's a uniqueness rule, like a seat booking, and giving one driver two trips is a real-world failure. So use a conditional update, a lease with fencing, or a single writer per region. Almost everything else is fine being eventually consistent: locations are slightly stale by design, surge multipliers are estimates, and trip history and analytics can lag. Being clear about that line is what keeps the hot path fast.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Keep live driver locations in memory (sharded and replicated). Stream the history to Kafka instead of writing every ping to a database.
  • Index moving cars with geohash/H3 cells. Search the rider's cell plus its neighbours, and widen the search if needed.
  • Rank candidates by driving time on real roads, not straight-line distance.
  • Matching in small batches every few seconds gives shorter average pickups than greedy matching.
  • Driver assignment needs strong consistency: use conditional updates, leases with fencing, or a single writer per region.
  • Model trips as state machines whose moves are validated and safe to repeat. Publish each move through an outbox.
  • Shard by city to keep failures contained. Split crowded cells into smaller ones. When a part fails, fall back to a simpler answer instead of going down.

Check your understanding

Why not store every driver location update in the primary database?

Only the latest position matters for matching, and it's small. The history goes to a stream, where other systems read it later without slowing matching down.

Two matcher instances pick the same driver for different riders at the same moment. Best prevention?

A conditional update lets exactly one matcher win. The loser sees that zero rows changed and moves straight to its next candidate.

Why rank nearby drivers by routing ETA instead of straight-line distance?

The rider cares about arrival time, not distance. Geo search narrows down the candidates; ETA ranks them.

A driver's phone loses signal in a tunnel and later sends 8 buffered location updates at once, out of order. What should the system do?

Ordering by when each event actually happened (event time, Chapter 18) keeps the live position correct. The rest is still kept as history for analytics and fares.

Why shard the whole stack by city?

Because the work is local, it splits cleanly into isolated cells. Each city can then be scaled on its own.
