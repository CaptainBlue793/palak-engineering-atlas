Design Ride Hailing

Uber, Lyft, Grab, Bolt: press a button and a stranger's car arrives in four minutes. Under the hood this is a real-time matching market — millions of vehicles streaming their location every few seconds, riders appearing anywhere, and a matching engine that must pair them in seconds without ever assigning the same driver twice.

  • Ingest a firehose of location updates without melting a database
  • Index moving objects geospatially and search nearby
  • Compare greedy and batched matching strategies
  • Guarantee a driver is assigned to exactly one trip
  • Model the trip lifecycle as a state machine with events
  • Compute surge pricing and ETAs

Requirements

✓Drivers go online and stream their location
✓Riders request a ride from A to B and see nearby cars, price and ETA

✓Live tracking during pickup and trip; fare charged at the end
✓Out of scope here: payments internals (Chapter 37), pooling, food delivery

📏 Non-functional

✓Match within a few seconds; location freshness ~5 s
✓A driver is never assigned two trips — this one needs strong consistency
✓Survive flaky mobile networks and tunnels
✓Highly available per city; a failure in one city must not affect others

The key insight: current driver locations are tiny and disposable. A million drivers' latest positions fit in memory on a single machine — replicated and sharded for availability, not capacity. Writing every ping to a durable database would be 250K writes/second of data nobody queries. Keep the hot state in memory, and stream the history to analytics storage asynchronously.

STEP 3 / 6

High-level design

From driver ping to matched tripStep-through
  1. Each driver app holds a WebSocket to a gateway and sends {driver_id, lat, lng, heading, status} every ~4 s (less often when stationary, batched when the network is bad). The gateway updates the in-memory geo index: “driver 812 is now in cell 9q8yy”.
  2. The same ping is published to Kafka for analytics, ETA model training, driver pay auditing and incident reconstruction — none of which are on the critical path.
  3. The Trip API creates a trip in REQUESTED state (idempotency key from the client prevents duplicate requests on retry) and asks the matcher for a driver.
  4. The matcher queries the geo index for available drivers in the rider's cell and its neighbours (Chapter 10), expanding the radius if too few are found.
  5. A driver 200 m away across a river may be 10 minutes out. The routing service scores candidates by road-network ETA with live traffic, plus driver rating, vehicle type and acceptance history.
  6. The best driver is atomically reserved (compare-and-set on driver status: AVAILABLE → OFFERED) so no other match can grab them, and a push offer with a 15-second timeout goes out. On decline or timeout, the reservation is released and the next candidate is offered.
  7. The trip moves to ACCEPTED; both apps subscribe to live updates; the driver's location is now streamed to the rider until drop-off, when fare calculation and payment kick off (Chapter 37).
STEP 4 / 6

Deep dive: matching

Greedy matching answers each request instantly with the nearest car. Batched matching waits a couple of seconds, collects several riders and drivers, and solves the assignment globally — which produces shorter pickups overall because it can avoid “nearest-first” mistakes. Uber found batching reduces both wait times and driver idle miles.

Deep dive: never double-assign a driver

Most of this system is happily eventually consistent — a location a second out of date is fine. But driver assignment is a uniqueness constraint, and two riders getting the same car is a serious failure. Options, in increasing order of coordination:

🔄

Conditional update (best)

Reserve with a compare-and-set: UPDATE drivers SET status='OFFERED', trip_id=:t WHERE id=:d AND status='AVAILABLE'. Zero rows updated = someone else got there first. One atomic operation, no locks held across network calls.

🔐

Lease + fencing

If the reservation spans multiple services, take a short lease with a fencing token (Chapter 17) so a paused matcher can't resurrect a stale assignment.

🧵

Single-writer per region

Route all matching for a city (or geo cell) through one matcher instance/partition — like a Kafka partition consumer. Serializing decisions per region removes races entirely and keeps latency low.

Deep dive: the trip state machine

Every trip is a small, strictly ordered state machine. Transitions are driven by events from two phones on unreliable networks, so every transition must be idempotent and validated — “already in this state” returns success, and illegal transitions are rejected.

Each transition writes to the trip store and an outbox (Chapter 17) so downstream services — pricing, payments, notifications, analytics — react reliably without the matcher calling them synchronously.
STEP 5 / 6

Deep dive: surge pricing & ETAs

📈

Surge

Divide the city into hexagons (H3, Chapter 10). A streaming job computes open requests vs available drivers per hex in a short window (Chapter 18). When demand outruns supply, a multiplier rises — dampening demand and pulling drivers toward the area. Smooth it across neighbouring hexes and over time so prices don't flicker, and cap it during emergencies.

🛣️

ETA & routing

A road graph with live traffic-weighted edges. Precomputation (contraction hierarchies) answers shortest paths in microseconds; ML models correct raw route times using historical pickup data. ETAs are needed for ranking candidates, showing arrival times and pricing — so this service is queried far more than it is updated.

📍

Map matching

Raw GPS is noisy (urban canyons, tunnels). Snap points to the road network with a hidden Markov model so the rider sees a car on the road, and distance-based fares are computed from matched paths, not GPS jitter.

📶

Flaky networks

Phones buffer location updates offline and send them in batches with original timestamps. Apps must tolerate duplicate and out-of-order events; critical actions (accept, start trip, end trip) carry idempotency keys and are retried until acknowledged.

STEP 6 / 6

Wrap-up: scaling and isolation

🏙️

Shard by city

Rides are inherently local: a rider in Berlin never matches a driver in Cairo. Run an independent stack (matcher, geo index, trip store) per city or metro — a natural cell architecture (Chapter 22) that bounds blast radius and lets you deploy city by city.

🧠

Hot-partition care

Airports and stadiums concentrate demand in one cell. Use finer cells where density is high (quadtree-style), and shard matchers by cell rather than only by city.

🛟

Degrade gracefully

If the ETA service is down, fall back to straight-line distance. If surge computation fails, default to 1.0×. If the geo index shard is lost, rebuild it in seconds from the next round of driver pings — it's disposable state.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Writing every GPS ping to the primary database. It's disposable state — keep current locations in memory and stream history elsewhere.
  • Matching on straight-line distance. A driver across a river is not nearby; rank by routing ETA.
  • Assigning drivers without an atomic reservation. Two matchers will pick the same car within milliseconds of each other.
🎤
Interview drill

Design: How do you find “available drivers near me” in 20 ms, for a city with 100K drivers?

How a strong answer sounds
Keep live positions in an in-memory geospatial index sharded by city, keyed by geohash/H3 cell. A query reads the rider's cell plus neighbours (expanding if too few candidates), which is a handful of hash lookups. Filter by status and vehicle type in memory, then rank the top candidates by routing ETA — the expensive call — rather than scoring everyone. The index is rebuilt from the next round of pings if a shard dies, so it needs no durability.

Reason: Where in this system do you actually need strong consistency, and where not?

How a strong answer sounds
Driver assignment needs it — a seat-like uniqueness constraint where double-assignment is a real-world failure — so use a conditional update, a lease with fencing, or a single writer per region. Almost everything else is fine eventually consistent: locations are stale by design, surge multipliers are approximations, trip history and analytics can lag. Being explicit about that boundary is what keeps the hot path fast.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Keep live driver locations in memory (sharded, replicated) and stream history to Kafka instead of writing every ping to a database.
  • Index moving objects with geohash/H3 cells and search the rider's cell plus neighbours, expanding as needed.
  • Rank candidates by road-network ETA, not straight-line distance.
  • Batch matching over a short window beats greedy matching on average pickup time.
  • Driver assignment needs strong consistency: use conditional updates, leases with fencing, or a single writer per region.
  • Model trips as validated, idempotent state machines and publish transitions via an outbox.
  • Shard by city for isolation; handle hot cells with finer partitioning; degrade gracefully when subsystems fail.

Check your understanding

Why not store every driver location update in the primary database?

Only the latest position matters for matching, and it's small. History goes to a stream for asynchronous consumers.

Two matcher instances pick the same driver for different riders at the same moment. Best prevention?

A conditional update makes exactly one matcher win; the loser moves to its next candidate immediately.

Why rank nearby drivers by routing ETA instead of straight-line distance?

The rider experiences arrival time, not distance. Geo search narrows candidates; ETA ranks them.

A driver's phone loses signal in a tunnel and later sends 8 buffered location updates at once, out of order. What should the system do?

Event-time handling (Chapter 18) keeps live state correct while preserving history for analytics and fares.

Why shard the whole stack by city?

Locality of the workload maps perfectly onto cell-based isolation and independent scaling per market.
