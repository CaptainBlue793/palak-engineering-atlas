Cost & Capacity Engineering

Every design decision in this course has a price tag. Replication ×3, multi-region active-active, 90-day log retention, a cache in front of everything: each is defensible, and together they can turn a healthy product into an unprofitable one. Senior engineers are expected to reason about cost per unit of value as fluently as about latency — and to find the cheapest design that still meets the SLO.

  • Think in unit economics rather than total spend
  • Know where cloud money actually goes — including the surprises
  • Compare serverless, containers and reserved capacity honestly
  • Plan capacity with headroom, lead times and commitment mixes
  • Apply the big levers without breaking your SLOs
  • Avoid the observability and egress cost traps

Unit economics: the only number that scales

“Our bill is $400K a month” means nothing on its own. Cost per unit does: per monthly active user, per order, per GB stored, per 1,000 API calls, per inference. It tells you whether growth makes things better or worse, and it turns cost into an engineering metric you can track like latency.

📉

Falling unit cost

Fixed costs amortize as you grow; caching and batching improve with scale. Healthy — growth improves margin.

➡️

Flat unit cost

Cost is dominated by per-request work. Fine, as long as revenue per unit is comfortably higher.

📈

Rising unit cost

Something is super-linear: fan-out, cross-shard queries, N² coordination, retained history. This is an architecture bug, and growth makes it worse.

🎤
A sentence worth saying in any design interview

“At 10× traffic this design costs roughly 10×, except storage which grows with retained history — so I'd add lifecycle policies and cap retention at 90 days. The dominant line item is CDN egress, so I'd measure cost per 1,000 video minutes and set a budget alert on it.” Almost nobody says this, and it instantly reads as senior.

Serverless vs containers vs owned capacity

There's no universally cheapest compute model — there's a crossover point. Serverless wins at low or spiky traffic (you pay nothing when idle); containers with autoscaling win in the broad middle; reserved or owned capacity wins at steady, high, predictable load.

Capacity planning

Forecast demand

Take the last 12 months, separate growth trend from seasonality (Black Friday, semester start, campaign launches), and add known step changes: a new market, a big customer onboarding, a partnership.

Convert demand into resources

Use measured unit costs from load tests (Chapter 19): requests/s per core, GB per million events, connections per instance. “We serve 1,200 rps per pod at 65% CPU” is the number that makes a plan real.

Add headroom for physics and failure

Target ~60–70% utilization (Chapter 4's queueing curve), plus enough spare to survive losing an AZ (N+1 across three zones means each carries ~50% at peak). Headroom is not waste; it's the SLO.

Respect lead times

Ordinary instances arrive in seconds. GPUs, large reserved blocks, database upgrades, IP address ranges and third-party quota increases take weeks. Capacity planning exists mostly because of these.

Choose a commitment mix

Cover the stable base with 1–3 year commitments (30–60% cheaper), the predictable middle with autoscaled on-demand, and interruptible batch work with spot (60–90% cheaper, can vanish with 2 minutes' notice). Never put a stateful primary on spot.

Re-forecast and review

Monthly: compare forecast with actuals, adjust commitments before they expire, and check unit cost trends per team. Cost drifts upward by default — nobody ever files a ticket to delete a forgotten cluster.

💸
The classic surprise bills

Cross-AZ traffic between chatty services (charged both ways) · NAT gateway processing for every byte to the internet · log ingestion from a debug statement in a hot loop · a retry storm multiplying a third-party API bill · idle non-production environments running 24/7 · storage snapshots never expiring · a cross-region replica added “temporarily” in 2023. Budget alerts on anomalies, not just totals, catch these in days instead of at the invoice.

The observability trap

Metrics are priced by unique time series: adding user_id as a label to one metric can create millions of series overnight and a five-figure invoice (Chapter 10's cardinality warning). Logs are priced by ingested volume: one INFO line per request at 50K rps is ~4 billion lines a day. Sample traces (1–5% plus all errors), aggregate before shipping, keep full-fidelity data only for short windows.

🧮

Query-priced systems

Warehouses charge per byte scanned. The same dashboard query costs cents or hundreds of dollars depending on partitioning, clustering and SELECT *. Partition by date, prune columns, materialize the dashboards' aggregates (Chapter 18), and put cost guardrails on ad-hoc queries.

🏷️

Make cost visible

Tag every resource by team and service, publish a unit-cost dashboard per service, and put cost regressions in the same review as latency regressions. Teams optimize what they can see; nobody optimizes a single company-wide invoice.

⚖️

Know when to stop

An engineer-month costs more than many optimizations save. Rank work by annualized saving ÷ engineering days, take the top few, and leave the rest. And never cut headroom or redundancy to hit a cost target — an outage costs more than a year of the savings.

Cost as a design constraint

The same requirement can usually be met at very different prices. Making the cheaper choice when it still meets the SLO is the skill.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Optimizing what's visible instead of what's expensive. People tune instance sizes while egress or observability quietly dominates the bill.
  • Cutting headroom to hit a cost target. One outage usually costs more than a year of the savings.
  • Cost work with no owner or measurement. Without per-team tagging and unit-cost dashboards, spend drifts back within a quarter.
🎤
Interview drill

Analyse: The CFO says infrastructure cost is growing faster than revenue. Where do you start?

How a strong answer sounds
Get cost per unit of value (per MAU, per order) and its trend — that tells you whether this is a scaling problem or an efficiency problem. Then break the bill down by service and line item, and look for super-linear growth: unbounded retention, fan-out, cross-shard queries, per-request work that should be batched. Attack the top two line items with the biggest levers (right-sizing, commitments, CDN offload, telemetry sampling, storage tiering), and publish unit cost per team so it stays fixed.

Trade-off: Would you run your database on spot instances to save 70%?

How a strong answer sounds
No for the primary — spot can be reclaimed with two minutes' notice, and stateful failover under forced churn is exactly when you lose data. Yes for genuinely interruptible work: batch ETL, transcoding, model training, CI runners, and possibly read-only analytics replicas that can be rebuilt. The rule is: spot for things that can restart without correctness risk, commitments for the steady base, on-demand for the elastic middle.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Track cost per user/request/GB, not total spend — rising unit cost is an architecture bug.
  • Compute, egress, storage and observability dominate most bills; the last two surprise teams most.
  • Serverless wins when idle or spiky, containers in the middle, commitments at steady high load.
  • Plan capacity from measured unit costs, with headroom for queueing and failure, and respect lead times.
  • Mix reserved, on-demand and spot to match how predictable each workload is.
  • Biggest levers: right-sizing, commitments, CDN/caching, storage tiering and telemetry sampling.
  • Make cost visible per team and service, and stop optimizing when engineering time costs more than the saving.
  • Never buy savings with headroom or redundancy you actually need.

Check your understanding

Your bill grows 3× when users grow 2×. What does that indicate?

Healthy systems scale sub- or roughly linearly. Rising unit cost means growth is making the economics worse.

An engineer adds user_id as a label on a request-latency metric. What happens?

Metrics are priced and stored per unique label combination. High-cardinality identifiers belong in logs or traces, not metric labels.

A video service pays enormous data-transfer costs. Biggest lever?

Egress dominates media workloads; offloading to edge caches is exactly why CDNs exist (Chapters 6, 32).

Which workload is a good fit for spot/preemptible instances?

Spot capacity can be reclaimed with minutes of notice — fine for interruptible, retryable batch work, not for stateful or critical paths.

Finance asks you to cut 30% of infrastructure cost. Which cut is the bad idea?

Redundancy and headroom are the SLO. One outage typically costs more than the savings from removing them.
