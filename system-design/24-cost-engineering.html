Cost & Capacity Engineering

Every design decision in this course has a price tag. Three copies of every piece of data, active-active in several regions, keeping logs for 90 days, a cache in front of everything. Each one is easy to justify. Together, they can turn a healthy product into one that loses money. Senior engineers are expected to think about cost per unit of value as easily as they think about latency. The goal is the cheapest design that still meets the SLO (the reliability and speed target you promised).

  • Think in unit economics rather than total spend
  • Know where cloud money actually goes — including the surprises
  • Compare serverless, containers and reserved capacity honestly
  • Plan capacity with headroom, lead times and commitment mixes
  • Apply the big levers without breaking your SLOs
  • Avoid the observability and egress cost traps

Unit economics: the only number that scales

“Our bill is $400K a month” means nothing on its own. Cost per unit does. Units are things like: per monthly active user (MAU), per order, per GB stored, per 1,000 API calls, per model prediction. Cost per unit tells you whether growth makes things better or worse. It also turns cost into an engineering number you can track, just like latency.

A worked example. This year the bill is $400K a month for 5M monthly users: $400,000 ÷ 5,000,000 = $0.08 per user. Next year the bill is $600K, and finance is alarmed. But users grew to 10M, so the cost per user fell to $0.06. The bill went up 50% while each user got 25% cheaper to serve: healthy growth. Had users only grown to 6M, the cost per user would be $0.10, and that would be the real warning sign, whatever the total.

📉

Falling unit cost

Fixed costs get spread over more users as you grow, and caching and batching work better at scale. This is healthy: growth improves your profit margin.

➡️

Flat unit cost

Most of the cost is work done per request. That's fine, as long as you earn comfortably more per unit than it costs.

📈

Rising unit cost

Something grows faster than your traffic (super-linear). Common causes: fan-out (one action triggers many), queries across many shards, every node coordinating with every other node (N²), or history you keep forever. This is an architecture bug, and growth makes it worse.

Why N² hurts: if every one of N nodes sends a heartbeat to every other node, that is N × (N − 1) messages per round. 10 nodes send 90; 100 nodes send 9,900. Ten times the nodes, over a hundred times the traffic. The same shape appears when every user's feed is rebuilt from everyone they follow, or when every query touches every shard. Gossip (Chapter 20) exists partly to remove exactly this cost.

🎤
A sentence worth saying in any design interview

“At 10× traffic this design costs roughly 10×. The exception is storage, which grows with how much history we keep. So I'd add lifecycle policies and keep data for at most 90 days. The biggest line item is CDN egress, so I'd track cost per 1,000 video minutes and set a budget alert on it.” Almost nobody says this, and it instantly sounds senior.

Where the money actually goes

A cloud bill has a handful of big buckets. Compute is servers, containers and functions, paid per hour or per millisecond. Storage is disks, databases and object storage, paid per GB per month. Egress is data sent out of the cloud (and between zones or regions), paid per GB transferred. Managed services are databases, queues and caches the provider runs for you, usually priced above the raw servers. Observability is logs, metrics and traces, priced by volume ingested and kept.

Egress is where image- and video-heavy products get surprised. Serve 500 TB a month straight from your servers, at a typical list price of 5–9 cents per GB, and that is roughly $25K–45K a month for bandwidth alone. A CDN usually charges less per GB, and every cached copy it serves is a byte your origin never has to send.

Serverless vs containers vs owned capacity

No way of buying compute is always cheapest. Instead there's a crossover point where one option overtakes another:

  • Serverless wins when traffic is low or spiky, because you pay nothing while idle.
  • Containers with autoscaling win in the broad middle.
  • Reserved or owned capacity (paid for in advance, at a discount) wins when load is high, steady and predictable.

The reason is what you pay for. Serverless charges only for work actually done, but at a higher price per unit of work. Containers and servers charge for every hour they run, busy or idle, at a lower price per unit. So the question is: what fraction of the time would a server actually be busy? A server that is busy 5% of the time wastes 95% of what you pay, and serverless wins easily. A server that is busy 70% of the time around the clock uses what you pay for, and the lower hourly price wins. Reserved capacity takes this further: you promise to pay for the server for 1–3 years, whether or not you use it, in exchange for a big discount.

Capacity planning

Capacity planning answers one question ahead of time: will we have enough machines, databases and quota for next quarter's peak, without paying for far more than we need? Too little, and the site falls over on the busiest day of the year. Too much, and you pay for idle servers every day. Autoscaling handles minutes-to-hours changes; capacity planning handles the things that take weeks to buy or set up.

Forecast demand

Take the last 12 months of traffic. Separate the long-term growth trend from seasonal ups and downs, like Black Friday, the start of a semester, or campaign launches. Then add changes you already know are coming: a new market, a big customer joining, a partnership.

Convert demand into resources

Use real numbers measured in load tests (Chapter 19): requests per second per CPU core, GB per million events, connections per instance. A sentence like “we serve 1,200 requests per second per pod at 65% CPU” is what makes a plan real.

Add headroom for physics and failure

Aim for about 60–70% utilization, because queues grow sharply above that (Chapter 4). Also keep enough spare capacity to survive losing a whole availability zone. With three zones and one spare (N+1), each zone runs at only about 50% at peak. Headroom isn't waste: it is what keeps your SLO.

Respect lead times

Ordinary servers arrive in seconds. But GPUs, large reserved blocks, database upgrades, IP address ranges and quota increases from other companies take weeks. These slow items are the main reason capacity planning exists.

Choose a commitment mix

Cover the part of the load that is always there with 1–3 year commitments (30–60% cheaper). Cover the predictable middle with autoscaled on-demand servers. Run batch work that can be interrupted on spot instances: spare capacity sold 60–90% cheaper, which the provider can take back with 2 minutes' notice. Never put a primary database, or anything else holding important state, on spot.

Re-forecast and review

Every month, compare the forecast with what really happened, adjust commitments before they expire, and check each team's cost-per-unit trend. Costs creep upward on their own. Nobody ever files a ticket to delete a forgotten cluster.

Worked example. The forecast for next Black Friday is 3,000 requests per second at peak. A load test showed one pod handles 1,200 requests per second at the 65% CPU target. So 3,000 ÷ 1,200 = 2.5, rounded up to 3 pods. But you run in 3 zones and must survive losing one. If a zone fails, the other two zones must still hold at least 3 pods, so each zone needs 2 pods, and you run 6 in total. Double what the raw maths said, and that doubling is the price of surviving a zone failure.

💸
The classic surprise bills
  • Cross-AZ traffic between services that talk a lot, charged in both directions.
  • NAT gateway fees for processing every byte your servers send to the internet.
  • Log ingestion from one debug statement inside code that runs millions of times.
  • A retry storm multiplying the bill for a paid third-party API.
  • Idle test and staging environments running 24/7.
  • Storage snapshots that never expire.
  • A cross-region replica added “temporarily” in 2023.

Set budget alerts on unusual jumps, not just on totals. They catch these within days, instead of when the invoice arrives.

Start with the biggest line items, not the easiest changes. A 50% saving on something that is 3% of the bill is worth less than a 10% saving on something that is 40% of it. The observability trap

Metrics are priced by the number of unique time series (one per combination of label values). Add user_id as a label on one metric and you can create millions of series overnight, and a five-figure invoice (the cardinality warning from Chapter 10). Logs are priced by volume. One INFO line per request at 50K requests per second is about 4 billion lines a day. So: keep only a sample of traces (1–5%, plus every error), summarize data before shipping it, and keep full detail only for short periods.

🧮

Query-priced systems

Data warehouses charge for every byte a query reads. The same dashboard query can cost a few cents or hundreds of dollars, depending on partitioning, clustering and whether it uses SELECT *. Partition tables by date, read only the columns you need, pre-compute the numbers dashboards show (Chapter 18), and put cost limits on one-off queries.

🏷️

Make cost visible

Tag every resource with its team and service. Publish a cost-per-unit dashboard for each service. Review cost increases in the same meeting as latency increases. Teams improve what they can see, and nobody improves one giant company-wide invoice.

⚖️

Know when to stop

A month of an engineer's time costs more than many optimizations save. Rank the work by yearly saving ÷ engineering days, do the top few, and leave the rest. And never cut headroom or redundancy to hit a cost target. One outage costs more than a year of those savings.

Cost as a design constraint

You can usually meet the same requirement at very different prices. The skill is picking the cheaper option when it still meets the SLO.

The trick is to read the requirement carefully before designing. “Instantly” usually means “the user who made the change sees it right away”, not “every user worldwide sees it in the same millisecond”. “Never lose an event” usually means “never lose it”, not “keep it in the most expensive storage forever”. Pinning down what is really required is often the single largest cost saving in a design.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Optimizing what's easy to see instead of what's expensive. People tune server sizes while egress or observability quietly makes up most of the bill.
  • Cutting headroom to hit a cost target. One outage usually costs more than a year of the savings.
  • Cost work with no owner and no measurement. Without tagging per team and cost-per-unit dashboards, spending creeps back up within three months.
  • Buying commitments before right-sizing. Reserving 3 years of over-sized servers locks in the waste at a discount. Shrink the fleet first, then commit to what is left.
  • Planning capacity from averages. Systems fail at the peak, not the average. Plan from the busiest hour, plus room to lose a zone.

Interview drill

Analyse: The CFO says infrastructure cost is growing faster than revenue. Where do you start?

How a strong answer sounds
Start with cost per unit of value (per monthly user, per order) and how it's trending. That tells you whether this is a scaling problem or an efficiency problem. Then break the bill down by service and line item. Look for anything growing faster than traffic: data kept forever, fan-out, queries across many shards, or per-request work that should be batched. Attack the two biggest line items with the biggest levers (right-sizing, commitments, CDN offload, telemetry sampling, storage tiering). Finally, publish cost per unit for each team, so the fix sticks.

Trade-off: Would you run your database on spot instances to save 70%?

How a strong answer sounds
Not for the primary. Spot machines can be taken back with two minutes' notice, and being forced to fail over a database again and again is exactly when you lose data. Yes for work that can truly be interrupted: batch ETL, video transcoding, model training, CI runners, and maybe read-only analytics copies that can be rebuilt. The rule: spot for anything that can restart without risk of wrong results, commitments for the steady base load, and on-demand for the part that goes up and down.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Track cost per user, per request or per GB, not the total bill. A rising cost per unit is an architecture bug.
  • Compute, egress, storage and observability make up most bills. The last two surprise teams the most.
  • Serverless wins for idle or spiky traffic, containers in the middle, and commitments for steady high load.
  • Plan capacity from measured numbers, with headroom for queues and failures, and allow for long lead times.
  • Mix reserved, on-demand and spot capacity to match how predictable each workload is.
  • The biggest levers: right-sizing, commitments, CDN and caching, storage tiering, and telemetry sampling.
  • Make cost visible per team and service. Stop optimizing when the engineering time costs more than the saving.
  • Never buy savings by cutting headroom or redundancy you actually need.

Check your understanding

Your bill grows 3× when users grow 2×. What does that indicate?

In a healthy system, cost grows no faster than usage, and often slower. A rising cost per unit means growth is making the economics worse.

An engineer adds user_id as a label on a request-latency metric. What happens?

Metrics are stored and priced per unique combination of label values. IDs with millions of possible values belong in logs or traces, not in metric labels.

A video service pays enormous data-transfer costs. Biggest lever?

For media products, egress is the biggest cost. Serving bytes from caches near users is exactly why CDNs exist (Chapters 6, 32).

Which workload is a good fit for spot/preemptible instances?

Spot machines can be taken back with a few minutes' notice. That's fine for batch work you can interrupt and retry, but not for anything holding state or on a critical path.

Finance asks you to cut 30% of infrastructure cost. Which cut is the bad idea?

Redundancy and headroom are what keep your SLO. One outage usually costs more than you'd save by removing them.
