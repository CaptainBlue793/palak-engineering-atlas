Design a Job Scheduler

“Send this reminder in 3 days.” “Run this report every weekday at 07:00.” “Retry this payout in 15 minutes.” “Run this 12-step onboarding workflow, which takes a week and must survive deploys.” Every platform eventually needs one of these — and gets it subtly wrong: jobs that fire twice, jobs that never fire, and a midnight stampede that takes out the database.

  • Find due jobs efficiently at 100M+ schedules
  • Compare polling, time buckets and timing wheels
  • Survive the cron alignment stampede
  • Trigger exactly once despite crashes
  • Run retries, priorities and per-tenant fairness
  • Support long-running durable workflows
STEP 1 / 6

Requirements

🛠️ Functional

✓One-off jobs (“at 14:05 on 12 Nov”) and recurring schedules (cron expressions, timezone-aware)
✓Execute a task: call a service, run a container, send a message
✓Retries with backoff, timeouts, cancellation, manual re-run
✓Multi-step workflows with waits, branches and compensations
✓Status, history and alerting per job

📏 Non-functional

✓Fire within ~1 s of the due time, at any scale
✓Never lose a job; never silently skip one
✓At-least-once execution with idempotent handlers — and at-most-once triggering per scheduled instant
✓Multi-tenant: one noisy tenant can't starve others
✓Survive worker crashes, deploys and region failover

The averages are unremarkable; the distribution is brutal. Humans write 0 0 * * *, so a huge fraction of all schedules are due at exactly midnight, on the hour, or on the minute. A scheduler that can handle 12K/s on average will still fall over at 00:00:00 unless it deliberately spreads that load.

The pragmatic combination

Durable schedules in a database (source of truth, sharded by tenant), a rolling window of the next few minutes loaded into an in-memory timing wheel per shard for precision, and a queue for handing work to executors. The database survives restarts; the wheel provides millisecond firing; the queue decouples firing from execution.

STEP 5 / 6

Firing exactly once

A worker picks up a job, starts running it, and its process dies. Did the job run? Another worker must decide — and “run it again” is only safe if the effect is idempotent. Two mechanisms do the heavy lifting.

🔑 The honest summary: exactly-once triggering is achievable (a unique key on (job_id, scheduled_for) means only one run row can exist), but exactly-once side effects are not — the worker can always die after calling the payment API and before recording that it did. That's why the handler must be idempotent (Chapter 17) and why leases need fencing tokens.

Execution: fairness, retries and poison jobs

⚖️

Per-tenant fairness

One customer scheduling 5 million jobs must not delay everyone else. Use per-tenant queues with weighted round-robin (or a concurrency cap per tenant), not a single global FIFO.

🔁

Retries

Exponential backoff with jitter, a maximum attempt count, and a deadline after which a late job is pointless (a “happy birthday” email three days late is worse than none). Failed jobs land in a dead-letter view with the error and a re-run button.

☠️

Poison jobs

A job that crashes its worker every time will take down your fleet one worker at a time. Track consecutive failures per job, quarantine after N, and run untrusted workloads in isolated sandboxes with CPU/memory limits.

🕰️

Timezones & DST

“Every day at 09:00 Europe/Berlin” is not a fixed UTC offset. Store the timezone with the schedule and compute the next occurrence with a real calendar library. And decide policy for the DST gap: 02:30 happens twice in autumn and not at all in spring.

⏭️

Catch-up policy

The scheduler was down for 3 hours. Should a 5-minute job now run 36 times? Per schedule, choose: run all (backfill), run once (collapse), or skip (only run the next occurrence). Getting this wrong is how outages become billing incidents.

🚫

Cancellation

Cancel must be idempotent and race-free: mark the run cancelled with a conditional update, and have workers check the flag at safe checkpoints. A job already in flight may need a compensating action rather than a hard kill.

STEP 6 / 6

Long-running workflows: durable execution

“Send a welcome email, wait 3 days, if they haven't activated send a reminder, after 7 days notify sales.” That's a week-long function with sleeps in the middle — and it must survive deploys, crashes and restarts. Durable execution engines (Temporal, AWS Step Functions, Cadence) solve this by event-sourcing the workflow itself.

How a workflow survives a crash on day 4Step-through
  1. You write await sendEmail(); await sleep('3 days'); if (!activated) await sendReminder();. The engine makes those awaits durable — the process does not sit there for three days.
  2. Each decision and result is appended to an event history: ActivityScheduled(send_email), ActivityCompleted, TimerStarted(3d). This is event sourcing (Chapter 17) applied to control flow.
  3. Anything non-deterministic — an API call, a database write, reading the clock — happens in an activity with its own retries and timeout. The workflow function itself must be deterministic, because it will be re-executed.
  4. A three-day sleep becomes a durable timer in the engine (backed by exactly the scheduler machinery from earlier in this chapter). No worker, no memory, no connection is held.
  5. The worker dies, or you deploy new code, or the whole cluster restarts. Nothing is lost, because no state lives in the process.
  6. A new worker re-runs the workflow function from the top, feeding it the recorded history: the first sendEmail() returns its recorded result instead of sending again, and execution resumes exactly where it stopped. Determinism is what makes this safe — which is why you never call Math.random() or now() directly in workflow code.
🧭
When not to build this

If your needs are “a handful of cron jobs”, use Kubernetes CronJobs or your cloud's scheduler and move on. Build (or adopt) a real scheduler when you need per-tenant schedules created at runtime, millions of timers, visibility into every run, or long-lived workflows. And prefer adopting Temporal/Step Functions over writing a durable-execution engine yourself — replay determinism is a deep rabbit hole.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Ignoring cron alignment. Everyone writes 0 0 * * *; without jitter you build a self-inflicted thundering herd.
  • Promising exactly-once execution. You can guarantee at-most-once triggering; effects need idempotent handlers.
  • Storing schedules in UTC only. “09:00 local” shifts with daylight saving — store the timezone and compute occurrences with a calendar library.
🎤
Interview drill

Design: Support both “every 5 minutes” and “in exactly 30 days” for 100M schedules.

How a strong answer sounds
Durable schedules in a sharded table indexed by next_run_at, with only a rolling window (the next few minutes) loaded into an in-memory hierarchical timing wheel per shard for millisecond precision. Long delays simply sit in the database until their window arrives. Firing enqueues a run row with a unique (job_id, scheduled_for) key, then hands the work to executors via a queue. Recurring schedules compute the next occurrence after each run, in the schedule's timezone.

Operate: The scheduler was down for two hours. What happens to everything that should have fired?

How a strong answer sounds
It depends on a per-schedule catch-up policy, which must be an explicit setting: run all (backfill reports), collapse to one (a sync job only needs to run once), or skip (a “good morning” push is pointless now). Without that policy you either flood downstream systems with 24 duplicate runs or silently lose work. On recovery, also rate-limit the catch-up so it doesn't become its own incident.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Durable schedules in a sharded database, a rolling in-memory timing wheel for precision, and a queue for execution.
  • Cron alignment creates enormous spikes — spread them with deterministic per-job jitter and keep a lane for truly time-critical jobs.
  • A unique key on (job_id, scheduled_for) gives at-most-once triggering; leases with fencing tokens stop zombie workers.
  • Exactly-once side effects are impossible — make handlers idempotent instead.
  • Give each tenant its own queue and concurrency budget; quarantine poison jobs.
  • Store timezones with schedules, and choose an explicit catch-up policy for downtime.
  • Long-running workflows become durable by event-sourcing their history and replaying deterministically.

Check your understanding

Two million jobs are scheduled for “daily at midnight”. What's the best mitigation?

Hash-based jitter flattens the spike while keeping each job's firing time consistent day to day — much cheaper than provisioning for the spike.

A worker fetches a job, calls the payment API, and crashes before recording success. What should the system guarantee?

You can't atomically couple an external call with your own state. Idempotency keys make the inevitable retry safe (Chapter 37).

Why do timing wheels beat “ORDER BY next_run_at LIMIT 500” for millisecond precision at scale?

A wheel trades durability (it's in memory, rebuilt from the store) for constant-time scheduling — so real systems use both.

The scheduler was down for 3 hours. A job scheduled every 5 minutes is now 36 runs behind. What's the right behaviour?

A report might need backfilling; a "send reminder" job must not fire 36 times. Make it an explicit, per-schedule setting.

Why must durable-workflow code be deterministic?

Replay reconstructs state by re-executing the code; randomness, clocks and I/O must live in activities whose results are recorded.
