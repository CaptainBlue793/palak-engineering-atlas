Design a Job Scheduler

“Send this reminder in 3 days.” “Run this report every weekday at 07:00.” “Retry this payout in 15 minutes.” “Run this 12-step onboarding workflow, which takes a week and must survive deploys.” Every platform ends up needing a job scheduler — and gets it subtly wrong. Jobs fire twice. Jobs never fire. A midnight stampede takes out the database.

  • Quickly find which jobs are due, even with 100M+ schedules
  • Compare three ways to do that: polling, time buckets and timing wheels
  • Survive the stampede when huge numbers of cron jobs fire at the same moment
  • Start each job exactly once, even when machines crash
  • Handle retries, priorities and fairness between customers (tenants)
  • Support long-running workflows that survive restarts (durable workflows)

STEP 1 / 6

Requirements

🛠️ Functional

✓One-off jobs (“at 14:05 on 12 Nov”) and repeating schedules (cron expressions — the standard “every weekday at 07:00” syntax — that respect time zones)
✓Run a task: call a service, run a container, send a message
✓Retries with backoff (waiting longer between each try), timeouts, cancellation, manual re-run
✓Multi-step workflows with waits, branches and compensations (“undo” steps)
✓Status, history and alerting per job

📏 Non-functional

✓Fire within ~1 s of the due time, at any scale
✓Never lose a job; never silently skip one
✓Run at least once, with handlers that are safe to repeat (idempotent) — and trigger at most once per scheduled time
✓Many customers share it: one busy customer can't block the others
✓Survive worker crashes, deploys and region failover

Why "at least once" and not "at most once"? When a worker crashes mid-job, the scheduler has two choices: run the job again (risking a duplicate) or give up (risking a lost job). A lost job is usually invisible — nobody notices the invoice that was never sent until a customer complains weeks later. A duplicate can be made harmless by writing the handler so a second run does nothing new. So the standard deal is: the scheduler promises at least once, and job authors promise idempotent handlers. Together they behave like "exactly once" from the outside.

The averages look easy. The problem is when the jobs are due. People write 0 0 * * * (“every day at midnight”). So a huge share of all schedules fall due at exactly midnight, on the hour, or on the minute. A scheduler that handles 12K/s on average will still fall over at 00:00:00 — unless it deliberately spreads that load out.

The arithmetic behind the numbers:

  • Average fire rate: most schedules run hourly or more often, a few run every minute. If the 100M schedules average about 10 runs a day, that is 1B runs/day ÷ 86,400 s ≈ 11.6K/s — the ~12K/s above.
  • Run history: every run writes one history row, so ~1B rows/day. Even at a modest ~200 bytes each that is ~200 GB/day, which is why history is kept in detail for a limited time (say 30 days) and then summarised or moved to cheap storage.
  • Schedule table: 100M × 300 bytes = 30 GB. That would fit on one machine, so size alone doesn't force sharding. The write rate does: every fire updates that schedule's next_run_at, so at 12K fires/s the table takes 12K index updates per second, all clustered around "now".
  • The spike: if 1 in 5 schedules is "daily at 00:00 UTC", that is 20M jobs due in the same second. At an execution capacity of 20K/s, draining them takes 20M ÷ 20K = 1,000 s — the last job runs about 17 minutes late.

How do you find the jobs that are due?

The rest of the system is ordinary service design. This is the part with real choices.

Reading the polling query. FOR UPDATE locks the rows it returns so another poller can't grab the same jobs. SKIP LOCKED tells a second poller to skip rows that are already locked instead of waiting for them. Together they let ten pollers share one table, each taking a different batch of 500, with no job handed out twice. It is the right answer for a small or medium system: one database, no extra moving parts.

Why polling stops scaling. Every poll scans the front of the next_run_at index, and every fired job rewrites its own next_run_at, which moves an index entry. At millions of schedules, all that activity lands on the same small "now" end of one index, on one database. Adding pollers adds lock contention rather than speed. Bucketing and wheels fix this by splitting "what's due now" into many small, independent pieces.

When to pick which: polling up to roughly ten million schedules or when you want the simplest thing; time buckets when you need horizontal scale and second-level precision is enough; a timing wheel (fed from the database) when you need sub-second precision; broker delays only for short retry delays, not for "in 6 months".

🏗️
The pragmatic combination

Real systems combine three parts:

  • A database holds all schedules (the source of truth), sharded by tenant. It survives restarts.
  • A timing wheel in memory, one per shard, holds only a rolling window — the jobs due in the next few minutes. It fires them to the millisecond.
  • A queue hands the work to executors (the workers that run jobs). Firing a job and running it are kept separate, so slow jobs don't delay firing.

Think of a clock face with a list of jobs at each second. Adding a job takes one step: compute slot = (now + delay) % 60 and add it to that slot's list. Each tick fires one slot's list — no scanning, no sorting, no database query. Delays longer than one full turn go into a slower wheel (minutes → hours → days). That stack of wheels is why it's called hierarchical.

A small trace. The wheel has 60 one-second slots and the pointer is at slot 10.

  • A job with a 5 s delay goes into slot (10 + 5) % 60 = 15. It fires 5 ticks later.
  • A job with a 75 s delay would also land in slot (10 + 75) % 60 = 25 — but that would fire it after 15 s, a full turn too early. So a single wheel must also store a "rounds left = 1" counter and skip it once. A hierarchical wheel instead puts it in the minute wheel; when that minute comes round, the job is moved down into the right second slot of the fine wheel (this move is called cascading).
  • A job due in 30 days never enters the wheel at all. It waits in the database until it falls inside the rolling window.

Edge cases the wheel must handle: after a crash the wheel is empty, so on start-up it reloads the window from the database and immediately fires anything already overdue. If a tick takes longer than a second (a huge slot), the next ticks must still run in order, not be skipped. And a job cancelled after it was loaded must be removed from its slot, or checked against the database just before firing.

The midnight stampede

Twenty million jobs are all due at 00:00:00. Your executors can run maybe 20,000 per second. What happens next should be something you decided on purpose, not an accident.

🧪 The trick is deterministic jitter: a small delay that is fixed per job, not random each time. Work out an offset from a hash of the job ID (hash(job_id) % 300s). Now “daily at midnight” really means “somewhere in the next five minutes, but at the same second every day”. Users get predictable timing; you get a flat load. Keep a separate lane for jobs whose timing truly matters.

Worked numbers for the simulator. Naive: 2M jobs arrive at once and drain at 20K/s, so the queue empties after 2M ÷ 20K = 100 s. The last job is 100 s late, and anything urgent is stuck behind the whole pile. With jitter over 300 s: 2M ÷ 300 ≈ 6,700 jobs become due each second — a third of capacity — so there is no backlog at all.

Why a hash and not random()? A random delay chosen fresh each day would make the job run at 00:02:13 today and 00:04:51 tomorrow. Users notice that. A hash of the job ID gives the same answer every time, so the job always runs at, say, 00:02:13. It also means that if two scheduler nodes both compute the time, they agree.

STEP 5 / 6

Firing exactly once

A worker picks up a job, starts running it, and then its process dies. Did the job run? Another worker has to decide. “Just run it again” is only safe if the job is idempotent — running it twice has the same effect as running it once. 🔑 The honest summary: starting a job exactly once is possible. A unique key on (job_id, scheduled_for) means only one run row can ever exist. But having its effects happen exactly once is not guaranteed. The worker can always die after calling the payment API and before recording that it did. That's why the handler must be idempotent (Chapter 17), and why leases (locks that expire) need fencing tokens.

Each safeguard stops a different failure:

  • Unique run key (job_id, scheduled_for) — stops two scheduler nodes (or a retry of the firing step) from creating two runs for the same 09:00 slot. The second insert simply fails.
  • Lease — a worker "borrows" a run for, say, 30 s and must renew it while working. If the worker dies, the lease runs out and another worker may take over. Without a lease, a crashed worker's job would stay "running" forever.
  • Fencing token — fixes the lease's own weak spot, the worker that isn't dead, only paused. Trace it: worker A takes the lease with token 41, then freezes for 40 s (a long garbage-collection pause). At 30 s the lease expires and worker B takes over with token 42 and finishes the payout. A wakes up and tries to record its result with token 41. The store has already seen 42, so it rejects 41. Without the token, A would happily send a second payout.
  • Idempotent handler — covers the gap nothing else can: a worker that called the payment API and died before recording it. The retry passes the same key (e.g. payout:job_88:2025-11-12T09:00) to the payment API, which recognises it and does not pay again.

Execution: fairness, retries and poison jobs

⚖️

Per-tenant fairness

One customer scheduling 5 million jobs must not delay everyone else. Give each tenant its own queue and take turns between them (weighted round-robin), or cap how many jobs each tenant can run at once. Don't use one shared first-come-first-served queue.

🔁

Retries

Wait longer after each failure (exponential backoff), add a little randomness (jitter), and set a maximum number of attempts. Also set a deadline after which running the job is pointless — a “happy birthday” email three days late is worse than none. Failed jobs go to a dead-letter view that shows the error and has a re-run button.

☠️

Poison jobs

A job that crashes its worker every time will take down your whole fleet, one worker at a time. Count failures in a row per job, and set the job aside (quarantine) after N. Run untrusted jobs in isolated sandboxes with CPU and memory limits.

🕰️

Timezones & DST

“Every day at 09:00 Europe/Berlin” is not a fixed offset from UTC, because of daylight saving time (DST). Store the timezone with the schedule, and work out the next run with a proper calendar library. Also decide what to do around the clock change: 02:30 happens twice in autumn and not at all in spring.

⏭️

Catch-up policy

The scheduler was down for 3 hours. Should a 5-minute job now run 36 times? Choose per schedule: run all (backfill), run once (collapse), or skip (only run the next one). Getting this wrong is how an outage turns into a billing incident.

🚫

Cancellation

Cancelling must be safe to repeat and free of races. Mark the run cancelled with a conditional update, and have workers check that flag at safe points. A job that is already running may need an “undo” action rather than being killed outright.

Two of these with numbers. Retries: with a base delay of 1 s that doubles each time, ten attempts wait 1 + 2 + 4 + … + 512 = 1,023 s, about 17 minutes in total. That is usually long enough to ride out a short outage of the service being called, and the jitter stops thousands of failed jobs from retrying in the same second. Timezones: Berlin is UTC+1 in winter and UTC+2 in summer, so "09:00 Europe/Berlin" means 08:00 UTC in January but 07:00 UTC in July. A schedule stored as "08:00 UTC" would silently start running at 10:00 local time every summer.

STEP 6 / 6

Long-running workflows: durable execution

“Send a welcome email, wait 3 days, if they haven't activated send a reminder, after 7 days notify sales.” That's a week-long function with sleeps in the middle. It must survive deploys, crashes and restarts. Durable execution engines (Temporal, AWS Step Functions, Cadence) solve this with event sourcing: they save every step the workflow takes as a log, and rebuild its progress from that log when needed.

Why not just a status column and a cron job? You could store onboarding_step = 'WAITING_REMINDER' and wake_at per user, and have a job check it every minute. That works for one workflow with three steps. It gets painful fast: every new branch needs a new status value, every step needs its own retry and timeout logic, and the real flow is scattered across a state table, a cron job and several handlers, so nobody can read it in one place. Durable execution lets you write the flow as one ordinary function and gives you the retries, timers and history for free. The price is the determinism rule described below.

How a workflow survives a crash on day 4Step-through
  1. You write await sendEmail(); await sleep('3 days'); if (!activated) await sendReminder();. The engine makes those awaits durable (they survive crashes). The process does not actually sit there for three days.
  2. Each decision and result is added to an event history: ActivityScheduled(send_email), ActivityCompleted, TimerStarted(3d). This is event sourcing (Chapter 17), applied to the steps of a program.
  3. Anything that could give a different answer each time — an API call, a database write, reading the clock — happens in an activity, which has its own retries and timeout. The workflow function itself must be deterministic (same input, same steps, every time), because it will be run again later.
  4. A three-day sleep becomes a durable timer in the engine, built on exactly the scheduler ideas from earlier in this chapter. No worker, memory or connection is tied up while it waits.
  5. The worker dies, or you deploy new code, or the whole cluster restarts. Nothing is lost, because no state lives inside the process.
  6. A new worker runs the workflow function again from the top, feeding it the recorded history. The first sendEmail() returns its recorded result instead of sending again, and the code picks up exactly where it stopped. This only works if the code is deterministic. That's why you never call Math.random() or now() directly in workflow code.
🧭
When not to build this

If you only need “a handful of cron jobs”, use Kubernetes CronJobs or your cloud's scheduler and move on. Build (or adopt) a real scheduler when you need schedules that customers create on the fly, millions of timers, a record of every run, or long-lived workflows. And adopt Temporal or Step Functions rather than writing a durable-execution engine yourself — getting replay right is a deep rabbit hole.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Ignoring cron alignment. Everyone writes 0 0 * * *. Without jitter, you create your own thundering herd (a crowd of requests all hitting at once).
  • Promising exactly-once execution. You can guarantee a job is triggered at most once. Its effects still need idempotent handlers.
  • Storing schedules in UTC only. “09:00 local” moves with daylight saving. Store the timezone and work out run times with a calendar library.

Interview drill

Design: Support both “every 5 minutes” and “in exactly 30 days” for 100M schedules.

How a strong answer sounds
Store schedules durably in a sharded table indexed by next_run_at. Load only a rolling window — the next few minutes — into an in-memory hierarchical timing wheel per shard, for millisecond precision. Long delays just sit in the database until their window comes round. Firing a job inserts a run row with a unique (job_id, scheduled_for) key, then hands the work to executors through a queue. Repeating schedules work out their next run after each run, in the schedule's timezone.

Operate: The scheduler was down for two hours. What happens to everything that should have fired?

How a strong answer sounds
It depends on each schedule's catch-up policy, which must be an explicit setting. Run all suits backfilling reports. Collapse to one suits a sync job that only needs to run once. Skip suits a “good morning” push that's pointless now. Without that policy you either flood other systems with 24 duplicate runs or quietly lose work. When recovering, also limit how fast the catch-up runs, so it doesn't cause an incident of its own.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Keep schedules in a sharded database, load the next few minutes into an in-memory timing wheel for precision, and use a queue to run the jobs.
  • Cron jobs all firing on the same second create huge spikes. Spread them with a fixed per-job delay (deterministic jitter), and keep a separate lane for truly time-critical jobs.
  • A unique key on (job_id, scheduled_for) means each job is triggered at most once. Leases with fencing tokens stop “zombie” workers that wake up late.
  • You can't guarantee side effects happen exactly once — make handlers idempotent instead.
  • Give each tenant its own queue and a limit on jobs running at once. Set aside poison jobs that keep crashing.
  • Store the timezone with each schedule, and choose a clear catch-up policy for downtime.
  • Long-running workflows survive crashes by recording their history as events and replaying it with deterministic code.

Check your understanding

Two million jobs are scheduled for “daily at midnight”. What's the best mitigation?

Hash-based jitter flattens the spike, and each job still fires at the same time every day. That's much cheaper than buying enough capacity for the spike.

A worker fetches a job, calls the payment API, and crashes before recording success. What should the system guarantee?

You can't make an outside call and your own database update succeed or fail together. So a retry will happen sometimes; idempotency keys make it safe (Chapter 37).

Why do timing wheels beat “ORDER BY next_run_at LIMIT 500” for millisecond precision at scale?

A wheel is fast because it lives in memory, but that also means it's lost in a crash and must be rebuilt from the database. So real systems use both.

The scheduler was down for 3 hours. A job scheduled every 5 minutes is now 36 runs behind. What's the right behaviour?

A report might need backfilling; a "send reminder" job must not fire 36 times. Make it an explicit, per-schedule setting.

Why must durable-workflow code be deterministic?

Replay rebuilds the state by running the code again. So randomness, clocks and I/O must go in activities, whose results are recorded and replayed.
