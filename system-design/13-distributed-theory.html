Distributed Systems Theory

Once your data lives on more than one machine, the network becomes part of your database. And networks lose messages, delay them and deliver them out of order. This chapter covers the hard limits this creates, and the clever algorithms that work within them.

Why distributed systems are hard

On one machine, a function call either returns or the whole program crashes. Across a network, there's a third outcome. A request can succeed, fail, or vanish with no answer.

When that happens, you can't tell what went wrong. Did the other side die? Is it just slow? Or did it do the work, and only the reply got lost? Think of mailing a letter and hearing nothing back. This is called partial failure (some parts work while others don't), and it's the root of everything in this chapter.

Here is a concrete case. Your order service asks the payment service to charge a customer $40, and waits 2 seconds. No reply comes. There are three possible stories:

  • The request never arrived. The customer was not charged. Retrying is correct.
  • The payment service crashed mid-way. Maybe it charged, maybe not. You don't know.
  • The charge worked, but the reply was lost. Retrying now charges the customer twice.

From the order service's point of view, all three look exactly the same: silence. A timeout tells you "I didn't hear back", never "it failed". That is why later chapters lean so hard on idempotency keys (so a retry is safe) and on algorithms that make progress without needing every machine to answer.

The 8 fallacies of distributed computing

Assumptions that engineers new to distributed systems often make. All of them are false.

1The network is reliable.
2Latency is zero.
3Bandwidth is infinite.
4The network is secure.
5Topology doesn't change.
6There is one administrator.
7Transport cost is zero.
8The network is homogeneous.

Each one hides a real bug. A few examples of what they cost you:

  • "Latency is zero" leads to code that makes 50 small network calls in a loop. At 2 ms each that is 100 ms, not the near-zero you'd get from 50 function calls.
  • "Bandwidth is infinite" leads to sending a 5 MB JSON response to every mobile client, which is slow and costly on a phone network.
  • "Topology doesn't change" leads to hard-coding a server's IP address. The day that server is replaced, everything that calls it breaks.
  • "The network is homogeneous" (all the same) forgets that a request may cross fast data-centre links, slow Wi-Fi and a busy mobile tower on the same trip.

The CAP theorem

A network partition is when the network breaks and some machines can't talk to others, even though they're all still running. When that happens in a distributed data store, you must choose between:

🔒

Consistency

Every read sees the latest write (this is called linearizability), or else gets an error.

🟢

Availability

Every request to a working node gets a real answer, not an error. The answer may be out of date (stale).

✂️

Partition tolerance

The system keeps working even when messages between nodes are lost.

⚠️
The common misunderstanding

“Pick 2 of 3” is misleading. Partitions will happen in any real network, so P isn't optional. What CAP really says is: during a partition, choose C or A. When there's no partition, you can have both.

Why you can't have both during a partition. Picture two copies of your data, one in the US and one in the EU, with x = 1 on both. The cable between them is cut. A user in the US writes x = 2. A moment later a user in the EU reads x. The EU node has only two choices:

  • Answer with x = 1. It stays available, but the answer is stale. It has given up consistency.
  • Refuse, with an error. It cannot check with the US node, so it won't risk a wrong answer. It stays consistent, but has given up availability.

There is no third option. The EU node cannot learn about x = 2 without the network. That one small scenario is the whole proof of CAP.

PACELC: the rest of the story

CAP only talks about partitions, and those are rare. PACELC adds the everyday trade-off: if Partition, choose A or C; Else (normal operation), choose Latency or Consistency.

Why is there a trade-off even when all is well? To keep copies identical, you must wait for other replicas to confirm each write before replying. That waiting adds latency to every request.

Some rough numbers make this real. Say a database keeps copies in Virginia and Frankfurt. A round trip across the Atlantic takes about 80–90 ms. If every write must be confirmed by both regions before you reply, every write takes at least ~90 ms. If you reply as soon as the local copy has it and send it to Frankfurt in the background, the write takes a few milliseconds. But for that ~90 ms, a reader in Frankfurt may see old data. You are trading latency against consistency on every single write, not just during rare outages.

🟢 PA / EL

Stays available during partitions, and fast the rest of the time. Cassandra, DynamoDB.

🔒 PC / EC

Always consistent, and pays for it in latency. Spanner, etcd, VoltDB.

⚖️ Tunable

You pick the consistency level for each request. MongoDB, Cosmos DB, Cassandra (QUORUM).

The consistency spectrum

Consistency isn't just on or off. It comes in levels. Stronger levels are easier to write correct code against, but they cost latency and availability. Click along the spectrum:

A small example shows the difference. Alice posts "I got the job!" and then Bob replies "Congrats!". Carol opens the page:

  • Under eventual consistency, Carol might see Bob's "Congrats!" with no post above it, because the replica she read from got Bob's reply first.
  • Under causal consistency, that can't happen. Bob's reply was caused by Alice's post, so everyone sees the post first.
  • Under read-your-writes, Alice herself always sees her own post after refreshing, even if Carol doesn't yet.
  • Under linearizable consistency, as soon as Alice's post is saved, every reader everywhere sees it.

The rule of thumb: pick the weakest level that your feature can live with, because each step stronger costs speed and uptime.

Time, clocks and ordering

“Just use timestamps” doesn't work in distributed systems. Here's why:

  • Each machine's clock drifts, because the quartz crystals inside aren't perfect.
  • When NTP (the time-sync service) corrects a clock, time can jump backwards.
  • Clocks on different machines commonly differ by several milliseconds (clock skew).

That's enough for “last write wins” to keep the older write by mistake.

Here is how, step by step. Server A's clock is 5 ms fast. Server B's clock is correct.

  1. At real time 10:00:00.000, a user sets their name to "Sam" on server A. A stamps it 10:00:00.005.
  2. At real time 10:00:00.003, the user fixes a typo and sets it to "Sammy" on server B. B stamps it 10:00:00.003.
  3. The replicas compare. "Sam" has the bigger timestamp, so last-write-wins keeps "Sam" and throws away "Sammy".

The newer write lost, and no error was raised anywhere. The user simply sees their fix undone.

⏰
Leslie Lamport's insight (1978)

We usually don't need the real time. We need to know which event could have caused which. If A sent a message and B received it, A happened before B. Logical clocks capture exactly that, using a simple counter instead of a real clock.

Lamport clocks playgroundInteractive
📏

Rules: (1) before any event, clock++. (2) Send the clock value with every message. (3) On receive, clock = max(clock, received) + 1. Result: if A caused B, then L(A) < L(B). The reverse isn't guaranteed: a smaller number doesn't prove A caused B. Vector clocks fix that by keeping one counter per node.

A short trace with three processes, all starting at 0:

  1. P1 has a local event. Its clock goes 0 → 1.
  2. P1 sends a message to P2. Its clock goes 1 → 2, and the message carries 2.
  3. P3 has two local events of its own: clock 0 → 1 → 2.
  4. P2 receives the message. Its own clock is 0, so it sets max(0, 2) + 1 = 3. The receive gets a bigger number than the send, as it must.

Now look at P3's event with value 2 and P1's send with value 2. They have the same number, yet neither caused the other: they happened independently. Lamport clocks can't tell you that. A vector clock can: each node keeps a list of counters, one per node, like [P1:2, P2:0, P3:0]. If neither list is "all less than or equal" to the other, the two events were concurrent. Databases like Riak and Dynamo-style stores use this to spot conflicting writes instead of silently picking one.

Consensus with Raft

How do several machines agree on one value, such as who the leader is or what the next log entry is? Some machines may crash, and messages may be lost. Reaching agreement anyway is called consensus. Raft (used by etcd, Consul, CockroachDB, TiKV, Kafka KRaft) is a consensus algorithm designed to be easy to understand:

Leader election

The leader sends regular “I'm alive” messages (heartbeats). Each follower has a random election timer. If a follower hears no heartbeat before its timer runs out, it becomes a candidate. It increases the term (the election round number) and asks for votes. With votes from a majority, it becomes leader.

Log replication

Clients send commands to the leader. The leader adds each one to its log and copies it to the followers. Once a majority has stored an entry, it's committed (final) and applied.

Safety

Nodes only vote for a candidate whose log is at least as up to date as their own. So a new leader always has every committed entry. Any two majorities share at least one node, so two leaders can't both win (no “split brain”).

Why do the timers need to be random? If all followers had the same timeout, they would all notice the dead leader at the same moment, all become candidates, and all vote for themselves. Nobody would get a majority, and the election would repeat forever. With random timeouts (say, anywhere from 150 to 300 ms), one follower usually wakes up first and collects votes before the others even start.

Why a majority, and not "all" or "any"? Waiting for all nodes means one dead node stops everything. Accepting any single node means two halves of a split cluster could each pick a leader. A majority sits in between. In a 5-node cluster a majority is 3, and any two groups of 3 must share at least one node (3 + 3 = 6, but there are only 5 nodes). That shared node remembers what it already voted for or stored, so two conflicting decisions can't both win.

Distributed transactions: two-phase commit

Sometimes one operation must update databases on different machines atomically (all or nothing). For example: take money in the payments DB and reserve stock in the inventory DB. Two-phase commit (2PC) does this with a coordinator, like a wedding officiant who asks both sides “I do?” before declaring it done:

Why can't the coordinator just tell each database "commit" in one step? Because one of them might fail. If payments commits and then inventory says "sorry, out of stock", the customer has paid for nothing, and payments has already made its change final. The first phase exists so that every participant can say "I'm able to do this" before anyone makes anything final. Only when every answer is yes does anyone commit.

Two-phase commitStep-through
  1. The coordinator asks every participant: “Can you commit transaction 7781?” Each one does all the work, saves it safely to disk, and keeps its locks on the data.
  2. A YES vote is a promise: “I will commit if told to, even if I crash and restart.” A single NO vote cancels (aborts) everything.
  3. Everyone voted yes. So the coordinator saves COMMIT to its own log and tells everyone. Participants commit and release their locks. All-or-nothing across machines ✓
  4. Suppose the coordinator crashes after PREPARE but before sending the decision. The participants are stuck. They promised to obey, so they can't cancel on their own, and they're still holding locks. The system blocks until the coordinator comes back.
  5. Either make the coordinator itself fault-tolerant with consensus (Spanner runs 2PC over Paxos groups). Or avoid distributed transactions entirely with sagas: a chain of local transactions, each with an “undo” step (a compensating action) in case a later step fails (Chapter 15).

When to use 2PC, and when not to. 2PC is a fit when a few databases inside one company, on a fast network, must change together and a short block is acceptable. It's a poor fit across services owned by different teams, across slow links, or with outside systems (a payment provider will not "prepare" and wait for you). Every participant holds its locks for at least two network round trips, so under heavy load 2PC also cuts throughput. In those cases, sagas are the usual answer.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Calling a whole system “CP” or “AP”. The choice is made per operation. A money transfer and a like counter need different answers.
  • Assuming clocks match across machines. Ordering by timestamp silently loses writes when clocks differ by a few milliseconds.
  • Using consensus everywhere. Raft/Paxos are for metadata, choosing leaders and configuration. They're not for every write in a high-volume data path.
🎤
Interview drill

Reason: Which of these need linearizability: a like counter, a username registration, a seat booking, a feed?

How a strong answer sounds
Username registration and seat booking do. Each must be unique, and a stale read causes a real-world clash (two people get the same seat). So send them through a single writer, or a conditional write on one shard. The like counter and the feed don't. Eventual consistency, with a few seconds of lag, is invisible to users and gives you availability and throughput. Saying the difference out loud is the point of the question.

Explain: A network partition splits your 5-node cluster 3–2. What happens, and what do you want to happen?

How a strong answer sounds
In a quorum-based (majority-vote) system, the majority side (3) keeps accepting writes. The minority side (2) must refuse them. Otherwise you get split brain: two sides writing different histories. If staying available on both sides matters more than one agreed order (say, for shopping carts), choose an AP design instead. Then accept that there will be conflicting versions to merge later. The key point: somebody has to lose during a partition. You're choosing who.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Partial failure is what makes distributed systems hard: you can't tell a slow machine from a dead one.
  • CAP: during a partition, choose consistency (refuse requests) or availability (answer with possibly stale data).
  • PACELC: even without partitions, stronger consistency costs latency.
  • Consistency comes in levels: linearizable → sequential → causal → read-your-writes → eventual.
  • Don't trust real-world clocks for ordering. Logical clocks capture what caused what.
  • Raft reaches consensus by electing a leader and copying its log to a majority. It needs a majority of nodes alive.
  • 2PC gives all-or-nothing commits across machines, but gets stuck if the coordinator fails.

Check your understanding

A distributed lock service must never let two clients hold the same lock. During a network partition it should be…

Two clients holding the same lock is a real bug. Refusing requests (being unavailable) is the safe choice. That's why etcd and ZooKeeper are CP.

A 5-node Raft cluster loses 2 nodes. What happens?

Raft needs a majority: ⌊N/2⌋+1 = 3 nodes. A 5-node cluster survives 2 failures. A 3-node cluster survives only 1.

Why is “last write wins by wall-clock timestamp” dangerous?

Machine clocks differ by milliseconds or more. Last-write-wins (LWW) then throws away writes without any error.

According to PACELC, what do you trade when there is NO partition?

Waiting for replicas to agree before responding improves consistency but adds latency to every request.

What's the classic failure mode of two-phase commit?

Once participants have prepared, they can't decide on their own. Because of this blocking, many systems prefer sagas or coordinators backed by consensus.
