Distributed Systems Theory

Once data lives on more than one machine, the network becomes part of your database — and networks lose, delay and reorder messages. This chapter covers the fundamental limits and the clever algorithms that work within them.

  • Understand why distributed systems are hard
  • Apply CAP and PACELC correctly
  • Navigate the consistency spectrum
  • Order events without trusting clocks
  • Watch Raft elect a leader and replicate
  • Know how two-phase commit works (and fails)

Why distributed systems are hard

On one machine, a function call either returns or the whole program crashes. Across a network, a request can succeed, fail, or vanish with no answer — and you can't tell whether the other side died, is slow, or did the work but the reply got lost. This is partial failure, and it's the root of everything in this chapter.

The CAP theorem

In a distributed data store, when a network Partition happens you must choose between:

🔒

Consistency

Every read sees the most recent write (linearizability) — or gets an error.

🟢

Availability

Every request to a working node gets a non-error response — possibly stale.

✂️

Partition tolerance

The system keeps working even when messages between nodes are lost.

⚠️
The common misunderstanding

“Pick 2 of 3” is misleading. Partitions will happen in any real network, so P isn't optional. CAP really says: during a partition, choose C or A. When there's no partition, you can have both.

PACELC: the rest of the story

CAP only talks about partitions — which are rare. PACELC adds the everyday trade-off: if Partition, choose A or C; Else (normal operation), choose Latency or Consistency. Synchronously replicating to keep replicas consistent adds latency to every request.

🟢 PA / EL

Available in partitions, low latency otherwise. Cassandra, DynamoDB.

🔒 PC / EC

Consistent always, pays latency. Spanner, etcd, VoltDB.

⚖️ Tunable

Per-request choice of consistency level. MongoDB, Cosmos DB, Cassandra (QUORUM).

Time, clocks and ordering

“Just use timestamps” fails in distributed systems. Each machine's clock drifts (quartz crystals are imperfect), NTP corrections can make time jump backwards, and skew between machines is commonly several milliseconds — enough for “last write wins” to keep the older write.

⏰
Leslie Lamport's insight (1978)

We usually don't need the real time — we need to know which event could have caused which. If A sent a message that B received, A happened before B. Logical clocks capture exactly that, with a simple counter.

Lamport clocks playgroundInteractive
📏

Rules: (1) before any event, clock++. (2) Send the clock value with every message. (3) On receive, clock = max(clock, received) + 1. Result: if A caused B, then L(A) < L(B). (The reverse isn't guaranteed — vector clocks fix that by keeping one counter per node.)

Consensus with Raft

How do several machines agree on one value — who the leader is, or what the next log entry is — when some may crash and messages may be lost? That's consensus. Raft (used by etcd, Consul, CockroachDB, TiKV, Kafka KRaft) is designed to be understandable:

Leader election

Each follower has a random election timeout. If it hears no heartbeat before it fires, it becomes a candidate, increments the term, and requests votes. A majority of votes makes it leader.

Log replication

Clients send commands to the leader, which appends to its log and replicates to followers. Once a majority has stored an entry, it's committed and applied.

Safety

Nodes only vote for candidates whose log is at least as up-to-date as their own, so a new leader always has every committed entry. Majorities always overlap — no split brain.

Distributed transactions: two-phase commit

What if one operation must update databases on different machines atomically — say, debit in the payments DB and reserve stock in the inventory DB? Two-phase commit (2PC) uses a coordinator:

Two-phase commitStep-through
  1. The coordinator asks every participant: “Can you commit transaction 7781?” Each does all the work, writes it durably, and holds its locks.
  2. A YES vote is a promise: “I will commit if told to, even if I crash and restart.” Any NO vote aborts everything.
  3. All voted yes, so the coordinator durably logs COMMIT and tells everyone. Participants commit and release locks. Atomic across machines ✓
  4. If the coordinator crashes after PREPARE but before sending the decision, participants are stuck: they promised to obey, so they can't abort alone — and they're holding locks. The system blocks until the coordinator recovers.
  5. Make the coordinator highly available with consensus (Spanner runs 2PC over Paxos groups), or avoid distributed transactions altogether with sagas — a sequence of local transactions with compensating actions (Chapter 15).
⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Saying “we'll use CP” or “AP” as a whole-system label. The choice is per operation: a balance transfer and a like counter want different answers.
  • Assuming clocks are comparable across machines. Timestamp-based ordering silently loses writes when clocks disagree by milliseconds.
  • Reaching for consensus everywhere. Raft/Paxos are for metadata, leadership and configuration — not for every write in a high-throughput data path.
🎤
Interview drill

Reason: Which of these need linearizability: a like counter, a username registration, a seat booking, a feed?

How a strong answer sounds
Username registration and seat booking do — they're uniqueness constraints where a stale read causes a real-world conflict, so route them through a single writer or a conditional write on one shard. The like counter and the feed don't: eventual consistency with a few seconds of lag is invisible to users and buys you availability and throughput. Naming the difference out loud is the point of the question.

Explain: A network partition splits your 5-node cluster 3–2. What happens, and what do you want to happen?

How a strong answer sounds
With a quorum-based system, the majority side (3) keeps accepting writes and the minority side (2) must refuse them, or you get split-brain and divergent history. If availability on both sides matters more than a single consistent order — say, for shopping carts — you choose an AP design instead and accept concurrent versions to reconcile later. The important part is that somebody has to lose during a partition; you're choosing who.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Partial failure is what makes distributed systems hard: you can't distinguish slow from dead.
  • CAP: during a partition, choose consistency (refuse) or availability (serve possibly stale data).
  • PACELC: even without partitions, stronger consistency costs latency.
  • Consistency is a spectrum: linearizable → sequential → causal → read-your-writes → eventual.
  • Don't trust wall clocks for ordering; logical clocks capture causality.
  • Raft achieves consensus through leader election and majority-replicated logs; it needs a majority alive.
  • 2PC gives atomic cross-node commits but blocks if the coordinator fails.

Check your understanding

A distributed lock service must never let two clients hold the same lock. During a network partition it should be…

Two clients holding a lock is a correctness violation. Refusing (unavailability) is the safe choice — which is why etcd/ZooKeeper are CP.

A 5-node Raft cluster loses 2 nodes. What happens?

Raft needs ⌊N/2⌋+1 = 3 nodes. A 5-node cluster tolerates 2 failures; a 3-node cluster tolerates only 1.

Why is “last write wins by wall-clock timestamp” dangerous?

Machine clocks disagree by milliseconds or more. LWW then drops writes without any error.

According to PACELC, what do you trade when there is NO partition?

Waiting for replicas to agree before responding improves consistency but adds latency to every request.

What's the classic failure mode of two-phase commit?

Prepared participants can't decide alone. That blocking behaviour is why many systems prefer sagas or consensus-backed coordinators.
