Estimation & Reading a Design Question

In system design you constantly estimate: how many requests per second is that? How much disk per year? How many servers? You rarely need exact answers — you need the right number of zeros, because 300 requests per second is one server and 300,000 is a fleet. This last prerequisite teaches the units, the handful of numbers worth memorising, quick mental math, why averages lie, and how to turn a vague prompt like “design a URL shortener” into clear requirements and numbers.

  • Convert between bits, bytes, KB, MB, GB, TB and PB without mistakes

  • Estimate storage and bandwidth from simple per-item sizes
  • Recall the latency numbers that drive design decisions
  • Explain why percentiles (p50, p99) matter more than averages
  • Split a prompt into functional and non-functional requirements, and translate “nines” into allowed downtime

Why estimate at all?

A design that's perfect for 1,000 users is wrong for 100 million, and a design built for 100 million is wasteful — slow to build, expensive to run — for 1,000. So before choosing any component, you need a rough idea of the scale: how much traffic, how much data, how fast it grows. That's what back-of-envelope estimation gives you: rough, quick numbers, done in your head or on a napkin.

The goal is the right order of magnitude — the right power of ten. Being off by 20% never changes a design. Being off by 1,000× changes everything. So we round aggressively: 86,400 becomes 100,000, 1,024 becomes 1,000, and nobody minds.

Units: bits, bytes and powers of ten

A bit is a single 0 or 1. A byte is 8 bits. Storage and file sizes are counted in bytes (capital B: MB, GB). Network speeds are counted in bits per second (small b: Mbps, Gbps). Mixing them up makes you 8× wrong.

Mental math with powers of ten

Write big numbers as a small number times a power of ten, then multiply the small numbers and add the exponents. Example: 5 million users × 20 requests each = (5 × 10⁶) × (2 × 10¹) = 10 × 10⁷ = 10⁸ requests per day. Dividing works the same way, subtracting exponents: 10⁸ ÷ 10⁵ = 10³. This one habit makes almost every estimate a few seconds' work.

Time, QPS and peaks

A day has 24 × 60 × 60 = 86,400 seconds. Round it to 100,000 (10⁵). Then “per day” becomes “per second” by dividing by 10⁵:

QPS means queries per second (also written RPS, requests per second). It's the standard way to talk about load. Traffic isn't flat — evenings are busier than 4 am, and launches or sales cause spikes — so you estimate an average and multiply by 2–3 for the peak, or more for spiky products like ticket sales. The system has to survive the peak, not the average.

Also separate reads from writes. Most products are read-heavy: a post is written once and viewed hundreds of times. The read:write ratio tells you where to spend effort — caches and read copies for read-heavy systems, careful write paths for write-heavy ones.

Storage and bandwidth

The two other numbers you almost always need follow the same pattern: count × size.

  • Storage = new items per day × bytes per item × days you keep them. Then multiply by the number of copies you keep (usually 3 for safety) and add room for indexes.
  • Bandwidth = requests per second × bytes per response. Convert to bits (× 8) to compare with network links, which are quoted in bits per second.

Worked example: a photo app serves 5,000 photo views per second at peak, each about 300 KB (a resized, compressed image). Bandwidth = 5,000 × 300 KB = 1.5 GB per second = 12 Gbps. That's more than one server's network card comfortably handles, and expensive to pay for from a cloud provider — a strong argument for a CDN, which is exactly where Chapter 6 goes.

Three lessons from that table

Memory is about 1,000× faster than an SSD random read, which is why caches exist. Crossing an ocean costs more than almost anything a computer does locally, which is why we put copies of data near users. Inside one data center a network hop is cheap compared with a cross-continent one, which is why services in the same region can call each other freely. Chapter 4 animates this table and builds full capacity estimates.

Rough per-server capacities help too, as long as you treat them as starting points to check, not facts. A typical web server might handle on the order of 1,000 simple requests per second; a well-tuned relational database a few thousand to tens of thousands of simple queries per second; an in-memory cache like Redis around 100,000 operations per second. Dividing your peak QPS by these gives a first guess at how many machines you need.

Averages lie: percentiles

Suppose 99 requests take 20 ms and one takes 2 seconds. The average is about 40 ms, which describes nobody's experience. System designers describe latency with percentiles instead:

  • p50 (the median): half of requests are faster than this. The typical experience.
  • p99: 99% of requests are faster; 1 in 100 is slower. The bad-but-common experience.
  • p99.9: 1 in 1,000 is slower. Matters at scale — with 10,000 requests a second, that's 10 unhappy requests every second.

The slow tail matters more than it seems: loading one page often makes dozens of backend calls, so a user hits somebody's p99 on almost every page load. Generate some latencies below and compare the markers.

Most requests are fast (~20–60 ms); a few hit a slow path — a cache miss, a garbage-collection pause, a busy disk. The average hides them; p99 exposes them. That's why reliability targets (Chapter 16) are written as percentiles.

Reading a system-design question

A design question — in an interview or at work — usually sounds short and vague: “Design a URL shortener.” Your first job is to turn it into clear requirements: what the system must do, and how well. There are two kinds:

🛠️ Functional requirements

What it does — the features. Usually phrased “users can…”.

“Users can paste a long URL and get a short one.”
“Visiting the short URL redirects to the long one.”

📏 Non-functional requirements

How well it does it — latency, availability, durability (never losing data), consistency, scale, security, cost.

“A redirect takes under 100 ms at p99.”
“Links keep working for 10 years.”

Then ask for, or assume and state, the scale: how many users, how many actions each, how big each item is, how long data is kept. “Make it scalable” means nothing until someone gives a number.

What “scale” roughly means

Peak loadRoughly what it takes (simple requests)
< 100 QPSOne modest server and one database. Most apps in the world live here.
1,000 – 10,000 QPSA few app servers behind a load balancer, a cache in front of the database, read copies of the database.
100,000+ QPSFleets of servers, heavy caching, data split across many databases, queues for slow work — the rest of this course.

Availability: what the “nines” mean

Availability is the share of time a system works. It's quoted in “nines”, and each extra nine cuts the allowed downtime by 10× — and usually costs much more to achieve:

AvailabilityDowntime per yearDowntime per month
99% (“two nines”)≈ 3.65 days≈ 7.3 hours
99.9% (“three nines”)≈ 8.8 hours≈ 44 minutes
99.99% (“four nines”)≈ 53 minutes≈ 4.4 minutes
99.999% (“five nines”)≈ 5.3 minutes≈ 26 seconds

Four nines leaves under five minutes a month — too little for a human to even notice and fix a problem. Systems at that level need automatic failover. That's why the target you pick shapes the whole design (Chapter 16).

Worked example: turn a vague prompt into numbers

“Design a URL shortener. 100 million new links are created per month. Links are clicked 10× more often than they're created. Each link record is about 500 bytes.”

Writes per second

A month has about 30 × 86,400 ≈ 2.6 million seconds. 100,000,000 ÷ 2,600,000 ≈ 40 new links per second on average, maybe 100 at peak.

Reads per second

10× the writes: ≈ 400 redirects per second, maybe 1,000–1,200 at peak. Reads far outnumber writes, so caching popular links will pay off.

Storage

100 million × 500 bytes = 50 GB per month, so ≈ 600 GB per year. Ten years is 6 TB — before copies. Fits on a few machines; not a huge data problem.

Conclusion

Modest write load, read-heavy, small data. So: a simple database plus a cache for hot links. No need for anything exotic. Chapter 26 designs this system end to end.

That's the whole habit: requirements → numbers → what the numbers imply. Chapter 1 turns it into a step-by-step framework, and Chapter 25 into an interview routine.

Common mistakes
  • Mixing up bits and bytes. A “100 Mbps” link moves 12.5 MB per second, not 100.
  • Designing for the average. Systems fall over at the peak. Multiply the average by 2–3× (more for spiky products).
  • Chasing precision. 86,400 vs 100,000 doesn't matter. Getting the number of zeros right does.
  • Forgetting copies. Stored data is usually kept 3 times, plus indexes and backups.
  • Quoting average latency. Use p50 and p99; the average hides the slow tail users actually feel.
  • Jumping into boxes and arrows before asking about requirements and scale. The numbers decide the design.

Interview drill

Estimate: A photo app has 10 million daily users. Each views 50 photos and uploads 1 photo (3 MB) per day. How many reads per second, and how much new storage per day?

How a strong answer sounds
Reads: 10M × 50 = 500M views a day ÷ ~10⁵ seconds ≈ 5,000 per second on average, so plan for 10,000–15,000 at peak. Uploads: 10M a day ÷ 10⁵ ≈ 100 per second. Storage: 10M × 3 MB = 30 TB of new photos a day — about 11 PB a year before any copies. The takeaway: reads are 50× the writes, so cache and use a CDN; and the photos are huge, so they belong in object storage, not the database.

Clarify: You're asked to “design a chat app”. What do you ask before drawing anything?

How a strong answer sounds
Functional scope: one-to-one only or groups too, and how big; online presence; read receipts; media; message history and search. Scale: daily active users, messages per user per day, average message size, how long history is kept. Non-functional: delivery latency target, ordering guarantees, whether messages must never be lost, availability target, and whether it's global. Then I'd turn those into messages per second, concurrent connections and storage per year before choosing components.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Estimation is about the right order of magnitude. Round freely: a day ≈ 10⁵ seconds, 2¹⁰ ≈ 1,000.
  • Storage is in bytes (B); network speed is in bits per second (b). 1 byte = 8 bits.
  • QPS = requests per day ÷ 10⁵; plan for a peak of 2–3× the average; separate reads from writes.
  • Storage = items × size × retention × copies. Bandwidth = QPS × response size (× 8 for bits).
  • Memory is ~1,000× faster than an SSD random read; an ocean round trip (~150 ms) dwarfs local work.
  • Describe latency with percentiles (p50, p99), not averages.
  • Turn every prompt into functional requirements, non-functional requirements and numbers before designing.

Check your understanding

An app has 10 million daily users, each making 10 requests a day, spread evenly. What's the average QPS?

10M × 10 = 100M requests a day. 100,000,000 ÷ 86,400 ≈ 1,160. Shortcut: 1 million a day ≈ 12 per second, so 100 million ≈ 1,200. Plan for 2–3× that at peak.

A service stores 2 million new records a day, 1 KB each, keeps them 5 years, and stores 3 copies. Roughly how much disk is that?

Per day: 2M × 1 KB = 2 GB. Per year: 2 GB × 365 ≈ 730 GB. Five years: ≈ 3.65 TB. Three copies: ≈ 11 TB. Before indexes and backups.

A team reports “average latency 45 ms”. Why might users still complain that the app feels slow?

The average blends many fast requests with a few slow ones. Percentiles show the slow tail directly. Because one page load fans out to many backend calls, a user regularly experiences the tail.

Which of these is a non-functional requirement?

Functional requirements say what the system does (features). Non-functional ones say how well: speed, availability, durability, scale, cost. A latency target is non-functional.

A service promises 99.9% availability. About how much downtime does that allow per month?

A month is about 30 × 24 × 60 = 43,200 minutes. 0.1% of that is ≈ 43 minutes. (99.99% would allow about 4.3 minutes; 99% about 7.2 hours.)
