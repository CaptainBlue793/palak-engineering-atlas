Design a News Feed

Facebook, Instagram, X, LinkedIn: open the app and see recent posts from people you follow, ranked and loaded instantly. The core challenge is deceptively simple — when do we assemble each person's feed: when someone posts, or when someone reads?

  • Scope a feed system and estimate its load
  • Compare fan-out on write vs fan-out on read
  • Solve the celebrity problem with a hybrid
  • Design the publish and read pipelines
  • Understand feed ranking signals

STEP 4 / 6

The core decision: push, pull or hybrid?

📤

Fan-out on write (push)

When someone posts, insert the post ID into every follower's precomputed feed. Reads are a single fast cache lookup. Writes are expensive and slow for huge accounts.

📥

Fan-out on read (pull)

Store posts once. When a user opens the app, fetch recent posts from everyone they follow and merge. Writes are trivial; reads are heavy and slow.

🔀

Hybrid

Push for normal accounts; pull for celebrities at read time and merge. Used in some form by Twitter/X, Instagram and Facebook.

STEP 5 / 6

High-level design

Publish & read pipelines (hybrid)Step-through
  1. Media is uploaded directly to object storage via a pre-signed URL (never through app servers), then the post service receives text + media IDs.
  2. Post saved durably in the sharded posts DB and cached by ID. The author immediately sees it — “read-your-writes” for their own profile.
  3. PostCreated goes to Kafka. The API responds now; fan-out happens asynchronously so posting stays fast.
  4. Workers look up the author's followers. If the author is a celebrity (e.g. >1M followers) → skip. Otherwise push the post ID into each active follower's Redis feed list, trimming to ~500 entries.
  5. Feed service gets GET /feed. Step one: read the precomputed ID list from the feed cache.
  6. For the few celebrities this user follows, pull their latest posts (heavily cached — millions of readers share them) and merge with the pushed IDs.
  7. Batch-fetch post bodies, author info and like counts from caches; filter deleted/blocked content; score with the ranking model; return 20 posts + a cursor.
STEP 6 / 6

Ranking the feed

Chronological feeds are simple but bury what matters. Ranked feeds score each candidate post with signals like affinity (how much you interact with the author), engagement (likes/comments velocity), recency (time decay) and content type preferences. Deep dives

💤

Don't precompute for inactive users

Most accounts don't open the app daily. Only fan out to users active in the last N days; rebuild a feed on demand (pull) when a dormant user returns.

🧠

Store IDs, not posts

Feed caches hold only 8-byte post IDs. 500 IDs × 300M active users ≈ 1.2TB — manageable in a Redis cluster. Post bodies live once in a separate post cache.

🗑️

Deletes & privacy

Pushed IDs can point to deleted posts or blocked authors. Filter during hydration at read time rather than chasing down every copy in millions of feeds.

🔥

Hot posts & counters

A viral post's like count changes thousands of times a second. Buffer counts (write-behind in Redis) and flush periodically; display approximate numbers (“1.2M likes”).

🌍

Multi-region

Serve feeds from the user's region. Replicate posts across regions asynchronously; eventual consistency of a few seconds is acceptable per the requirements.

🧯

Graceful degradation

If ranking is slow, fall back to chronological. If the feed cache is cold, pull a smaller feed. A slightly worse feed is far better than an error screen.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Choosing push or pull globally. The right answer is hybrid, decided per account by follower count.
  • Storing whole posts in every follower's feed. Store IDs and hydrate from a cache — otherwise an edit means rewriting millions of rows.
  • Unbounded feed lists. Cap each user's materialized feed (a few hundred entries) and fall back to a query for deep scrolling.
🎤
Interview drill

Design: A user with 80M followers posts. Trace what happens.

How a strong answer sounds
Nothing fans out. The account is flagged as a celebrity, so the post is written once to the posts store and cached. When each follower loads their feed, the service merges their precomputed feed (from normal accounts) with a small pull query for the handful of celebrities they follow, then ranks and returns. That keeps write amplification bounded while read cost stays roughly constant, because nobody follows thousands of celebrities.

Operate: Feed loads are at p99 = 900 ms and the target is 200 ms. Where do you look?

How a strong answer sounds
Break the request down: feed-ID fetch, hydration of posts, ranking, and media URLs. The usual culprits are hydration (N+1 lookups instead of a multi-get), a cold or undersized cache, ranking model latency, and tail amplification from fanning out to many shards. Fixes in order of payoff: batch the hydration, raise cache hit rate, cap the candidate set before ranking, and add request hedging for the slowest shard.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • The feed problem is a trade-off between write-time and read-time work.
  • Push makes reads O(1) but explodes for accounts with millions of followers.
  • Pull makes posting trivial but reads too expensive at 60K feeds/sec.
  • Hybrid pushes normal accounts, pulls celebrities, and skips inactive users.
  • Fan-out runs asynchronously through a log; feed caches store IDs only.
  • Hydrate, filter and rank at read time; paginate with cursors.

Check your understanding

A pop star with 90M followers posts. In a pure push model, what goes wrong?

Fan-out cost is proportional to followers. That's exactly why celebrity posts are pulled at read time instead.

Why store only post IDs in each user's feed cache?

Copying full posts into millions of feeds wastes memory and makes updates a nightmare. Hydrate IDs from a shared post cache.

Why does the post service publish to Kafka instead of doing fan-out itself?

Async fan-out decouples user-facing latency from the heavy background work (Chapter 14).

A user who hasn't opened the app in 6 months returns. With a hybrid model that skips inactive users, what happens?

Fall back to fan-out on read for cold users — a one-time cost instead of maintaining feeds nobody reads.

What consistency model does a news feed typically need?

Availability and latency matter more than instant consistency for feeds; the author gets read-your-writes on their own profile.
