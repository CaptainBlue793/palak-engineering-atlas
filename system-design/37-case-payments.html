Design a Payment System

Most systems in this course aim to stay up and be fast. Payments aim to be correct. Never charge twice. Never lose a charge. And always be able to prove — years later — exactly what happened to every cent. This case study is where idempotency, ledgers, sagas and reconciliation all come together.

  • Follow the money from checkout to settlement (the money actually arriving) and payout
  • Make each charge happen exactly once using idempotency keys
  • Record money in a double-entry ledger that is never edited, only added to
  • Handle unknown outcomes, webhooks and outages at the PSP (payment service provider)
  • Check your books against the processor's records every day (reconciliation)
  • Keep PCI scope (the card-security rules you must follow) small, and handle refunds and chargebacks

STEP 1 / 6

Requirements

🛠️ Functional

✓Charge a customer's card at checkout (authorize = reserve the money, then capture = actually take it)
✓Full and partial refunds; handle chargebacks (when a customer disputes a charge with their bank)
✓Marketplace: split each payment between sellers and platform fees, then pay sellers out
✓Multiple currencies; ledger and reporting; daily reconciliation
✓Not covered: being the card network itself. We plug into a PSP (Stripe/Adyen)

📏 Non-functional

✓Money moves exactly once — no double charges, no lost charges
✓Auditable & immutable: every change can be traced and rebuilt; records kept ~7 years
✓Correct over always-available: refuse rather than guess
✓PCI DSS compliance (the card industry's security standard); card data never touches our servers
✓Peak 10K payments/s (Black Friday); p99 < 2 s for checkout

Why "correct over always-available"? Compare the two ways of being wrong. If the payment service is down and checkout says "please try again", you lose one sale for a few minutes. If it guesses and charges a card twice, the customer sees two charges, calls support, may file a chargeback (which costs you the money plus a fee of around $15), and may never shop with you again. When a mistake costs more than a refusal, refuse. This is the opposite of a news feed, where showing a slightly stale post is far better than showing an error.

Why authorize and capture are two steps. An online shop often doesn't know at checkout whether it can ship: the item may be out of stock, or the order may be split into several boxes. Authorizing first reserves the money so the customer can't spend it elsewhere. Capturing later, when the order ships, means you only take money for what you actually send. If the order is cancelled, you simply release the authorization — no refund, no fees for a refund, nothing on the customer's statement except a hold that disappears.

10K TPS (transactions per second) is modest for this course. A well-tuned relational database can handle it, sharded by merchant or account. The hard part isn't speed. It's never losing data, being able to prove what happened, and being exact to the cent. That's why payment systems are usually built on boring, proven technology: transactional databases where everyone always sees the same, up-to-date data.

Where the numbers come from. Here is the arithmetic, step by step, so you can redo it with different inputs in an interview:

  • Average rate: 50M payments ÷ 86,400 seconds in a day ≈ 580/s. Round to ~600.
  • Peak rate: shopping is bursty. Black Friday and flash sales can run 10–20× the daily average, so plan for ~10K/s (about 17× average).
  • Ledger rows: each payment writes ~6 lines over its life (capture, platform fee, PSP fee, settlement, payout, sometimes a refund). 50M × 6 = 300M rows/day, and 300M × 365 ≈ 110B rows/year.
  • Storage: 110B rows × 200 bytes ≈ 22 TB/year. Records are kept ~7 years, so ~150 TB in total before replicas and indexes. Old years move to cheaper storage, but they must stay queryable for audits.
  • Peak writes: 10K payments/s × 6 lines = 60K ledger inserts/s at the worst moment. At ~200 bytes each that is only ~12 MB/s of data, but 60K transactional inserts per second is more than one database primary should carry, which is why the ledger is sharded by account.
🪙
Rule zero: never use floating point for money

Floating-point numbers can't store most decimals exactly: 0.1 + 0.2 !== 0.3. Instead, store whole numbers of the smallest unit (cents, or micros for currency exchange) plus an ISO-4217 currency code like USD. Never mix currencies in one amount field. Rounding rules must be written down and applied the same way everywhere — especially when splitting a payment three ways.

Example: split $10.00 between three sellers. In cents that is 1000 ÷ 3 = 333 remainder 1. So two sellers get 333 cents and one gets 334, and a written rule decides who gets the extra cent (say, the first seller). If each service rounded on its own, you could end up paying out 333 + 333 + 333 = 999 cents and "losing" a cent — or paying 1002 and creating one. Also note that not every currency has cents: the Japanese yen has no minor unit, and a few currencies use three decimal places, so the number of decimals comes from the currency code, not from a fixed "× 100".

STEP 3 / 6

The payment flow

Checkout → authorization → capture → settlementStep-through
  1. The card form is an iframe or SDK supplied by the PSP. The browser sends the card straight to the PSP, which sends back a token (pm_1Nxyz…) — a stand-in ID for the card. Your servers never see a card number. That keeps you in the lightest level of PCI rules (SAQ-A). Storing raw card numbers (PANs) is a huge security project nobody wants.
  2. Checkout calls POST /payments with Idempotency-Key: order_7321, the amount in minor units (e.g. cents), the currency and the token. The payment service inserts a row with a unique constraint on the key. If the same request comes again, it hits that constraint and gets the original result back instead of charging again.
  3. The PSP asks the card network and the customer's bank (the issuer) to reserve the money, sometimes with a 3-D Secure check (an extra “is this really you?” step). Authorizing does not move money; it holds the amount for a few days. Online shops authorize at checkout and capture when the order ships. In a physical store, both happen at once.
  4. On capture, the payment row moves to CAPTURED. In the same database transaction, balanced double-entry lines are written to the ledger. So the payment status and the books can never disagree.
  5. The same transaction also writes an outbox row (Chapter 17). A relay process publishes it as a PaymentCaptured event. Fulfilment ships the order and emails go out. There's no two-phase commit (a slow, fragile cross-service lock-step) anywhere.
  6. Disputes, delayed captures, bank transfers and failures arrive later as signed webhooks (the PSP calling your URL). Check the signature, ignore duplicates by event ID, and cope with events arriving out of order — a refund.succeeded can arrive before charge.succeeded.
  7. Days later, the PSP deposits the money (minus its fees) into your bank account and sends a settlement report. A daily job matches every line against your ledger. Sellers are paid in batches from the balance you owe them (merchant-payable).

The payment as a state machine

Each payment row has a status, and only certain moves between statuses are allowed. Writing them down as a state machine is what lets you handle late, duplicated and out-of-order webhooks safely: you check "is this move allowed from the current state?" before applying anything.

  • CREATED → AUTHORIZED → CAPTURED → SETTLED is the happy path.
  • AUTHORIZED → VOIDED when an order is cancelled before shipping. Authorizations also expire after some days (the exact limit depends on the card network and type), so a job must capture or void them in time.
  • CAPTURED → PARTIALLY_REFUNDED / REFUNDED / DISPUTED. The sum of refunds can never exceed the captured amount — check that inside the same transaction that records the refund.
  • PENDING_UNKNOWN is entered when a call to the PSP times out, and left only when you learn the real outcome (see below).

Example: a refund.succeeded webhook arrives for a payment still marked AUTHORIZED, because charge.succeeded is delayed. A naive handler would record a refund on money you never captured. A state-machine handler sees the move is not allowed yet, stores the event, and applies it once the capture event arrives (or asks the PSP for the payment's current status).

Deep dive: exactly-once charging

Networks time out, users double-click, mobile apps retry, and load balancers resend requests. Without idempotency (making a repeated request have the same effect as doing it once), each of those can become a second charge on a real customer's card.

🔑 The key must come from the client and be based on the order — not a new random value on each attempt. Store it with a unique constraint, and on a repeat, return the saved response. Keep keys for ~24 h. If a request with the same key arrives with a different amount, reject it — that's a bug, not a retry.

Why a unique constraint, and not "check first"?

The obvious approach is: look up the key, and if it isn't there, charge. That fails when two copies of the request arrive at the same moment — exactly what a double-click produces:

  1. Request A: SELECT … WHERE key = 'order_7321' → nothing found.
  2. Request B, 3 ms later: the same SELECT → still nothing, because A hasn't inserted yet.
  3. Both insert a payment and both call the PSP. Two charges.

With a unique constraint, the database itself makes the decision atomically: A's insert succeeds and B's insert fails with a duplicate-key error. B then reads A's row. If A is still in progress, B returns "409 — request in progress, retry shortly"; if A has finished, B returns A's saved response. There is no window where both can win.

Why the key comes from the order, not a random value per attempt: if the app generated a fresh random key on every retry, each retry would look like a brand-new payment and the constraint would never fire. order_7321 stays the same no matter how many times the request is sent, so every copy collides with the first one.

❓
The unknown-outcome problem

You call the PSP and the connection dies. Did the charge happen? From your side, you can't know. The only safe moves:

  • Always send an idempotency key to the PSP too, so retrying can't charge twice.
  • Mark the payment PENDING_UNKNOWN and ask the PSP about your key before retrying.
  • Never mark a payment failed just because it timed out. Check first.

Why is "mark it failed" so dangerous? Walk it through: the charge actually went through, you show "payment failed", the user tries again with a new order, and now they have been charged twice for one purchase — the exact thing this system exists to prevent. Why is "retry without asking" dangerous? Without a key at the PSP, the retry is a second charge. The query-then-decide approach costs a few seconds of waiting; the other two cost real money.

Reconciliation is the final safety net for anything that still slips through.

Deep dive: the double-entry ledger

A ledger is an append-only list of entries: you only ever add to the end. Every event writes lines where money taken out of one account (debits) equals money put into another (credits). So the books always balance, and any account's balance is just the sum of its history.

Nothing is ever changed or deleted. A mistake is fixed by adding a correcting entry — like writing a new line in a pen-and-paper chequebook instead of erasing. Accounting has worked this way for 500 years.

Here is one entry set, worked by hand. A $50 order is captured and the platform keeps a 10% fee (amounts in cents):

AccountDebitCreditMeaning
PSP receivable5000the PSP now owes us $50
Merchant payable4500we now owe the seller $45
Platform fee revenue500we earned $5
Total50004500 + 500 = 5000balanced ✓

The check "debits = credits" is a built-in bug detector. If code forgets the fee line, the set sums to 5000 vs 4500, and the database (or a check before commit) refuses it. A plain balance column has no such self-check.

Why not just keep a balance column and update it? Besides losing the history, it invites a classic race. Two refunds for the same seller run at once: both read balance = $100, both subtract $20, both write $80. The seller should be at $60 — $20 has silently vanished (a "lost update"). Appending two ledger lines can't lose either one; the balance is simply their sum. And when an auditor asks "why is this seller owed $60?", the answer is the list of lines, not a number that someone overwrote.

STEP 5 / 6

Deep dive: reconciliation

However careful your code is, your records will drift from reality. Causes include timeouts, webhooks that never arrived, adjustments made by the PSP, currency rounding and fraud reversals. Reconciliation is the daily job that compares three records line by line: your ledger, the processor's settlement file and the bank statement. Every mismatch raises an alert. Until the mismatches are resolved, the books can't be closed for the day.

Why three records and not just two? Your ledger says what you think happened. The PSP's settlement file says what the processor says happened. The bank statement shows what money actually arrived. Matching ledger ↔ PSP file catches missing or duplicate charges; matching PSP file ↔ bank catches a payout that never landed or a fee you weren't told about.

A sized example. At 50M payments a day, even a 0.01% mismatch rate is 5,000 rows to explain every morning. So the job must sort mismatches into buckets and auto-resolve the known, harmless kinds (below), leaving only the truly odd ones for a human. One common harmless kind is a timing difference: a charge captured at 23:59 may land in tomorrow's settlement file because the PSP's day ends at a different time (or time zone) than yours. The usual fix is to wait one extra cycle before calling an unmatched row a real problem.

Typical results: in our ledger but not in the file — still in progress, or a charge we think worked but didn't. In the file but not in our ledger — the unknown-outcome case: the customer was charged, so we must record it and ship the order. Amount mismatch — partial capture, currency exchange or fees. Duplicates — a real double charge: refund right away and page someone.
STEP 6 / 6

Wrap-up: the rest of the iceberg

🔀

Multi-PSP routing

Send payments to different PSPs based on cost or country, or away from one that is having an outage. Retry a soft decline (a “try again” refusal, not a hard no) on a second processor. Keep a layer in between so switching PSPs doesn't mean rewriting the ledger — but expect their webhooks to behave quite differently.

🧾

Sagas with orders

“Reserve stock → charge → ship” spans several services. Use a saga with compensations (Chapter 15) — each step has an “undo” step. A failed shipment triggers a refund rather than rolling back a transaction. The refund itself must also be safe to repeat (idempotent).

💱

Multi-currency

Store all three: the original currency and amount, the exchange (FX) rate used, and the converted amount. Never recalculate an old conversion using today's rate. Keep separate accounts per currency so the ledger balances within each currency.

🕵️

Fraud & risk

Real-time scoring runs before authorization, within a strict time limit. It uses signals like how many attempts in a short time (velocity), device fingerprint, mismatched details and ML models. More checks run in the background afterwards, feeding human review queues. You trade off blocking good customers against the cost of chargebacks.

⚖️

Chargebacks

Weeks later, a customer disputes a charge with their bank. The money is pulled back from you, plus a fee, and you must send evidence before a deadline. Treat disputes as a proper state machine with deadlines (SLAs), not an afterthought.

📤

Payouts

Pay sellers in scheduled batches from the balance you owe them. Hold some money back as a reserve for future refunds. Handle failed bank transfers (retry, notify, reverse the entry) and tax and KYC (know-your-customer identity check) rules.

🎤
What interviewers listen for

Idempotency keys from start to finish · a double-entry ledger written in the same transaction as the status change · an outbox instead of writing to two systems separately · a clear plan for the unknown outcome · reconciliation as a safety net · money as integers · auditability. The classic miss is saying “the payment service calls the PSP and updates the order” without any of that.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Treating a timeout as a failure. The charge may have gone through. Check using the idempotency key before retrying or refunding.
  • Floating-point money. Use whole numbers of the smallest unit (cents), and store the currency with every amount.
  • Overwriting a stored balance instead of adding ledger entries. You lose the audit trail and gain race conditions.
  • Trusting webhooks blindly. An unsigned or unverified webhook lets anyone on the internet mark an order as paid. Verify the signature, then treat the webhook as a hint and apply it through the state machine.
  • Generating a new idempotency key on every retry. Then no two retries ever match, and the key protects nothing.
🎤
Interview drill

Design: A customer is charged twice for one order. Walk me through how that happened and how you prevent it.

How a strong answer sounds
Most likely the client retried after a timeout, and the second request created a second charge because there was no idempotency key. Or there was a key, but no unique constraint enforced it, so two requests arriving at the same time both got inserted. Prevention: a client-supplied key based on the order, a unique index on it, the saved response returned on a repeat, and the same key passed on to the PSP. Detection: reconciliation flags the duplicate the next day. Fix: an automatic refund plus an alert, because silent double charges destroy trust.

Reason: Why do payment systems prefer boring, strongly consistent technology?

How a strong answer sounds
Because when eventual consistency goes wrong here, it costs real money. A lost write is a lost payment. A write applied twice is a double charge. Reading things in the wrong order gives a wrong balance. Meanwhile the speed needed is modest — 10K TPS is doable on a sharded relational database — so there's no reason to give up correctness for scale. The interesting engineering is in idempotency, ledgers, reconciliation and failure handling, not in the database choice.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Keep card data out of your systems: let the PSP swap cards for tokens, which shrinks your PCI scope.
  • Idempotency keys make each charge happen exactly once. The client supplies them, a unique constraint enforces them, and the response is saved for repeats.
  • Never treat a timeout as a failure. Ask the PSP by key, or reconcile, before retrying.
  • Record money as balanced double-entry lines that are never edited, written in the same transaction as the status change.
  • Publish payment events through an outbox; let fulfilment react to them later.
  • Verify webhook signatures, drop duplicates and put them in order. Expect them to arrive late and out of order.
  • Reconcile daily against the processor and the bank. Treat every mismatch as an incident.
  • Use integers for money, store the FX rate alongside amounts, and refuse rather than guess.

Check your understanding

Your API call to the PSP times out after 30 s. What should the payment service do?

A timeout tells you nothing about whether the charge happened. Checking with the same, unchanging key is the only safe way to find out.

Why write ledger entries in the same database transaction as the payment status change?

The status and the accounting must always change together, all or nothing — that's the core rule. Effects on other systems then flow from an outbox.

What must be true of every entry set in a double-entry ledger?

Balanced entries that are never edited make the books check themselves and easy to audit. Mistakes are fixed with new, reversing entries.

Reconciliation finds a charge in the PSP settlement file that doesn't exist in your ledger. Most likely cause and action?

The customer really paid. Your records must catch up, and the customer must get what they bought.

Why should amounts be stored as integer minor units?

Being off by fractions of a cent, across billions of rows, is both an accounting failure and an audit nightmare.
