Design a Payment System

Everything else in this course optimizes for availability and latency. Payments optimize for correctness: never charge twice, never lose a charge, and always be able to prove — years later — exactly what happened to every cent. This case study is where idempotency, ledgers, sagas and reconciliation come together.

  • Model the flow from checkout to settlement and payout
  • Make charges exactly-once with idempotency keys
  • Record money in an immutable double-entry ledger
  • Handle unknown outcomes, webhooks and PSP outages
  • Reconcile your books against the processor daily
  • Keep PCI scope small and handle refunds and chargebacks

✓Auditable & immutable: every change reconstructable, retained ~7 years
✓Consistency over availability: refuse rather than guess
✓PCI DSS compliance; card data never touches our servers
✓Peak 10K payments/s (Black Friday); p99 < 2 s for checkout

10K TPS is modest by this course's standards — a well-tuned relational database can do it, sharded by merchant or account. The hard requirements aren't throughput; they're durability, auditability and exactness. That's why payment systems are usually built on boring, transactional, strongly consistent technology.

🪙
Rule zero: never use floating point for money

0.1 + 0.2 !== 0.3. Store integer minor units (cents, or micros for FX) plus an ISO-4217 currency code, and never mix currencies in one amount field. Rounding rules must be explicit and consistent — especially when splitting a payment three ways.

STEP 3 / 6

The payment flow

Checkout → authorization → capture → settlementStep-through
  1. The card form is an iframe/SDK from the PSP. The browser sends the card directly to the PSP, which returns a token (pm_1Nxyz…). Your servers never see a card number, which keeps you in the lightest PCI scope (SAQ-A). Storing raw PANs is a project nobody wants.
  2. Checkout calls POST /payments with Idempotency-Key: order_7321, the amount in minor units, currency and the token. The payment service inserts a row with a unique constraint on the key — a retry hits the constraint and returns the original result instead of charging again.
  3. The PSP asks the network and issuer to reserve the funds (possibly with 3-D Secure challenge). Authorization is not money movement: it holds the amount for a few days. E-commerce authorizes at checkout and captures on shipment; in-store does both at once.
  4. On capture, the payment row moves to CAPTURED and balanced double-entry lines are written in the same database transaction. State and books can never disagree.
  5. The same transaction writes an outbox row (Chapter 17). A relay publishes PaymentCaptured; fulfilment ships the order, and emails go out. No cross-service two-phase commit anywhere.
  6. Disputes, delayed captures, bank transfers and failures arrive as signed webhooks. Verify the signature, dedupe by event ID, and handle out-of-order delivery (a refund.succeeded can arrive before charge.succeeded).
  7. Days later the PSP deposits the net amount in your bank account and provides a settlement report. A daily job reconciles every line against your ledger, and payouts to sellers are batched and paid from the merchant-payable balance.

Deep dive: exactly-once charging

Networks time out, users double-click, mobile apps retry, and load balancers replay requests. Without idempotency, each of those can become a second charge on a real customer's card.

🔑 The key must be supplied by the client (derived from the order, not random per attempt), stored with a unique constraint, and the stored response returned on replay. Keep keys ~24 h. If a request with the same key arrives with a different amount, reject it — that's a bug, not a retry.
❓
The unknown-outcome problem

You call the PSP and the connection dies. Did the charge happen? You cannot know from here. The only safe moves: (1) always send an idempotency key to the PSP too, so a retry is harmless; (2) mark the payment PENDING_UNKNOWN and query the PSP by your key before retrying; (3) never let a timeout mark a payment failed without verification. Reconciliation is the final safety net for whatever still slips through.

Deep dive: the double-entry ledger

A ledger is an append-only list of balanced entries. Every event writes lines whose debits equal credits, so the books always balance and any balance is a sum over history. Nothing is ever updated or deleted — a mistake is fixed with a correcting entry, exactly like accounting has worked for 500 years.

STEP 5 / 6

Deep dive: reconciliation

However careful the code is, reality drifts: timeouts, webhooks that never arrived, PSP-side adjustments, currency rounding, fraud reversals. Reconciliation is the daily job that compares your ledger with the processor's settlement file and the bank statement, line by line. Every discrepancy is an alert, and unresolved ones block the books from closing.

Daily three-way matchSimulator
Typical outcomes: in our ledger but not in the file (still in flight, or a charge we think succeeded and didn't), in the file but not in our ledger (the unknown-outcome case — the customer was charged, so we must book it and fulfil the order), amount mismatch (partial capture, FX, fees) and duplicates (a real double charge → refund immediately and page someone).
STEP 6 / 6

Wrap-up: the rest of the iceberg

🔀

Multi-PSP routing

Route by cost, by country, or away from a PSP having an outage. Retry a soft decline on a second processor. Keep an abstraction layer so a PSP swap doesn't rewrite the ledger — but expect real differences in webhook semantics.

🧾

Sagas with orders

“Reserve stock → charge → ship” spans services. Use a saga with compensations (Chapter 15): a failed shipment triggers a refund, not a rolled-back transaction, and the compensation is itself idempotent.

💱

Multi-currency

Store the original currency and amount, the FX rate used, and the converted amount — all three. Never re-derive a historical conversion at today's rate. Keep per-currency accounts so the ledger balances within each currency.

🕵️

Fraud & risk

Real-time scoring (velocity, device fingerprint, mismatch signals, ML models) runs before authorization with a strict latency budget, and asynchronously afterwards for review queues. Balance false declines against chargeback costs.

⚖️

Chargebacks

Weeks later a customer disputes a charge. You get the money pulled back plus a fee, and must submit evidence within a deadline. Model disputes as a first-class state machine with SLAs, not an afterthought.

📤

Payouts

Batch seller payouts on a schedule from the merchant-payable balance; hold reserves against future refunds; handle failed bank transfers (retry, notify, reverse the entry) and tax/KYC requirements.

🎤
What interviewers listen for

Idempotency keys end to end · double-entry ledger written in the same transaction as the state change · outbox instead of dual writes · explicit handling of the unknown outcome · reconciliation as a safety net · integer money · auditability. Saying “the payment service calls the PSP and updates the order” without any of that is the classic miss.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Treating a timeout as a failure. The charge may have succeeded; verify by idempotency key before retrying or refunding.
  • Floating-point money. Use integer minor units and store the currency with every amount.
  • Updating a stored balance instead of appending ledger entries. You lose auditability and gain race conditions.
🎤
Interview drill

Design: A customer is charged twice for one order. Walk me through how that happened and how you prevent it.

How a strong answer sounds
Most likely the client retried after a timeout and the second request created a second charge, because there was no idempotency key — or the key wasn't enforced with a unique constraint, so two concurrent requests both inserted. Prevention: client-supplied key derived from the order, a unique index on it, the stored response returned on replay, and the same key passed to the PSP. Detection: reconciliation flags the duplicate next day; remediation: automatic refund plus an alert, because silent double charges destroy trust.

Reason: Why do payment systems prefer boring, strongly consistent technology?

How a strong answer sounds
Because the failure modes of eventual consistency are financial. A lost write is a lost payment; a double-applied write is a double charge; an unordered read is an incorrect balance. Throughput requirements are modest (10K TPS is achievable on a sharded relational database), so there's no reason to trade correctness for scale. The interesting engineering is in idempotency, ledgers, reconciliation and failure handling — not in the datastore.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Keep card data out of your systems: tokenize via the PSP to shrink PCI scope.
  • Idempotency keys — client-supplied, uniquely constrained, response cached — make charges exactly-once.
  • Never treat a timeout as a failure; query by key or reconcile before retrying.
  • Record money as immutable, balanced double-entry lines written atomically with state changes.
  • Publish payment events through an outbox; drive fulfilment asynchronously.
  • Verify, dedupe and order webhooks; expect them out of order and delayed.
  • Reconcile daily against the processor and bank; treat every discrepancy as an incident.
  • Use integers for money, store FX rates with amounts, and prefer refusing over guessing.

Check your understanding

Your API call to the PSP times out after 30 s. What should the payment service do?

A timeout says nothing about whether the charge happened. Verification with a stable key is the only safe resolution.

Why write ledger entries in the same database transaction as the payment status change?

Atomicity between state and accounting is the core invariant; external effects then flow from an outbox.

What must be true of every entry set in a double-entry ledger?

Balanced, immutable entries make the books self-checking and auditable; corrections are new reversing entries.

Reconciliation finds a charge in the PSP settlement file that doesn't exist in your ledger. Most likely cause and action?

The customer really paid. Your records must catch up, and the customer must get what they bought.

Why should amounts be stored as integer minor units?

Cent-level drift across billions of rows is both an accounting failure and an audit nightmare.
