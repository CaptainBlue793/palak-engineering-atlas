Graph & Social Data

Followers, friends, org charts, permissions, fraud rings, recommendations, dependency trees: a huge share of interesting data is about relationships. Relational databases store relationships fine. But they are bad at following them step by step (traversing). A simple question like “who are the friends of my friends?” can become a query that overloads a production database.

  • See why multi-hop queries explode in SQL
  • Model graphs as edge tables, adjacency lists or property graphs
  • Traverse efficiently — including bidirectional search
  • Build “people you may know” from mutual friends
  • Understand how Facebook-scale social graphs are actually served
  • Know when a graph database is (and isn't) the answer

The multi-hop problem

A hop is one step along a relationship: from you to a friend is one hop, to a friend's friend is two. In SQL, each hop is another self-join (joining the friendship table to itself).

Say everyone has 200 friends on average. One hop touches 200 rows. Two hops touch 40,000. Three hops touch 8 million. And much of that is wasted, because most of those paths lead to the same people again.

Why so much repetition? Friends tend to know each other. If you and Ben share 30 friends, and you and Cara share 25, then walking "you → Ben → his friends" and "you → Cara → her friends" visits many of the same people twice. The join builds a row for every path, not every person, and only DISTINCT at the very end throws the duplicates away — after all the work has been done. That's why the calculator below shows far more rows produced than people reached.

-- "Friends of friends of friends" — three self-joins, exponential intermediate rows
SELECT DISTINCT f3.friend_id FROM friends f1
  JOIN friends f2 ON f2.user_id = f1.friend_id
  JOIN friends f3 ON f3.user_id = f2.friend_id
WHERE f1.user_id = 42;

Real social networks are “small worlds”: with 200 friends each, almost everyone is within 4–5 hops of you. So a traversal with no limit doesn't just get slow. It ends up returning half the planet. Real systems limit the depth, limit how many neighbours they follow from each person (fan-out), and rank results hard.

Three ways to store a graph

A simple table of (from_id, to_id, type, created_at), with an index in each direction. Each row is one edge (one relationship). It supports transactions, everyone knows it, and it's perfectly good for one or two hops.

CREATE TABLE edges (
  from_id  BIGINT, to_id BIGINT, type SMALLINT, created_at TIMESTAMPTZ,
  PRIMARY KEY (from_id, type, to_id)          -- "who do I follow?" is a range scan
);
CREATE INDEX ON edges (to_id, type, from_id);    -- "who follows me?" — the reverse index

Sharded by from_id, so all of one user's outgoing edges live on the same shard. The reverse index (“who points at me?”) must be kept up to date separately. Often that's a second row written in the same transaction, or written later in the background through an outbox (Chapter 17).

Store each node's (person's) neighbours as a single value: a list, a wide-column row, or a Redis sorted set. One key lookup returns all their connections at once. That's exactly what a traversal wants.

# Redis / wide-column shape
following:42        → [17, 88, 1201, …]        # sorted set scored by time
followers:42        → [3, 17, 954, …]
counts:42           → {following: 312, followers: 11840}   # denormalized counters
Strengths
  • One read per hop, no joins
  • Easy to cache and to shard by node
  • Fast “is A connected to B?” by checking if B is in A's set
Weaknesses
  • Huge lists for celebrities (millions of entries)
  • Two writes per edge (one per direction), which you must keep in sync
  • No query language for one-off questions

A dedicated graph database stores nodes and edges as real objects, each with its own properties. It uses index-free adjacency: each node holds direct pointers to its edges. So a hop just follows a pointer, instead of doing an index lookup.

// Cypher (Neo4j): people my friends know, that I don't, ranked by mutual friends
MATCH (me:Person {id: 42})-[:FRIEND]->(f)-[:FRIEND]->(fof)
WHERE fof <> me AND NOT (me)-[:FRIEND]->(fof)
RETURN fof.name, count(f) AS mutuals
ORDER BY mutuals DESC LIMIT 10;

Good when following the connections is the whole question: fraud rings, supply chains, permission hierarchies, knowledge graphs. Less good as the main store for high-volume transactional data. Spreading it across many machines is also genuinely hard (see below).

Which one when? Start with the edge table if you already run a relational database and your queries are one or two hops ("who do I follow?", "does A follow B?"): you get transactions and SQL for free. Move hot paths to adjacency lists in a key-value store or cache when read volume is huge and every read is "give me this person's connections" — one lookup instead of an index scan. Choose a property graph database when deep or unpredictable traversals are the main job and the data fits on a few machines. Many real systems use two: an edge table as the source of truth, with adjacency lists cached in front of it.

Shortest path: search from both ends

“How am I connected to this person?” is a shortest-path query. The basic tool is BFS (breadth-first search): visit all friends, then all their friends, and so on, layer by layer.

A one-way BFS to depth 4, with 200 friends each, visits ~1.6 billion nodes. Instead, search from both people at once and meet in the middle. That's two searches of depth 2 (about 40,000 nodes each), roughly the square root of the work. It's like two friends digging a tunnel from opposite ends.

A small trace. Suppose the real path is Ann → Ben → Cal → Dee → Eve. A one-way BFS from Ann has to expand layer 1 (Ann's friends), layer 2, layer 3, and reach layer 4 before it finds Eve. A two-way search takes turns: expand Ann's friends (finds Ben), expand Eve's friends (finds Dee), expand Ben's friends (finds Cal), expand Dee's friends — and Cal is already marked as seen from Ann's side. The two searches have met, so the path is Ann → Ben → Cal plus Cal → Dee → Eve. Each side only went 2 layers deep. Two practical rules: always expand whichever side's frontier (the current outer layer) is smaller, and stop as soon as any node is seen from both sides.

How a social graph is really served

Facebook's TAO is the classic design, and it's boring on purpose. The source of truth is ordinary MySQL, split into shards. Everything that makes it fast is caching.

Why caching works so well here: a social graph is read far more than it's written. People look at profiles, feeds and friend lists constantly, but add a friend only now and then. Say the service gets 1 billion reads per second and 99% hit a cache. The databases then see 10 million reads per second — still a lot, but spread over thousands of shards. At a 90% hit rate they'd see 100 million, ten times more. Every percentage point of hit rate is worth a fleet of database machines.

A read-mostly graph serviceStep-through
  1. The API is deliberately tiny: obj_get(id), assoc_get(id, type, limit), assoc_count(id, type), assoc_range. There are no free-form queries, so every way of reading data is limited in size and easy to cache.
  2. A follower cache close to the application handles most reads. On a miss, it asks the leader cache for that shard. The leader puts writes in order and shields the database from stampedes, where many requests miss at once (Chapter 6).
  3. Well over 90% of reads are cache hits, so MySQL sees only a trickle. Edges are sharded by from_id, so reading someone's connections is one range scan on one shard.
  4. A new friendship writes the edge, adds 1 to the counter, and invalidates (deletes) the affected cache entries in every region. Counts are stored separately (denormalized) because you can't run COUNT(*) over 10 million followers on every page view.
  5. “A follows B” also has to update B's follower list. That second write happens later, in the background, and is idempotent (safe to repeat). So the two directions can disagree for a moment. At this scale, that trade-off is accepted (Chapter 17).
⭐

Celebrity nodes

A node with 100M edges breaks every assumption. You can't read the whole list, cache it, or send something to everyone on it. Store its counts separately, read the list in pages, and treat celebrities as a special case (Chapter 27's hybrid fan-out).

✂️

Partitioning is genuinely hard

Splitting a graph across machines means cutting edges, and every cut edge becomes a network call. Finding the best balanced split is NP-hard (no fast exact method is known). So systems simply shard by node ID and accept edges that cross shards. That's another reason to keep traversals shallow.

🔒

Privacy at read time

Visibility rules (blocked, private, friends-only) must be applied every time a relationship is read. That's a permission check for this particular viewer, possibly on thousands of edges (Chapter 11). Even precomputed results must be filtered before they're shown.

Graph algorithms you'll be asked about

ProblemApproachWhere it's used
Mutual friends / PYMK2-hop neighbourhood intersection, ranked by count + featuresFriend suggestions, LinkedIn's degrees
Degrees of separationBidirectional BFS with fan-out capsConnection paths, spam-ring analysis
Influence / authorityPageRank: nodes pass importance to their neighbours, repeated until the scores settleWeb search, recommendation seeds, trust scoring
CommunitiesLabel propagation, Louvain modularityGroup suggestions, fraud rings, topic clusters
Similar items / peopleNode embeddings (node2vec, GNNs) + vector searchRecommendations (Chapter 10's ANN index)
Triangle counting / clustering coefficientEdge intersection over neighboursSpam detection, network health metrics

A few of these in plain words:

  • Mutual friends — take your friend set and theirs and keep the overlap. If you have 300 friends and they have 5,000, loop over your 300 and check each one against their set held in a hash set (constant-time lookups): 300 quick checks, not 1.5 million comparisons.
  • PageRank — every node starts with the same score. In each round, each node splits its score among the nodes it links to. After enough rounds (often a few dozen) the scores stop changing, and nodes that many important nodes point to end up on top.
  • Label propagation — give every node its own label, then repeatedly let each node take the label most common among its neighbours. Tightly knit groups quickly settle on a shared label, and those groups are your communities.
  • Triangle counting — a triangle is three people who all know each other. Real friend groups have many; fake accounts that befriend random strangers have very few, which makes it a useful spam signal.
🧮
Online vs offline

Heavy algorithms (PageRank, communities, embeddings) run offline. A batch system (Spark GraphX, Pregel-style engines) processes a snapshot of the graph and saves the results to a fast store. Online, while a user waits, you only do 1–2 hop lookups plus a ranking model. Mixing up the two is the classic design mistake here.

Do you actually need a graph database?

Reach for one when…
  • Traversals of unknown depth are the main query (“how is X connected to Y?”)
  • The data is truly varied: many kinds of nodes and edges, each with properties
  • Analysts need to explore relationships freely
  • The dataset fits comfortably on a few machines
Stick to SQL/KV when…
  • Queries are 1–2 hops (“my followers”, “my permissions”). An indexed edge table is faster and simpler
  • You need extreme write throughput or petabyte scale
  • You'd be adding a second database (and a second thing to keep consistent) for one feature

Examples: Neo4j and Memgraph (property graph, Cypher), Amazon Neptune and JanusGraph (Gremlin/SPARQL), TigerGraph and Nebula (analytics at scale), plus pgRouting/recursive CTEs when you just need a little traversal in Postgres.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Traversals with no limit. Three hops in a social graph reach most of the network. Always limit the depth and the neighbours followed per node.
  • Running PageRank or community detection online. Those are batch jobs. Serve their results from a store; don't compute them per request.
  • Forgetting the reverse edge. “Who follows me” needs its own index, written in the same transaction or later in the background. If it's written later, it will briefly be out of sync.

Interview drill

Design: Design “mutual friends” shown on every profile view, for 500M users.

How a strong answer sounds
It's a 2-hop intersection: the friends two people have in common. For large accounts, that's far too expensive to compute naively on every profile view. Keep each user's friend set in a cache (a sorted set or bitmap). For two normal users, loop over the smaller set and check each friend against the larger one. That takes microseconds. For accounts with huge friend lists, limit the work: sample, or show “50+ mutual friends” instead of an exact count. Don't precompute anything per pair of users; there are far too many pairs.

Scale: A celebrity with 100M followers posts. What breaks in a graph store, and what do you do?

How a strong answer sounds
You can't read that edge list, or send the post to every follower, while a request waits. And the follower list is too big to cache as one value. Treat nodes with huge edge counts as a special case. Store their counts separately (denormalized counters) and read the edge list in pages. Switch that account from fan-out-on-write (push the post to every follower's feed) to fan-out-on-read: followers pull the celebrity's posts when they load their feed, and merge them in (Chapter 27).

🧠 Key takeaways

  • In SQL, each extra hop multiplies the work. Social graphs are small worlds, so a traversal with no limit reaches everyone.
  • Store edges in an indexed edge table or in adjacency lists, sharded by node, with a reverse index kept up to date separately.
  • Graph databases use index-free adjacency (direct pointers). It pays off when following connections to any depth is the query.
  • Searching from both ends turns a depth-4 problem into two depth-2 problems.
  • Serve social graphs with heavy caching over a plain sharded database, plus stored counters.
  • The real scaling problems are celebrity nodes, edges that cross shards, and privacy checks per viewer.
  • Run PageRank, communities and embeddings offline. Keep live queries to 1–2 hops plus ranking.

Check your understanding

Why does a 3-hop friends-of-friends query bring down a relational database?

The rows in between grow 200 → 40,000 → 8,000,000, and most of them are duplicate paths to the same people. Indexes and MVCC aren't the problem: even with perfect indexes, each extra hop multiplies the rows by the average friend count.

You need “who follows me?” as well as “who do I follow?”. What does that imply?

Edges point one way. Looking them up the other way needs its own index or row, and that's extra write work to keep correct.

Finding a connection path of length 4 between two users: what's the efficient approach?

With d friends each, the work drops from roughly d⁴ to 2·d². Precomputing paths for every pair of users is impossible at social-network sizes.

How should “people you may know” be produced for 500M users?

Finding candidates is expensive, and slightly old results are fine. That's exactly the kind of work to precompute in a batch job.

Why is sharding a social graph across machines fundamentally awkward?

That's why large systems shard by node, keep traversals shallow, and rely on caching instead of traversing across many machines.
