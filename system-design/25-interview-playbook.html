What's actually being evaluated

There is rarely one “right” answer. Interviewers watch how you think: whether you drive toward a working design, justify decisions with requirements and numbers, and recognise weaknesses before being told.

    Step 2 — Estimation cheat sheet

    ⏱️ Time

    1 day = 86,400 s ≈ 10⁵ s
    1 month ≈ 2.5 × 10⁶ s
    1 year ≈ 3 × 10⁷ s
    1M req/day ≈ 12 req/s
    1B req/day ≈ 12K req/s

    💾 Sizes

    char ≈ 1 byte · int64 = 8 B
    UUID = 16 B · timestamp = 8 B
    tweet-ish row ≈ 300 B–1 KB
    photo ≈ 200 KB–2 MB
    1 min HD video ≈ 50–100 MB

    🖥️ Capacity rules of thumb

    app server ≈ 1–10K req/s
    Postgres ≈ ~10K simple QPS
    Redis ≈ ~100K ops/s
    Kafka broker ≈ ~1M msgs/s
    1 Gbps ≈ 125 MB/s

    Rules of thumb vary enormously with hardware and workload — say “roughly” and state your assumption. Latency numbers are in Chapter 4.

    🎯 Estimates exist to drive decisions, not to be precise. After computing, say what it means: “12K peak writes/s is beyond one Postgres primary → we need sharding or a write-optimised store”, or “50 TB/year fits comfortably — storage isn't our bottleneck”.

    Step 3 — API & data model

    🔌 API

    • List only the endpoints behind your core features: POST /v1/tweets, GET /v1/feed?cursor=
    • Name the protocol: REST for clients, gRPC internally, WebSocket/SSE for push
    • Mention auth (who's calling), pagination (cursors), idempotency keys for writes

    🗄️ Data model

    • Entities, key fields, and access patterns (“fetch a user's latest 20 posts”)
    • Access patterns choose the database and the partition key — not the other way round
    • Call out the hot entity and its partition key early: it's where the deep dive will go

    🗺️
    Walk a request through the diagram

    Once the boxes are drawn, narrate the two or three most important flows end to end: “User posts a photo: client gets a presigned URL from the Upload service, uploads to S3, the ObjectCreated event lands on Kafka, the thumbnail worker…”. Walking flows exposes missing components faster than staring at boxes.

    Step 5 — Deep dives & bottlenecks

    Pick the 1–2 hardest problems for this specific system (the interviewer may steer you) and go deep. A generic checklist for finding them:

    🔥

    Hot spots

    Celebrity users, viral posts, hot partitions, one huge tenant. How do you detect and spread them?

    💥

    Single points of failure

    Walk each box: what happens when it dies? Replicas, failover, degraded modes?

    🔄

    Consistency

    Where can data disagree? Replication lag, cache staleness, dual writes, retries. Which ones matter to users?

    📈

    10× growth

    Which component breaks first at 10× traffic or data? What's the next architecture step?

    🔭

    Operations

    Key metrics and alerts, rollout of schema changes, backfills, cost drivers.

    🛡️

    Security & abuse

    AuthZ on every object, rate limits, spam, privacy and data retention.

    Speaking in trade-offs

    Senior engineers rarely say “X is better”. They say “X gives us A at the cost of B, and given requirement R, that's the right call”. Flip the cards to rehearse the language.

    ⚖️

    Consistency vs availability

    tap to flip
    “For the like counter, stale-by-a-few-seconds is fine, so we choose an AP store and async aggregation. For the account balance, we need linearizable writes, so we accept unavailability during a partition.”
    📤

    Push vs pull

    fan-out on write vs read
    “Precomputing feeds makes reads O(1) but costs write amplification — fine for normal users, catastrophic for celebrities, so we go hybrid.”
    🧮

    Normalize vs denormalize

    integrity vs read speed
    “Denormalizing the author name into each post saves a join on the hottest query; the cost is updating many rows on a rename, which is rare — so we do it async.”
    ⏱️

    Latency vs throughput

    batching
    “Batching writes to Kafka every 5 ms raises throughput 10× and adds at most 5 ms latency — acceptable for analytics events, not for the trading path.”
    🔗

    Sync vs async

    coupling vs simplicity
    “Charging the card stays synchronous because the user must see the result; sending the receipt email goes through a queue so an email-provider outage can't break checkout.”
    🧱

    Build vs buy

    control vs time
    “A managed Kafka service costs more per GB, but we have two engineers and no on-call for brokers. We revisit at 10× volume.”

    Common mistakes

    🏃
    Jumping straight to boxes

    Drawing Kafka and Cassandra in minute two, before knowing the scale or features. Requirements first, always.

    🏗️
    Over-engineering

    Microservices, multi-region active-active and a service mesh for 10K users. Complexity must be earned by requirements.

    🏷️
    Buzzword bingo

    “We'll use Kafka” with no explanation of why, what the topics and partition keys are, or what consumers do.

    🤐
    Silent thinking

    Going quiet for three minutes. Think out loud — interviewers can only score reasoning they can hear.

    🙈
    Ignoring failure

    A perfect happy path with no word about what happens when the cache, a shard or a region dies.

    🧊
    Defending a bad decision

    When the interviewer pokes a hole, they're usually helping. Say “good point — that breaks when… let's change it to…”. Adapting is a top signal.

    Interview drill

    Practise: You've drawn the high-level design and the interviewer says nothing. What do you do?

    How a strong answer sounds
    Drive. Say what you'd deep-dive into and why: “The riskiest part here is the fan-out on write for large accounts — can I spend a few minutes there?” Then walk a request through the diagram, name the bottleneck you expect at 10×, and offer a choice: “I can go deeper on the data model, or on the failure behaviour — which is more useful?” Silence is an invitation to lead, not a trap.

    Practise: Halfway through, the interviewer says “what if we need strong consistency for that counter?”

    How a strong answer sounds
    Treat it as a requirement change, not an attack. Say what breaks (the cached, eventually-consistent counter no longer works), give the options (single-writer shard for that key, conditional updates, a consensus-backed store, or a per-user sharded counter with exact aggregation on read), pick one with a reason tied to the new requirement, and name the cost you just accepted — usually throughput or availability during partitions.

    🧠 Key takeaways

    • Interviewers score structured thinking, justified trade-offs, depth where it matters and communication — not one correct diagram.
    • Timebox: requirements, estimates, API/data, high-level design, deep dives, wrap-up.
    • Ask about users, features, scale, latency, consistency, availability and constraints before designing.
    • Estimate to drive decisions, and state what each number implies.
    • Let access patterns pick your databases and partition keys.
    • Walk requests through the diagram; deep-dive hot spots, failures and consistency.
    • Speak in trade-offs tied to requirements, adapt when challenged, and practise out loud.

    Check your understanding

    It's minute 3. The prompt is “Design Instagram”. What should you be doing?

    Scope and non-functional requirements determine everything else — and prevent designing features nobody asked for.

    200M DAU each make 2 writes per day. Roughly what's the average write QPS?

    400M writes/day ÷ ~10⁵ seconds ≈ 4,000–4,600/s average; multiply by a peak factor for capacity planning.

    The interviewer asks, “what happens if your Redis cache cluster goes down?” Best response?

    Failure questions test whether you understand second-order effects and have concrete mitigations.

    Which statement best shows trade-off reasoning?

    It names both sides of the trade-off and ties the choice to a specific property of this system.

    How should you choose a database for a new system in an interview?

    Data model, query shapes, transactions, write volume and latency targets drive the choice — then name alternatives and why they lose.
