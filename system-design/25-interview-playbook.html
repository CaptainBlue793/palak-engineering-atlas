The System Design Playbook

You now know the building blocks. This chapter shows you how to use them the same way every time — a repeatable performance. You'll learn to run a 45-minute design discussion, make quick estimates with confidence, talk about trade-offs like a senior engineer, avoid the classic mistakes, and practise with purpose. It works just as well for real design reviews at work as for interviews.

What's actually being evaluated

There is rarely one “right” answer. Interviewers watch how you think. Do you steer toward a working design? Do you back up each decision with the requirements and some numbers? Do you spot weaknesses before someone points them out?

Why does it work this way? A real design job has no answer key either. The company wants to know how you will behave in a design review next month: will you ask good questions, make a sensible call with incomplete information, and notice the risks? So the interviewer grades the process, using a rubric much like the table below. Read across a row to see what “one level higher” looks like.

A concrete example of the difference, on the same question — “how do you store the posts?”:

  • Mid-level: “In a database, maybe Postgres.” It works, but nothing ties it to this system.
  • Senior: “We write about 5K posts/s at peak and read them by author and time, so a table keyed by (author_id, created_at) fits. One Postgres primary handles roughly 10K simple writes/s, so we're fine for now, and we can shard by author_id later.”
  • Staff+: the senior answer, plus “sharding by author means a celebrity's posts all land on one shard; reads of that shard are cached, so it's the write side we'd watch — and here is the metric that tells us when to act.”

Pacing: the 45-minute plan

The most common failure isn't a wrong answer. It's running out of time before you reach the interesting parts. Use this timer when you practise. It tells you what you should be doing and saying at each point.

Look at where the minutes go in the 45-minute plan: about 5 for requirements, 4 for estimates, 5 for API and data model, 12 for the high-level design, 15 for deep dives and 4 to wrap up. The deep dive gets the biggest slice because that is where senior-level signal comes from. If you are still drawing boxes at minute 30, you have spent your best minutes on the part everyone gets right. A simple habit helps: glance at the clock at each phase change and say out loud “let me move on to…”.

    Step 1 — Requirements: the question checklist

    Spend the first few minutes narrowing the problem. Every answer you get rules out whole families of designs. Go through this list for any problem and try to cover every category.

    Here is how much one answer can change. Take “design a chat app”. If the answer to “group chats?” is “only one-to-one”, you never need to copy a message to 500 group members. If the answer to “must messages survive if the phone was offline for a week?” is “yes”, you need durable storage per user, not just a live connection. Two questions, and you've already decided two major parts of the design. Asking them costs thirty seconds; guessing wrong costs the interview.

    Summarise on the board: the 3–5 core features in scope, what is explicitly out of scope, and non-functional targets with numbers — how fast, how big, how reliable. Later you'll point back at these to justify every decision.

    Step 2 — Estimation cheat sheet

    You estimate for one reason: to find out which parts of the design are hard. A system with 100 writes per second and one with 100,000 need completely different databases, and you can't tell which one you're building until you do the arithmetic. The numbers below are the ones you'll reach for most. Learn the time conversions by heart; the rest you can round freely.

    Where does “1M requests/day ≈ 12 req/s” come from? A day has 86,400 seconds. 1,000,000 ÷ 86,400 ≈ 11.6, which rounds to 12. Most people just use 10⁵ seconds per day to make the division easy (1M ÷ 10⁵ = 10 req/s) and accept being about 15% low. That error doesn't matter, because the next step — multiplying by a peak factor of 2–10× — is far less certain.

    ⏱️ Time

    1 day = 86,400 s ≈ 10⁵ s
    1 month ≈ 2.5 × 10⁶ s
    1 year ≈ 3 × 10⁷ s
    1M req/day ≈ 12 req/s
    1B req/day ≈ 12K req/s

    💾 Sizes

    char ≈ 1 byte · int64 = 8 B
    UUID = 16 B · timestamp = 8 B
    tweet-ish row ≈ 300 B–1 KB
    photo ≈ 200 KB–2 MB
    1 min HD video ≈ 50–100 MB

    🖥️ Capacity rules of thumb

    app server ≈ 1–10K req/s
    Postgres ≈ ~10K simple QPS
    Redis ≈ ~100K ops/s
    Kafka broker ≈ ~1M msgs/s
    1 Gbps ≈ 125 MB/s

    These rules of thumb vary hugely with hardware and workload. Say “roughly” and state your assumption out loud. Latency numbers are in Chapter 4.

    A worked example, the way you'd say it out loud. “Say 100M daily users, and each posts 0.2 times a day. That's 20M posts/day ÷ 10⁵ s ≈ 200 posts/s on average; at 3× peak, 600/s. Each post is about 1 KB, so 20M × 1 KB = 20 GB/day, or about 7 TB/year; with 3 copies for safety, about 22 TB/year. Reads: if each user opens the feed 10 times a day, that's 1B reads/day ≈ 10K reads/s. So writes are small, storage is modest, and reads are the challenge — I'll design around caching the feed.” Notice the last sentence: every estimate ends with a conclusion.

    🎯 Estimates are there to drive decisions, not to be precise. After each one, say what it means for the design: “12K peak writes/s is more than one main Postgres database can take → we need to split the data across machines (sharding) or use a store built for heavy writes”.     Step 3 — API & data model

    This step turns the features you agreed on into something precise. The API says exactly what the outside world can ask for. The data model says what you must store to answer those requests. Doing it before drawing boxes stops you from building components nobody calls. Keep it short — 3 to 5 endpoints and 2 to 4 tables is normal.

    🔌 API

    • List only the endpoints behind your core features: POST /v1/tweets, GET /v1/feed?cursor=
    • Name the protocol: REST for clients, gRPC between internal services, WebSocket/SSE when the server pushes updates
    • Mention auth (who's calling), pagination (returning results page by page with cursors), and idempotency keys for writes (so a retried request isn't applied twice)

    🗄️ Data model

    • Entities, key fields, and access patterns — the ways the data will be read and written (“fetch a user's latest 20 posts”)
    • Access patterns choose the database and the partition key (the field that decides which machine stores each row) — not the other way round
    • Point out the busiest entity and its partition key early: that's where the deep dive will go

    A small example for a photo-sharing app. Access pattern: “show a user's profile grid, newest first, 30 at a time”. That points to a posts table partitioned by user_id and sorted by created_at, so one user's posts sit together and come back in order with one read. If you had picked post_id as the partition key instead, the same request would have to ask every shard, because one user's posts would be scattered everywhere. That is what “access patterns choose the partition key” means in practice.

    🗺️
    Walk a request through the diagram

    Once the boxes are drawn, talk through the two or three most important flows from start to finish. For example: “User posts a photo: the client gets a presigned URL (a temporary upload link) from the Upload service, uploads to S3, the ObjectCreated event lands on Kafka, the thumbnail worker…”. Walking through flows reveals missing pieces faster than staring at boxes.

    Step 5 — Deep dives & bottlenecks

    Pick the 1–2 hardest problems for this particular system (the interviewer may steer you) and go deep. Here's a general checklist for finding them:

    🔥

    Hot spots

    Celebrity users, viral posts, one partition getting most of the traffic, one huge customer. How do you spot them and spread the load?

    💥

    Single points of failure

    Go through each box: what happens when it dies? Are there copies (replicas), automatic switch-over (failover), or a reduced mode that keeps working?

    🔄

    Consistency

    Where can two copies of the data disagree? Replicas that lag behind, stale caches, writing to two systems at once, retries. Which of these do users actually notice?

    📈

    10× growth

    Which part breaks first at 10× the traffic or data? What would you change next?

    🔭

    Operations

    Key metrics and alerts, rolling out database schema changes, backfills (filling in data for old records), what drives the cost.

    🛡️

    Security & abuse

    Checking permissions (authorization) on every object, rate limits, spam, privacy, and how long data is kept.

    How do you choose which 1–2 to go deep on? Look back at your estimates and requirements, and pick the part where the numbers are biggest or the rules are strictest. A worked example: in a feed system your estimate said 10K reads/s but only 200 writes/s, and one account has 50M followers. The hard part is plainly “what happens when that account posts” (a hot spot), not “how do we store 1 KB posts”. Say that reasoning out loud — choosing the right deep dive is itself a senior signal.

    A good deep dive has a shape: state the problem with a number (“one post would mean 50M feed writes”), list two or three options, pick one and say why, then name what can still go wrong with your choice and how you'd notice it (a metric or alert).

    Speaking in trade-offs

    Senior engineers rarely say “X is better”. They say “X gives us A at the cost of B, and given requirement R, that's the right call”. Flip the cards to practise this way of talking.

    Why this template? Every real choice gives something up. Saying only the benefit sounds like you haven't seen the cost; saying only “it depends” sounds like you can't decide. The template fixes both: it names the benefit, names the cost, and then decides using a fact from this system. When the fact changes, the decision can change too — which is exactly what the interviewer will test by changing a requirement halfway through.

    ⚖️

    Consistency vs availability

    tap to flip
    “For the like counter, being a few seconds out of date is fine, so we choose a store that stays available during network splits (an AP store) and add up counts in the background. For the account balance, every read must see the latest write (linearizable), so we accept being unavailable during a network split.”
    📤

    Push vs pull

    fan-out on write vs read
    “Building feeds ahead of time makes each read a single quick lookup, but every post has to be written into every follower's feed. That's fine for normal users and a disaster for celebrities with millions of followers, so we mix both approaches.”
    🧮

    Normalize vs denormalize

    integrity vs read speed
    “Copying the author name into each post saves a join on our busiest query. The cost is updating many rows when someone renames, which is rare — so we do that in the background.”
    ⏱️

    Latency vs throughput

    batching
    “Batching writes to Kafka every 5 ms raises throughput 10× and adds at most 5 ms latency — acceptable for analytics events, not for the trading path.”
    🔗

    Sync vs async

    coupling vs simplicity
    “Charging the card stays synchronous (the user waits for it) because they must see the result. Sending the receipt email goes through a queue, so an outage at the email provider can't break checkout.”
    🧱

    Build vs buy

    control vs time
    “A managed Kafka service costs more per GB, but we have two engineers and nobody to be on call for Kafka servers. We'll look again at 10× volume.”

    Common mistakes

    🏃
    Jumping straight to boxes

    Drawing Kafka and Cassandra in minute two, before you know the scale or the features. Requirements first, always.

    🏗️
    Over-engineering

    Microservices, several regions all serving traffic at once, and a service mesh — for 10K users. Only add complexity when a requirement demands it.

    🏷️
    Buzzword bingo

    “We'll use Kafka” — with no word on why, what the topics and partition keys are, or what the consumers do.

    🤐
    Silent thinking

    Going quiet for three minutes. Think out loud — interviewers can only score the reasoning they can hear.

    🙈
    Ignoring failure

    A perfect “everything works” path, with no word about what happens when the cache, a shard or a whole region dies.

    🧊
    Defending a bad decision

    When the interviewer points out a flaw, they're usually helping. Say “good point — that breaks when… let's change it to…”. Adapting well is one of the strongest signals you can give.

    Practice problem bank

    Practise out loud, on a timer, drawing by hand. Filter by concept to work on your weak spots. Problems marked 📘 are fully worked through in this course.

    A practice routine that works:

    1. Attempt first, read later. Pick a problem, start the timer and go through all six phases on your own before you open any worked solution.
    2. Compare against the case study. For 📘 problems, read the matching chapter and write down what you missed — usually a requirement you never asked about or a failure you never considered.
    3. Redo it a few days later without notes. Remembering the design is not the goal; being able to re-derive it from the requirements is.
    4. Practise with someone else when you can. Being interrupted and challenged is a different skill from designing alone.

    Interview drill

    Practise: You've drawn the high-level design and the interviewer says nothing. What do you do?

    How a strong answer sounds
    Take the lead. Say what you'd dig into and why: “The riskiest part here is copying each post into every follower's feed for large accounts — can I spend a few minutes there?” Then walk a request through the diagram, name the bottleneck you expect at 10×, and offer a choice: “I can go deeper on the data model, or on what happens when things fail — which is more useful?” Silence is an invitation to lead, not a trap.

    Practise: Halfway through, the interviewer says “what if we need strong consistency for that counter?”

    How a strong answer sounds
    Treat it as a change in requirements, not an attack. Say what breaks: the cached counter that was allowed to lag no longer works. Give the options: send all writes for that key to one shard, use conditional updates (only write if the value hasn't changed), use a store backed by a consensus protocol, or split the counter into pieces and add them up exactly when reading. Pick one, with a reason tied to the new requirement. Then name the cost you just accepted — usually throughput, or availability during network splits.

    🧠 Key takeaways

    • Interviewers score organised thinking, trade-offs you can justify, depth where it matters and communication — not one correct diagram.
    • Give each phase a fixed slice of time: requirements, estimates, API/data, high-level design, deep dives, wrap-up.
    • Before designing, ask about users, features, scale, latency, consistency, availability and constraints.
    • Estimate to drive decisions, and say what each number means for the design.
    • Let the ways data is read and written (access patterns) pick your databases and partition keys.
    • Walk requests through the diagram; dig into hot spots, failures and consistency.
    • Talk in trade-offs tied to requirements, adapt when challenged, and practise out loud.

    Check your understanding

    It's minute 3. The prompt is “Design Instagram”. What should you be doing?

    Scope and non-functional requirements (scale, speed, reliability) shape everything else — and stop you designing features nobody asked for.

    200M DAU each make 2 writes per day. Roughly what's the average write QPS?

    400M writes/day ÷ ~10⁵ seconds ≈ 4,000–4,600/s on average. For capacity planning, multiply by a peak factor (busy hours can be several times the average).

    The interviewer asks, “what happens if your Redis cache cluster goes down?” Best response?

    Failure questions test whether you understand knock-on effects (the cache dies, so the database gets flooded) and have concrete fixes.

    Which statement best shows trade-off reasoning?

    It names both sides of the trade-off and links the choice to a specific fact about this system.

    How should you choose a database for a new system in an interview?

    The data model, the kinds of queries, the need for transactions, write volume and latency targets decide it. Then name the alternatives and why they lose.
