Design an Ad Click Aggregator

Advertisers spend real money based on these numbers, so this system has an unusual combination of demands: ingest a million events per second, show counts that are fresh within a minute, and produce billing numbers that are exactly right even after machines crash, events arrive late, and bots try to inflate the totals. It's the practical showcase for everything in the streaming chapter.

  • Ingest ad clicks at a million events per second
  • Aggregate with windows, watermarks and late data
  • Avoid hot partitions caused by viral ads
  • Get exactly-once counts despite crashes and retries
  • Serve dashboard queries from rollups in milliseconds
  • Reconcile streaming results with a batch recompute for billing
STEP 1 / 6

Requirements

🛠️ Functional

✓Record every ad impression and click with attributes (ad, campaign, country, device)
✓Advertiser dashboard: clicks over time, filtered and grouped, for any range up to a year
✓Billing-grade totals per campaign per day
✓Filter invalid/bot traffic; support reprocessing after logic changes

📏 Non-functional

✓Scale: 10B events/day, peak ~1M/s
✓Freshness: dashboards within ~1 minute
✓Exactly-once for billing; approximate is fine for live dashboards
✓Queries p99 < 1 s over months of data
✓The click redirect itself must be instant — users must never wait for analytics

STEP 3 / 6

High-level design

Click → count → dashboard → invoiceStep-through
  1. The ad links to /click?ad=…&sig=…. The redirect service validates a signature (so clicks can't be forged), assigns a click_id, writes the event to a local buffer and returns a 302 to the advertiser's page within milliseconds. Nothing downstream is on the user's critical path.
  2. Events are batched into Kafka, partitioned to spread load and preserve per-key ordering. Kafka absorbs the 1M/s spikes and lets several independent consumers read the same stream (Chapter 14).
  3. Flink keys by (ad_id, country, device), drops duplicates by click_id, filters known bots, and counts into 1-minute tumbling windows with a watermark allowing ~2 minutes of lateness (Chapter 18).
  4. Completed windows are upserted into an OLAP store keyed by (minute, ad_id, dims). Upserts make the write idempotent: reprocessing the same window overwrites rather than double-counts.
  5. “Clicks per hour for campaign 42 in Germany, last 30 days” reads a few thousand pre-aggregated rows instead of scanning billions of events.
  6. A second consumer archives raw events to the data lake as Parquet, partitioned by hour (Chapter 9). This is the source of truth for audits, backfills and any change to counting logic.
  7. Overnight, a batch job recomputes yesterday from raw data with the full fraud model, and reconciles against the streaming numbers. Small drift is expected and corrected; large drift pages someone. Advertisers are billed from the batch result.
λ
This is a lambda architecture — on purpose

Streaming gives freshness (dashboards in seconds) and batch gives correctness (complete, late-inclusive, re-runnable). Money justifies running both. If you only needed dashboards, a single streaming path (kappa) would be simpler — and if you only needed invoices, a nightly batch job alone would do.

STEP 4 / 6

Deep dive: hot partitions

Partitioning by ad_id keeps each ad's events in order on one partition — until a Super Bowl ad becomes 40% of all traffic. That partition's consumer can't keep up, its lag grows, and dashboards for every ad on that partition fall behind.

🧪 Go viral with key = ad_id: one partition's lag explodes while seven consumers idle. Switch to a salted key: the viral ad spreads over all partitions, each producing a partial count that the aggregator sums by ad_id. The cost is that per-ad ordering is lost — fine for counting, not for event sequences that must stay ordered.

Deep dive: counting exactly once

Three independent things can inflate or lose counts: the client retrying, the consumer reprocessing after a crash, and bots. Each needs its own defence.

STEP 6 / 6

Wrap-up

🤖

Invalid traffic

Real-time heuristics (rate per IP/device, impossible timings, known bot ASNs) plus offline ML review. Billing waits for the hold-back window so refunds for invalid clicks are computed before invoicing.

🔁

Reprocessing

Because raw events live in the lake and rollups are idempotent upserts, you can replay a month through fixed logic and overwrite the affected windows — no manual patching of counters.

⏱️

Late events

Mobile SDKs upload offline clicks hours later. Allow lateness for a bounded window, route the rest to a side output, and let the nightly batch job fold them into the final numbers.

🌍

Multi-region

Aggregate locally in each region and merge partial counts centrally — counting is associative, so partial sums merge cleanly (and HLL sketches merge too).

💾

Retention & cost

Raw events for 30–90 days in hot storage then archive; minute rollups for 3 months; hour for a year; day forever. Storage cost is dominated by raw data, query cost by granularity.

🔭

Monitoring the pipeline

Consumer lag per partition, watermark delay, dropped-late-event count, streaming-vs-batch drift, and rollup freshness — these are the SLIs that tell you the numbers can be trusted.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Doing analytics work in the redirect. The user's click must be a fast 302; everything else is asynchronous.
  • Incrementing counters on reprocessing. Upsert the window total instead, so replays are idempotent.
  • Deleting raw events. Without them you can't backfill, re-run corrected logic, or defend an invoice.
🎤
Interview drill

Design: Advertisers dispute a bill. What lets you answer them?

How a strong answer sounds
Raw events in the lake, partitioned by hour, with a click ID per event and the signature that proved the click was legitimate. The nightly batch job recomputes the invoice from that raw data with the current fraud model, and its output — not the streaming dashboard — is what's billed. You can show the drift between streaming and batch, the number of events filtered as invalid, and replay any window to demonstrate the number.

Scale: One advertiser's campaign is 60% of all traffic. What do you change?

How a strong answer sounds
Salt the partition key (ad_id:0–N) so that campaign's events spread across partitions, and sum the partial counts by ad_id downstream — counting is associative, so partial aggregates merge cleanly. Ordering within the ad is lost, which is fine for counting. Also check the serving store: the same hot key can become a hot row there, so pre-aggregate before writing and consider a separate partition for the whale.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Keep the user-facing click redirect instant; everything analytical happens asynchronously behind Kafka.
  • Aggregate in event-time windows with watermarks; expect and handle late events.
  • Hot keys create hot partitions — salt the key and merge partial counts.
  • Exactly-once counting needs dedupe by event ID plus idempotent upserts of window totals.
  • Bot filtering is a separate problem from exactly-once processing.
  • Serve dashboards from a hierarchy of rollups in a columnar OLAP store; use HLL sketches for uniques.
  • Archive raw events so any logic change can be replayed; bill from a nightly batch recompute reconciled against the stream.

Check your understanding

A single ad generates 60% of all clicks. Events are keyed by ad_id. What happens and what's the fix?

All events with the same key land on one partition. Salting spreads them; counting is associative so partials merge.

A consumer crashes and reprocesses the last minute of events. Why does writing the window total (upsert) beat incrementing a counter?

Idempotent writes make reprocessing safe — the cornerstone of exactly-once effects.

Why keep raw events in a data lake when rollups answer every dashboard query?

Aggregates are lossy. Raw data is the only way to answer a question you didn't anticipate — or to prove the invoice.

A mobile SDK uploads clicks that happened 3 hours ago, past the 2-minute allowed lateness. What should happen?

Counting them "now" corrupts the timeline, and a 3-hour watermark would make every dashboard 3 hours stale. Batch fixes the record.

Why can't you sum “unique users per hour” from 60 per-minute unique-user counts?

Distinct counts aren't additive. Storing HyperLogLog sketches per minute lets you merge them into any larger window.
