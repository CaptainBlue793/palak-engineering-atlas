Tail Latency & the OS

A service answers in 10 ms on average, and its users complain that it is slow. Both are true. The average hides the one request in a hundred that took a second, and at scale that one in a hundred is not rare: a page that needs a hundred back-end calls will usually include one of them. The slow few are the tail of the latency distribution, and most of what puts requests there is not the application's code. It is the machinery of the previous thirty-seven chapters, each piece of which occasionally makes a thread wait: for a core, for a page, for a quota to refill, for a lock. This chapter is about seeing that tail, finding which mechanism is behind it, and designing so that it matters less.

Percentiles, not averages

Sort a million response times. The value halfway along is the median, or p50. The value 99% of the way along is p99: one request in a hundred is slower than this. p99.9 is one in a thousand. Latency is almost never spread evenly round its mean. It has a tight cluster of fast responses and a long, thin tail of slow ones.

1,000,000 requests to one service

mean        12 ms       dragged up by the tail; almost nobody experiences "12 ms"
p50          9 ms       the typical request
p99        180 ms       10,000 requests were slower than this
p99.9    1,100 ms       1,000 requests took over a second

Report and alert on percentiles, never the mean. And never average percentiles from different machines or minutes: the p99 of the whole is not the average of the p99s. Keep histograms and combine those.

Fan-out multiplies the tail

One user request rarely touches one server. A search fans out to a hundred index shards; a product page calls dozens of services. The request is as slow as its slowest call. If each call independently has probability p of being slow, then with N calls:

P(user request is slow) = 1 − (1 − p)^N

p = 1% slow calls        N = 1      1.0%
                         N = 10     9.6%
                         N = 100   63.4%       most user requests hit at least one slow call
                         N = 1000  99.996%

So a back end whose p99 is poor gives its users a poor median. To have 99% of user requests fast with a fan-out of 100, each back-end call must be fast 99.99% of the time. This is why large systems care about p99.9 and beyond, which sounds like perfectionism until you do the arithmetic.

Feel the tail

Each back-end call normally takes about 10 ms; occasionally one takes about a second. Move the fan-out and watch the share of slow user requests. Then switch on hedging.

A model: fast calls take 8 to 12 ms and slow ones 800 to 1,200 ms, independently of each other. A user request finishes when its last call does. With hedging, a call that hasn't answered after 20 ms is sent again to another replica, and the first answer wins. The same 2,000 random requests are used for every setting, so changes are due to the controls.

Things to notice. With one slow call in a hundred, a single call is slow 1% of the time, ten calls about 10%, and a hundred calls about 63%: the median user request is now a slow one, although 99% of back-end calls are fast. Hedging changes the picture completely. A call is slow only if both copies are, which is one in ten thousand, so even at a fan-out of 100 only about 1% of user requests are slow, and the cost is roughly 1% more back-end calls. It doesn't fix whatever makes calls slow. It stops one slow machine from deciding the answer.

CPU throttling: a cliff, not a slope

The second row deserves a closer look, because it is the commonest self-inflicted cause in containers and it is easy to misread. A CPU limit is enforced in fixed periods, 100 ms by default. A limit of "2 CPUs" means 200 ms of CPU time per 100 ms of wall time, summed over all the container's threads.

Limit: 2 CPUs = 200 ms of CPU time per 100 ms period.   The service has 8 busy threads.

$ cat /sys/fs/cgroup/app/cpu.stat

nr_throttled 61322             # throttled in 30% of all periods
throttled_usec 2811440290     # 47 minutes spent frozen, in total

Average CPU use for that container may be well under its limit, and dashboards will say it has headroom, while a third of its periods end in a freeze. The cure is to match the number of busy threads to the quota (Chapter 27), to raise or remove the limit and rely on shares instead, and to watch nr_throttled, not average CPU.

"The Tail at Scale"

In 2013 Jeffrey Dean and Luiz André Barroso of Google published a short paper with that title, and it changed how large services are designed. Its starting point was that slow responses in a big system can't be eliminated at the source, because the sources are too many and too ordinary: shared machines, background daemons, garbage collection, queueing, maintenance work, power management. Their own example is the fan-out arithmetic above: if one server in a hundred answers slowly, a request that touches a hundred servers is slow 63% of the time. So instead of only chasing causes, they proposed making the system tail-tolerant, the way systems are made fault-tolerant, by building a predictable whole out of unpredictable parts. Their best-known result came from a test on Bigtable, reading the values for 1,000 keys spread over 100 servers. Sending a hedged request to a second replica after a 10 ms delay cut the 99.9th-percentile latency from 1,800 ms to 74 ms, while sending just 2% more requests. The same paper introduced tied requests, where each copy tells the other server to cancel when it starts, and the idea of returning "good enough" results when a few shards are late. It is why a search page will sometimes show results from slightly fewer servers than exist, and nobody notices.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Monitoring the average. A mean of 12 ms can hide a p99 of a second. Track p50, p99 and p99.9.
  • Averaging percentiles. The mean of each server's p99 is not the fleet's p99. Merge histograms.
  • Treating the tail as noise. With fan-out, the back end's tail is the user's typical case.
  • Reading average CPU as "not throttled". A container can be throttled in a third of its periods at 40% average use. Read nr_throttled.
  • Hedging requests that aren't safe to repeat. A hedged "charge the card" charges twice. Hedge reads, or make writes idempotent.
  • Retrying without a limit. When a service is slow because it is overloaded, retries are more load. Cap them.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is tail latency and why does it matter more as systems grow?

How a strong answer sounds
Tail latency is the response time of the slowest requests: the 99th percentile and beyond, as opposed to the median or the mean. It matters because of fan-out. A single user action in a large system triggers many back-end calls, and the user waits for the slowest. If each call is slow with probability one per cent, a request needing 100 calls is slow with probability one minus 0.99 to the hundredth power, about 63%. So a back end's rare slow case becomes the common experience. Users also make many requests per session, so nearly everyone meets the tail sooner or later. That is why large services set targets at p99 or p99.9, why averages are close to useless as a health signal, and why they add techniques such as hedged requests that tolerate an occasional slow server instead of relying on every server being fast every time.

Deeper: A containerised service has a p99 of 95 ms and a p50 of 4 ms. CPU usage averages 35% of its limit. What do you suspect and how do you confirm it?

How a strong answer sounds
A p99 just under 100 ms with a tiny median is the signature of CPU throttling, because the default enforcement period for cgroup CPU limits is 100 ms. The limit is a quota of CPU time per period shared by all the container's threads. If the service has many more runnable threads than the quota covers, for example a runtime that sized its pools for the host's 32 cores under a 2-CPU limit, a burst of work spends the whole quota in the first part of the period and every thread is then frozen until the next one. Requests arriving during the freeze wait up to the remainder of the period, which gives a tail near 100 ms. Average usage looks fine because between bursts the service is idle. I'd confirm by reading cpu.stat in the container's cgroup: nr_throttled as a fraction of nr_periods, and throttled_usec rising. Fixes: size thread and GC pools to the quota, raise or remove the hard limit and rely on CPU shares, and alert on the throttled ratio. If nr_throttled were zero I'd move on to run-queue latency, major faults and lock waits with off-CPU tools.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Describe latency with percentiles. The mean describes almost no real request.
  • With fan-out N, a per-call slow probability p becomes 1 − (1 − p)N for the user: 1% turns into 63% at N = 100.
  • Most tail latency is waiting: for a core, a quota, a page, a disk or a lock. Each has a counter you can read.
  • cgroup CPU limits freeze a whole container when its quota runs out, producing tails close to the period length.
  • Reduce the tail with headroom, isolation, pre-faulted memory and correctly sized pools.
  • Tolerate what remains with hedged requests, timeouts and partial results.

Check your understanding

A service reports a p99 latency of 200 ms. What does that mean?

The 99th percentile is the value that 99% of requests are at or below. One in a hundred is slower.

Each back-end call is slow 1% of the time, independently. A user request makes 100 such calls and waits for all of them. Roughly how often is the user request slow?

1 − 0.99100 ≈ 0.634. The chance that all hundred calls are fast is only about 37%.

A container is limited to 1 CPU with the default 100 ms period. Four of its threads become busy at the start of a period, on four free cores. What happens?

The quota is CPU time summed over all threads. When it is gone, the whole group is throttled until the next period.

How does a hedged request reduce tail latency?

Waiting a little before duplicating keeps the extra load to a few per cent while removing most slow outliers, which are usually confined to one replica.

Which of these is not a typical operating-system cause of tail latency?

A constant cost shifts the whole distribution. The tail comes from things that happen only sometimes: queueing, faults, throttling, lock waits.
