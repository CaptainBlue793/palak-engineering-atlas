CPU Scheduling I: FCFS, SJF, Round Robin

Whenever a core becomes free, the kernel has to answer one question: of all the runnable tasks, which one runs next? The answer decides whether your shell feels instant while a compile runs in the background, whether a batch job finishes in an hour or three, and whether a request behind a slow one waits a millisecond or a second. This chapter defines what "good" means with three metrics, then runs the four classic policies against each other in a simulator so you can see each one's trade-off for yourself.

What "good scheduling" means

Treat each task as a job that arrives at some time and needs some amount of CPU, its burst. Three measures, all per job, then averaged:

  • Turnaround time = completion time − arrival time. How long the whole job took, from the user's point of view. Batch jobs care about this.
  • Waiting time = turnaround − burst. Time spent ready but not running. The part the scheduler controls.
  • Response time = first time it runs − arrival time. How long before anything happens. Interactive tasks (a shell, an editor, a request handler) care about this.

Policies trade these against each other and against throughput (jobs finished per second, hurt by switching overhead) and fairness (no job waits forever). No policy wins on every measure, which is why real schedulers combine ideas from all four below.

FCFS and the convoy effect

First-come, first-served runs jobs in arrival order, each to completion. It's simple, fair in a queue-at-the-bank sense, and has no switching overhead. Its weakness: one long job at the front makes every short job behind it wait. With jobs of 24, 3 and 3 ms arriving together, FCFS gives waiting times of 0, 24 and 27 ms, an average of 17 ms. Run the two short ones first and the average drops to 3 ms. Short jobs stuck behind a long one are called the convoy effect, and you'll see it anywhere work is processed strictly in order: a single-threaded event loop, a database connection running one slow query, a disk queue.

SJF and SRTF: shortest first

Shortest job first picks the ready job with the smallest burst. It provably minimises average waiting time for a given set of jobs: moving a short job ahead of a long one reduces the short job's wait by more than it increases the long one's. Its preemptive version, shortest remaining time first (SRTF), also preempts the running job when a new arrival needs less time than the running job has left. Two problems keep SJF out of general-purpose kernels:

  • You don't know burst lengths in advance. Schedulers can only predict from history, typically with an exponential average: predictednext = α × last burst + (1 − α) × predictedprevious.
  • Starvation. A steady stream of short jobs can postpone a long one forever.

The idea survives where burst sizes are known or estimable: batch schedulers that take a time limit with each job, and request routers that send small requests down a separate fast lane.

Round robin and the quantum

Round robin gives each ready job a fixed slice, the quantum, then preempts it and moves it to the back of the queue. No job waits more than (n − 1) quanta for its next turn, so response time is good and nothing starves. The quantum is the tuning knob. Too long and RR degenerates into FCFS. Too short and the machine spends its time switching (Chapter 6's panel), and every job's turnaround grows because they all finish near the end. A rule of thumb from the textbooks: most CPU bursts should finish within one quantum.

/* Round robin over jobs that all arrived at t = 0: the core of the policy. */
struct job { const char *name; int remaining, first_run, done_at; };

void round_robin(struct job *jobs, size_t n, int quantum) {
	int t = 0;
	size_t left = n;
	while (left > 0) {
		for (size_t i = 0; i < n; i++) {               /* the "queue" is a circular scan */
			struct job *j = &jobs[i];
			if (j->remaining == 0) continue;
			if (j->first_run < 0) j->first_run = t;       /* response time = first_run - arrival */
			int run = j->remaining < quantum ? j->remaining : quantum;
			t += run;                                     /* real schedulers add the switch cost here */
			j->remaining -= run;
			if (j->remaining == 0) { j->done_at = t; left--; }
		}
	}
}

Real general-purpose schedulers want RR's guarantees and SJF's efficiency without knowing burst lengths. They get there by watching behaviour: a task that keeps blocking after short bursts (an editor, a shell) is treated as short and favoured; one that uses its whole slice is treated as long. That's the multi-level feedback queue and Linux's fair scheduler, in Chapter 8.

The convoy effect in Redis: one KEYS command

Redis executes commands one at a time on a single thread, in the order they arrive: FCFS. Most commands take microseconds, so this works beautifully. Then someone runs KEYS * on a production instance with 20 million keys. That single command walks the whole keyspace for several seconds, and every other client's sub-millisecond GET waits behind it: the convoy effect, with timeouts and failovers to follow. Redis's documentation warns against KEYS in production for exactly this reason. The fix is round robin applied by hand: SCAN returns a small batch of keys per call with a cursor, so the long job is broken into short slices and other clients' commands run in between. ⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Confusing waiting time with response time. Waiting counts all time spent ready; response counts only until the first run.
  • Forgetting to subtract arrival time. Turnaround is completion minus arrival, not completion time.
  • Assuming a smaller quantum is always better. Below the switch-and-refill cost, overhead dominates and turnaround gets worse.
  • Calling SJF "optimal" without the qualifier. It minimises average waiting time; it can starve long jobs and needs knowledge you usually lack.
  • Getting the round-robin tie wrong in exams. State your convention: does a new arrival join before or after the job just preempted?

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Three jobs of 24, 3 and 3 ms arrive together. Compare FCFS and SJF.

How a strong answer sounds
FCFS runs them in order, so waiting times are 0, 24 and 27 ms, an average of 17 ms: the two short jobs are stuck behind the long one, the convoy effect. SJF runs the 3 ms jobs first: waits of 0, 3 and 6 ms, an average of 3 ms. SJF is optimal for average waiting time, but it needs to know burst lengths in advance and can starve long jobs if short ones keep arriving. Round robin with a 4 ms quantum would give 6, 4 and 7, about 5.7 ms on average, without needing to know anything, and bounds everyone's response time.

Deeper: How would you pick the round-robin quantum for a server?

How a strong answer sounds
Large enough that the switch and cache-refill cost is a small fraction of each slice, and large enough that most CPU bursts, like handling one request, finish within one quantum, so they don't get preempted mid-request. Small enough that with the expected number of runnable tasks per core, (n − 1) × quantum stays within the latency budget. In practice that lands in the low milliseconds; Linux's fair scheduler adapts the slice to the number of runnable tasks rather than fixing one value.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Turnaround = completion − arrival; waiting = turnaround − burst; response = first run − arrival.
  • FCFS is simple but suffers the convoy effect when job lengths vary.
  • SJF minimises average waiting (SRTF, its preemptive form, even more), but needs burst estimates and can starve long jobs.
  • Round robin bounds response time at (n − 1) quanta and never starves; the quantum trades responsiveness for overhead.
  • Real schedulers infer "short" from behaviour, combining RR's fairness with SJF's efficiency.

Check your understanding

A job arrives at t = 2 ms, first runs at t = 5 ms, needs 4 ms of CPU and finishes at t = 12 ms. What are its turnaround, waiting and response times?

Turnaround = 12 − 2 = 10; waiting = 10 − 4 = 6; response = 5 − 2 = 3.

What is the convoy effect?

Strict arrival order lets one long job hold up everything behind it, inflating average waiting time.

Why don't general-purpose kernels use pure SJF?

SJF is optimal on paper but needs information the kernel doesn't have, and it isn't fair to long jobs.

What happens to round robin as the quantum becomes very large?

If every burst fits in one quantum, nothing is ever preempted, which is FCFS. Very small quanta have the opposite problem: overhead.

Five CPU-bound jobs share a core under round robin with a 10 ms quantum. What's the longest a job waits between turns?

(n − 1) × q = 4 × 10 = 40 ms, ignoring switch costs. That bound is RR's main selling point.
