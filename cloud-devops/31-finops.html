FinOps: Cloud Cost Management

In a data centre, spending money needed a purchase order and a signature. In the cloud, every engineer spends money with every merge, and the invoice arrives a month later, addressed to someone else. FinOps is the practice of closing that loop: making cost visible to the people whose decisions create it, and treating it as an engineering measurement like latency or error rate. This chapter covers how to read a cloud bill, how to attribute it to teams, the order in which to cut it, how commitments trade flexibility for discount, and why moving data is the charge that surprises people most. The simulator lets you buy a commitment and then find out whether your forecast was right.

  • Read a cloud bill and find where the money actually goes
  • Attribute cost to teams with accounts, tags and usage-based splits
  • Rightsize from measured usage, and schedule what does not need to run
  • Compare on-demand, committed and spot pricing, and compute a commitment's break-even
  • Identify the data-transfer charges in an architecture
  • Use unit cost, not total cost, to judge whether spending is healthy

Reading the bill

A cloud bill is millions of lines of the same shape: a quantity of some usage type (instance-hours, gigabyte-months, requests, gigabytes transferred) multiplied by a rate. The provider's cost explorer groups those lines, and the first grouping to look at is by service.

$ aws ce get-cost-and-usage --time-period Start=2026-09-01,End=2026-10-01 --granularity MONTHLY \
    --metrics UnblendedCost --group-by Type=DIMENSION,Key=SERVICE

Amazon Elastic Compute Cloud - Compute     $41,870
Amazon Relational Database Service         $18,240
EC2 - Other                                $13,905    # NAT gateways, data transfer, disks, snapshots
Amazon Simple Storage Service               $9,410
Amazon CloudWatch                           $6,730    # almost all of it log ingestion
Elastic Load Balancing                      $3,120
Everything else                             $2,480
                                   Total   $95,755

Three habits make this useful. Open the vague lines. "EC2 - Other" hides NAT gateway processing, cross-zone data transfer and orphaned disks, and is where most surprises live. Look at the trend, not the total. A line that grew 40% in a month is a question even if it is small. Divide by something. A bill that doubles while orders triple is good news. The number to track is unit cost: cost per order, per active customer, per thousand requests. It tells you whether the system is becoming more or less efficient, which the total cannot.

For anything deeper than the explorer's charts, providers export the raw line items (AWS calls it the Cost and Usage Report; Google Cloud and Azure export billing data to their warehouses), and teams query them with SQL like any other data set.

Tagging and allocation

A bill nobody owns is a bill nobody reduces. Allocation means every pound of spend is attributed to a team, and that team sees it.

  • Accounts are the cleanest split: one per workload per environment gives a bill per workload with no effort (Chapter 30).
  • Tags split within an account. Agree on a short required set (team, service, environment, cost-centre) and apply it in code, never by hand.
  • Shared resources have to be divided by measurement. A Kubernetes cluster is one line on the bill; tools such as OpenCost divide node cost between namespaces by what their pods request.
provider "aws" {
  default_tags {                         # every resource this configuration creates carries these
    tags = {
      team        = "payments"
      service     = "checkout"
      environment = "prod"
      cost-centre = "cc-4410"
    }
  }
}

Tags must be switched on as cost-allocation tags in the billing settings before they appear in reports, and they are not retroactive. Some charges cannot be tagged at all (support plans, some data transfer), so decide a rule for splitting them. What you do with the numbers is a choice between showback, where each team is shown its costs, and chargeback, where the cost is actually moved to the team's budget. Showback with a monthly review gets most of the benefit and far fewer arguments.

Rightsizing

Most cloud resources are bigger than they need to be, because sizes are chosen once, cautiously, before anyone has data. Rightsizing is choosing again from measurements. Work in this order, because each step changes the numbers the next one uses.

$ kubectl get vpa checkout -n shop -o jsonpath='{.status.recommendation.containerRecommendations[0]}'
{"containerName":"checkout","target":{"cpu":"310m","memory":"920Mi"},"upperBound":{"cpu":"650m","memory":"1400Mi"}}

$ kubectl get deploy checkout -n shop -o jsonpath='{.spec.template.spec.containers[0].resources.requests}'
{"cpu":"2","memory":"4Gi"}            # requested: more than six times what it uses. Nodes are bought for requests

Scheduling deserves its own mention because the arithmetic is so stark. A week has 168 hours. A development environment used from 08:00 to 18:00 on weekdays is needed for 50 of them. Switching it off for the other 118 saves 70% of its compute cost, and needs only a scheduled scale-to-zero.

Savings plans and reservations

Cloud providers sell the same computer at three prices, depending on what you promise them.

ModelYou promiseTypical discountRiskUse it for
On-demandNothingNone: the list priceNonePeaks, new workloads, anything you cannot forecast
Commitment, 1 yearTo pay for a fixed amount of usage every hour for a year, used or notAbout 25–40%You shrink, migrate or rightsize, and keep payingThe steady base you are confident will exist in a year
Commitment, 3 yearsThe same, for three yearsAbout 45–60%Three years is a long time for a forecastThe most stable core: databases, base load of a mature product
SpotThat the provider may take it back at two minutes' noticeAbout 60–90%InterruptionBatch jobs, CI, stateless workers that tolerate loss (Chapter 3)

Commitments come in two forms. A reservation is for a specific instance family in a specific region, with the deepest discount. A savings plan (Google Cloud's equivalent is a flexible committed-use discount) is a promise to spend a number of dollars per hour on compute of any shape, for slightly less discount and far more freedom to change instance types. Most teams should start with the flexible kind.

One piece of arithmetic governs the decision. A committed unit costs (1 − discount) of the on-demand price for every hour, used or not. An on-demand unit costs the full price only for the hours it runs. So a commitment pays off only for capacity that is in use for more than (1 − discount) of the time: more than 70% of hours at a 30% discount, more than 50% at a 50% discount. That is why the rule is commit to the base, not the peak. Two numbers track how well it is going: coverage, the share of your usage that is on a commitment, and utilisation, the share of your commitment that is being used. Utilisation below 100% is money paid for nothing.

Egress costs

Storing and computing are priced where you expect. Moving data is priced in places that do not appear on any architecture diagram. Rough list prices on AWS, in US dollars, at the time of writing; the other providers are similar in shape:

PathRough price per GBNote
Internet → cloudFreeGetting data in costs nothing. This is deliberate
Cloud → internet$0.09, falling with volume"Egress". The charge that makes leaving a provider expensive
Between zones in a region$0.01 each wayEvery call between services in different zones, every database replica
Between regionsAbout $0.02Replication for multi-region designs (Chapter 33)
Through a NAT gateway$0.045 processingOn top of any transfer charge, for everything private subnets send out
To object storage in the same region, via a gateway endpointFreeThe fix for the line above, for storage traffic

Three small sums show the scale. A service that sends 50 TB a month to its users pays 50,000 × $0.09 = $4,500 for egress. Microservices spread over three zones that exchange 200 TB a month across zone boundaries pay $0.01 in each direction: $4,000, for traffic that never left the building. A cluster in private subnets that pulls 30 TB of images and storage objects through a NAT gateway pays 30,000 × $0.045 = $1,350 that a free gateway endpoint would remove. The defences are a CDN in front of anything public (the Networks course has a chapter on CDNs), zone-aware routing so that calls prefer the caller's own zone, endpoints for storage, and compression. Every day repeats, and a month is 730 hours. A committed unit costs the discounted price for all 24 hours; load above the commitment is bought on demand at full price. "Best of the six" compares the commitment levels on offer for the future you chose, which is knowledge you do not have when you sign.

Things to try.

  • The starting position commits to the daily peak of 60 for one year. It costs $3,066 a month against $3,042 for buying nothing: 1% more, with $937 a month paid for idle capacity. The top 20 units are in use 42% of the time, and they need 70%.
  • Step the commitment down. 40 units cost $2,652 and 20 units cost $2,604, a 14% saving and the best on offer. Units 21 to 40 run 16 hours in 24, which is 67%: just under break-even at 30% off.
  • Change the term to three years. Break-even drops to 50%, those same units now pay, and the best level moves up to 40 at $2,068, a 32% saving. A deeper discount justifies committing further up the curve, for longer.
  • Commit to 60 for three years, then rightsize. The bill is $2,190 where on-demand would now be $1,825: 20% more than buying nothing, with $1,278 a month of commitment idle for three years. Rightsizing first and committing to 20 would cost $1,290.
  • Spiky load, commit to 100 for three years: $3,650 against $1,947, which is 88% more. The peak lasts two hours a day. The best level is 20, the base.
  • Steady load, commit to 40: the only case in which you collect the whole discount, 30% or 50%. Every unit above the flat line is pure waste.
  • Daily cycle, commit 40 for one year, then growth: a 14% saving and now the best of the six. Being under-committed costs you a missed discount. Being over-committed costs you money. That asymmetry is why you commit to the base you are sure of.
  • In all 108 settings, adding a block of commitment lowers the bill exactly when that block is in use for more than (1 − discount) of the time.

Use case: Pinterest and the forecast that became a contract

When Pinterest prepared to go public in 2019, its filing disclosed something most companies of its size have and few discuss: an agreement, signed in 2017, to spend at least $750 million with Amazon Web Services over about six years. Large customers negotiate these enterprise agreements for a discount across their whole bill, and the mechanism is exactly the one in the simulator, scaled up. You forecast your usage, promise to pay for it, and receive a lower price in return. Lyft's filing the same year showed a commitment of at least $300 million over three years, and Snap's, two years earlier, $2 billion to Google Cloud over five. A forecast can be wrong in either direction. Press reports at the time said that in 2018 Pinterest had paid for a year of capacity in advance and then needed more than it had reserved, particularly around the holiday season, and bought the difference at a higher price, at a cost of some $20 million. Growing faster than planned is the pleasant way to be wrong. The other way, committing to capacity for a product that shrinks, or for instances you are about to rightsize away, leaves you paying for idle machines until the term ends. The practical lessons are the ones on the panel. Commit in layers that expire at different times, not in one block. Cover the base you would bet on, and leave the rest on demand. And do the rightsizing before you sign, because a commitment freezes your waste into the contract.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Committing before rightsizing. You promise to keep paying for the capacity you were about to remove.
  • Committing to the peak. The top of the curve is used a few hours a day and loses money at any discount.
  • Judging by the total bill. Without a unit cost you cannot tell growth from waste.
  • Tagging by hand, or late. Tags are not retroactive, and hand-applied tags are inconsistent within a week.
  • Rightsizing from averages. A service averaging 20% CPU may peak at 90%. Use a high percentile over weeks.
  • Development environments that run all night and all weekend.
  • Ignoring data transfer because it is not on the architecture diagram.
  • Logs and metrics kept at full detail for ever. Ingestion is often the fifth-largest line on the bill (Chapter 22).
  • No budget alarms. The first signal of a runaway cost is then the invoice.
  • A cost team that sends spreadsheets to engineers who have no way to see their own numbers day to day.
  • Cutting cost by cutting redundancy without saying so. Removing the second zone is a reliability decision, and belongs in that conversation.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What are the pricing models for cloud compute, and when do you use each?

How a strong answer sounds
There are three. On-demand is the list price with no promise: use it for peaks and for anything new or unpredictable. Commitments, meaning reserved instances or savings plans, give roughly 30% off for one year or 50% for three, in exchange for paying for a fixed amount every hour whether you use it or not. They suit the steady base load. The break-even is simple: a committed unit is worth it only if it is in use for more than one minus the discount of the time, so above 70% of hours at 30% off. Spot is spare capacity at 60 to 90% off that can be taken back at short notice: use it for batch jobs, CI and stateless workers that tolerate interruption. A sensible fleet uses all three: commitments under the base, on-demand or spot for the daily rise, spot for anything interruptible.

Deeper: The cloud bill grew 40% in a quarter and the finance director wants to know why. How do you approach it?

How a strong answer sounds
First I find out whether it is a problem. If traffic or customers grew 50% in the same quarter, unit cost fell and the answer is that the business grew. So I start by dividing: cost per order or per active user, this quarter against last. If unit cost rose, I look for where. I group the bill by service, then by account or team tag, and compare the two quarters line by line, sorted by the size of the increase and not by total. Usually two or three lines explain most of it. Then I open those lines by usage type, which separates more hours from a higher rate from data transfer. Typical findings are: a commitment expired and that usage fell back to on-demand; a new service logging at debug level; a NAT gateway or cross-zone traffic that grew with a new architecture; an environment or cluster someone forgot; storage growing with no lifecycle rule. Each has an owner and a fix, and I would report them as a short list with amounts and dates. Then the lasting changes, since otherwise we repeat this every quarter: budget alarms and anomaly detection per account, a dashboard where each team sees its own spend and unit cost, required tags enforced in the pipeline, and a monthly review. For the director the summary is one sentence on how much of the growth is the business growing, how much is waste we are removing, and when the bill will show it.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • FinOps makes cost visible to the engineers whose decisions create it, and measures it like any other signal.
  • Track unit cost. A rising total with a falling unit cost is growth.
  • Allocate with accounts first, tags second, and usage-based splits for shared platforms.
  • Order of work: delete, rightsize, schedule, cheaper pricing, commit, re-architect.
  • A commitment pays only for capacity used more than (1 − discount) of the time. Commit to the base.
  • Watch coverage and utilisation. Rightsize before you sign.
  • Data transfer is charged out to the internet, between zones, between regions and through NAT gateways.

Check your understanding

A one-year commitment gives 30% off. Above what share of hours must a unit of capacity be in use for the commitment to beat on-demand?

The committed unit costs 0.7 of the hourly price for every hour. On-demand costs 1.0 for the hours used. They are equal at 70% usage.

The monthly bill rose from $80,000 to $100,000 while orders rose from 2 million to 3 million. What happened to efficiency?

Unit cost is the measure. $80,000 / 2M = 4.0 cents; $100,000 / 3M ≈ 3.3 cents.

A team plans to rightsize its services next quarter and buy a three-year commitment this week. What is wrong with the order?

Rightsize first, then commit to the smaller, measured base.

Pods in private subnets download 20 TB a month from object storage in the same region through a NAT gateway. What removes most of that cost?

NAT gateways charge per gigabyte processed. A gateway endpoint routes storage traffic privately and free of that charge.

A development environment is used 08:00–18:00 on weekdays and runs all week. Roughly how much of its compute cost would scheduling save?

50 of the week's 168 hours are needed. Switching off for the other 118 saves 118 / 168 ≈ 70%.
