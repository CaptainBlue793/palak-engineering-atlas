SLOs, Error Budgets & Alerting

The last three chapters produced metrics, logs and traces. This one decides what to do with them at three in the morning. An alert is a decision to interrupt a person, and most teams make that decision badly in both directions: they are woken for things that did not matter, and they sleep through things that did. The cure is to stop alerting on what machines are doing and alert on what users were promised. That promise is a service level objective, the room it leaves is an error budget, and the speed at which the budget is being spent, the burn rate, is the best alert signal anyone has found. The simulator runs five incidents past four alert rules so that you can see which rules catch what.

SLIs and SLOs

A service level indicator (SLI) is a number that measures how well the service is doing something users care about. The most useful form is always the same: a ratio of good events to all valid events.

              good events              requests answered without a server error
   SLI  =  ───────────────    e.g.    ──────────────────────────────────────────
             valid events                     all requests (except health checks)
Kind of SLIA good event isFits
AvailabilityA request that did not fail with a server errorAny request-driven service
LatencyA request answered within a threshold, such as 300 msAnything interactive. State it as a share of requests, not as an average
FreshnessA read that returned data newer than some agePipelines, caches, search indexes, replicas
CorrectnessAn output that passed a checkBatch jobs, data processing
DurabilityA stored object that can still be readStorage systems

Measure as close to the user as you can: at the load balancer or the gateway, not inside the service, because a request the service never received is not in the service's own counters. And exclude what is not a real user request.

A service level objective (SLO) is a target for an SLI over a window: "99.9% of requests succeed, measured over the last 30 days". Three properties make an SLO work. It is set from what users notice, not from what the system happens to achieve. It is less than 100%, because 100% is unreachable and leaves no room to change anything. And it is agreed by the people who build the service and the people who depend on it, because its real function is to settle arguments in advance.

SLISLOSLA
What it isA measurementAn internal target for that measurementA contract with customers
Example99.93% of requests succeeded in the last 30 daysAt least 99.9%Below 99.5% in a month, the customer gets a credit
If missedNothing: it is a factThe team changes its prioritiesMoney is paid
Set byEngineeringEngineering and product togetherThe business and its lawyers

The SLA is always looser than the SLO, so that the team hears about a problem long before the customer is owed anything.

# Availability SLI over 30 days, as a recording rule
groups:
  - name: checkout-slo
    rules:
      - record: slo:checkout_requests:error_ratio_rate5m
        expr: |
          sum(rate(http_requests_total{service="checkout", code=~"5.."}[5m]))
          / sum(rate(http_requests_total{service="checkout"}[5m]))

# Latency SLI: the share of requests faster than 300 ms, straight from the histogram
  sum(rate(http_request_duration_seconds_bucket{service="checkout", le="0.3"}[5m]))
/ sum(rate(http_request_duration_seconds_count{service="checkout"}[5m]))

Error budgets

If the objective is 99.9%, then 0.1% of requests are allowed to fail. That allowance is the error budget, and it is easier to reason about as a quantity than as a percentage.

SLO 99.9% over 30 days            budget = 0.1%

  in time:      30 days × 24 × 60 = 43,200 minutes    × 0.001  =  43.2 minutes of total outage
  in requests:  200 requests a second × 2,592,000 s   × 0.001  =  518,400 failed requests

  a 10-minute total outage spends   10 ÷ 43.2  =  23% of the month's budget
  an hour at 5% errors spends       60 × 0.05 ÷ 43.2  =  6.9%

The budget turns reliability into something that can be spent. Releases, experiments, migrations and plain bad luck all draw on it. While there is budget left, the team ships; when it is gone, the agreed error budget policy applies, typically that feature releases pause and work goes to reliability until the service is back inside its objective. Chapter 1 introduced that policy as the thing that aligns developers and operators. The rest of this chapter uses the budget for something else: deciding when to wake someone.

Burn-rate alerts

The naive alert is a threshold: "page if the error rate is above 1% for five minutes". It has two failures. A service failing 0.4% of its requests for three days never trips it, and has spent 40% of the month's budget before anyone knows. And the threshold is arbitrary: is 1% bad? For a service with a 99.9% objective it is ten times the allowed rate; for one with a 99% objective it is exactly on target.

The burn rate fixes both by measuring against the objective. It is how fast the budget is being spent, relative to the speed that would use it up exactly at the end of the window:

                 error ratio over some recent window
  burn rate  =  ──────────────────────────────────────          (budget ratio = 1 − SLO)
                          budget ratio

  SLO 99.9%:   0.1% errors  →  burn rate 1     the budget lasts exactly 30 days
               1.44% errors →  burn rate 14.4  the budget is gone in 30 ÷ 14.4 = 2.1 days
               100% errors  →  burn rate 1000  the budget is gone in 43 minutes

An alert on burn rate says "at this pace we will miss the objective", which is the only thing worth being woken for. To make it both quick and trustworthy, each alert uses two windows: a long one, so that it only fires when a meaningful share of the budget is really at stake, and a short one, so that it stops firing once the problem is over instead of staying on until the long window has emptied. And there are several pairs, because a fast burn needs a page in minutes while a slow burn needs a ticket by tomorrow. The set below comes from Google's SRE workbook and is the usual starting point.

Things to try. Go through the incidents with each rule and keep a mental table.

  • 5% errors for an hour. The fixed threshold pages after 5 minutes, with 0.6% of the month's budget gone. The burn-rate rules page after 18 minutes, with 2.1% gone. On this incident the simple rule is faster, and it is fair to notice that. The burn-rate rule waited until 2% of the budget was really at stake, which is what it promises.
  • 0.4% errors for three days. The threshold never fires. Neither does the single fast burn-rate rule. The incident spends 40% of the month's budget unseen. The multi-window rule opens a ticket after 17.7 hours, with 9.8% spent: not a page, because nothing here needs anyone out of bed.
  • A two-minute blip. It costs 0.9% of the budget and clears by itself. Only the "any bad minute" rule pages for it.
  • Nothing at all. That same rule pages 14 times in four quiet days, once for every noisy minute. Every other rule stays silent. This is the difference between an alert on a rate and an alert on a budget.
  • A total outage. The burn-rate rules page in the first minute, with 2.3% of the budget spent; the threshold needs its five minutes, by which time 11.6% is gone.
  • After the fix. For the hour of 5% errors, the single one-hour rule keeps paging for 42 minutes after recovery, because the bad hour is still in its window. The multi-window rule lets go after 26. After a total outage the figures are 59 and 29 minutes, and a ticket stays open as a record of what the incident cost.
  • The table you end up with: the multi-window rule is the only one of the four that pages for both outages, opens a ticket for the slow leak, and says nothing about the blip or the quiet days.

Alert fatigue

A person who is paged ten times a night for things that needed no action learns, correctly, that pages do not matter. Then the eleventh page is the real one. This is alert fatigue, and it is a property of the alerting system, not a weakness of the people in it. Every alert that reaches a person should pass four tests.

  • It is about a symptom users feel: errors, latency, data that is wrong or stale. Not CPU, not a full disk that is hours from mattering, not a single pod restarting.
  • It is urgent: if it can wait until morning, it is a ticket.
  • It is actionable: there is something for a person to do. If the response is always "wait and it clears", automate the wait or delete the alert.
  • It says what to do: the alert links to a runbook and a dashboard, and names the service and the objective at risk.

The alert manager helps with the volume. It groups related alerts into one notification, inhibits the consequences when a cause is already firing (no page for each of fifty services if the whole cluster is unreachable), and silences known work. And the team keeps score: pages per on-call shift, and for each page whether it led to an action. An alert that has fired ten times without anyone doing anything is deleted or fixed, not tolerated.

Use case: Three Mile Island and a hundred alarms

The clearest account of alert fatigue is not from software. In the early hours of 28 March 1979, a routine fault in the cooling system of the Three Mile Island nuclear plant in Pennsylvania led to a relief valve opening and then failing to close. The presidential commission that investigated the accident described what the operators faced. Within the first minutes more than a hundred alarms were sounding or flashing in the control room, with no way of telling the important ones from the rest, and the printer that logged alarm messages fell hours behind events. Meanwhile the one indication that mattered was misleading: a light on the panel showed that the valve had been told to close, not that it had closed, so the operators believed it was shut while coolant drained away for over two hours. They acted reasonably on what they could see, and it made things worse. The commission's criticism was aimed at the design of the room, not at the people in it. Every lesson from this chapter is there at full scale. An alarm for everything is an alarm for nothing. A signal must report the state the user depends on, not the command that was sent. And the moment of crisis is exactly when the volume of alerts is highest and the capacity to read them is lowest, which is why the work of deciding what deserves a page has to be done beforehand.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Paging on causes: CPU above 80%, memory high, a pod restarted. Users may be perfectly fine.
  • An SLO of 100%, or one copied from another team, or one set tighter than users can perceive.
  • An SLO nobody agreed to. If missing it changes nothing, it is a dashboard, not an objective.
  • Averages as SLIs. "Mean latency under 200 ms" is met while one user in fifty waits five seconds.
  • Static thresholds. Too sensitive for brief spikes and blind to slow, steady burns.
  • One long window. The alert keeps firing for an hour after the fix, and people learn to ignore it.
  • Alerts with no runbook and no owner. The page arrives and the person has to work out what it even means.
  • Never deleting alerts. Every incident adds one; nothing removes any.
  • Measuring the SLI inside the service. When the service is down, its own metrics say nothing is failing.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What are an SLI, an SLO and an error budget?

How a strong answer sounds
An SLI is a measurement of how well a service does something users care about, expressed as the share of good events: for example, the fraction of requests that succeed, or that are answered within 300 ms. An SLO is the target for that measurement over a window, such as 99.9% over 30 days, agreed between the people who run the service and those who depend on it. The error budget is the difference from 100%: with 99.9%, 0.1% of requests, or about 43 minutes of total outage a month, may fail. The budget is what makes the objective useful. It can be spent on releases and experiments, it tells us when to slow down and work on reliability, and the rate at which it is being spent is what we alert on. An SLA is the external contract, set looser than the SLO.

Deeper: Your team is paged about forty times a week and most pages need no action. How do you fix the alerting?

How a strong answer sounds
I would start with data: export a month of pages and classify each by alert name, whether anyone took an action, and whether users were affected. Usually a handful of alerts produce most of the noise, and most of them are about causes, such as CPU, memory or a restarting pod, with thresholds someone picked years ago. Then I rebuild from the user's side. For each user-facing service, define one or two SLIs measured at the edge, agree an SLO with the product owner, and replace the cause-based pages with multi-window burn-rate alerts on that SLO: fast burn pages, slow burn opens a ticket. The cause-based signals move to dashboards, where the person handling a page will look at them. Whatever remains has to pass four tests, user impact, urgency, an action to take and a runbook, and gets a named owner. In the alert manager I group by service and inhibit downstream alerts when a shared dependency is already firing, so one failure produces one page. Then I keep it healthy: a weekly review of every page with the question "did this need a human, now?", a target such as no more than two pages per shift, and a rule that an alert which fires repeatedly without action is tuned or deleted. I would expect the page count to fall by an order of magnitude, and the important thing to say to the team is that detection does not get worse: the burn-rate alerts catch the slow problems that the old thresholds never saw.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • An SLI is good events over valid events, measured near the user. An SLO is its target over a window. An SLA is a looser contract.
  • The error budget is 1 minus the SLO: 43.2 minutes of outage a month at 99.9%.
  • Burn rate is the error ratio divided by the budget ratio. At 14.4 the month's budget is gone in two days.
  • Alert on burn rate with two windows: a long one for significance, a short one so that the alert clears quickly.
  • Fast burns page; slow burns open tickets; causes go on dashboards.
  • A page must be about a user-felt symptom, urgent, actionable and linked to a runbook.
  • Count pages and prune alerts. Fatigue is a defect of the system, not of the people.

Check your understanding

A service has an SLO of 99.9% over 30 days. It suffers a total outage of 20 minutes. Roughly how much of the month's error budget did that use?

The budget is 0.1% of 43,200 minutes, which is 43.2 minutes. Twenty minutes of total failure is 20 ÷ 43.2, about 46%.

With a 99.9% SLO, a service's error ratio over the last hour is 1.44%. What is its burn rate?

Burn rate is the error ratio divided by the budget ratio: 1.44% ÷ 0.1% = 14.4, and 30 days ÷ 14.4 is 2.1 days.

A service fails 0.4% of requests for three days. The only alert is "error rate above 1% for 5 minutes". What happens?

0.4% is four times the allowed rate and never crosses a 1% threshold. A burn-rate rule over a long window is what catches slow, steady damage.

Why do burn-rate alerts combine a long window with a short one?

A long window alone keeps firing long after recovery, because the bad period is still inside it. Requiring the short window to be burning as well clears the alert much sooner.

Which of these should wake the on-call engineer?

Only the third is a user-felt symptom that is urgent now. The certificate is a ticket; the CPU and the restarts belong on a dashboard.
