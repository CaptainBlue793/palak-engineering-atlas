DevOps & SRE Interview Playbook

Interviews for DevOps, platform and SRE roles test something that most technical interviews do not: how you behave when a system you did not build is misbehaving and the information is incomplete. Knowing facts matters, and this course has supplied a good many. What interviewers are really listening for is method: do you ask before you assume, do you narrow the problem down, do you stop the damage before chasing the cause, and can you say what you would check and why. This chapter maps the questions to the chapters that answer them, walks through the two commonest troubleshooting scenarios, gives the Kubernetes question nearly everyone is asked, shows how to talk about incidents, and lists the traps. The drill puts sixteen broken pods in front of you.

  • Know what each kind of interview round is testing
  • Apply one repeatable method to any troubleshooting question
  • Explain what happens between kubectl apply and a pod serving traffic
  • Read a pod's status and events and name the likely cause
  • Tell an incident story that shows judgement, with numbers and no blame
  • Recognise statements that sound right and are not

Three habits help in every round. Ask before answering: what scale, what constraints, what has already been tried. Think aloud: the interviewer can only assess reasoning they can hear. And say when you do not know, then say how you would find out. "I have not used that, but I would expect it to work like this, and I would check by doing that" is a strong answer. A confident guess that is wrong is the weakest one.

Troubleshooting walkthroughs

Troubleshooting questions are deliberately vague. The vagueness is the test: a candidate who starts typing commands at "the site is slow" has already lost marks. Use the same method every time.

"The site is slow." A good answer sounds like this, in outline. Slow for whom: everyone, or one country, or logged-in users? Measured how: the p99 on our dashboard, or one person's impression? Since when, and did anything ship around then? Suppose the interviewer says "all users, p99 went from 200 ms to 3 s, starting 20 minutes ago, and there was a deploy 25 minutes ago". Then say you would roll the deploy back, and continue the diagnosis while that happens. If there was no deploy, follow the request. Is latency high at the load balancer or only at one service? Tracing (Chapter 23) shows which span grew. If it is one service: are its pods saturated (CPU throttling, memory, connection pool), or waiting on a dependency such as the database? If the database: a slow query, lock contention, or a missing index after a data growth? At each step name the signal you would look at, using the four golden signals from Chapter 21: latency, traffic, errors, saturation.

"The deployment succeeded, but users are getting errors." "Succeeded" needs unpacking: the pipeline was green, or the rollout completed, or the pods are Ready? Each means something different. Then: are the errors from the new pods only? If so, compare versions and roll back. If old and new pods both fail, the change was probably not the code: look for a migration, a config or secret change, or a dependency. If pods are Ready and users still fail, the readiness probe is not testing what users need, and that is a finding in itself. Do not forget the path in front of the pods: the Service's selector, the Ingress rule, and whether old pods were removed before the load balancer stopped sending to them.

Kubernetes questions

The networking interview has "what happens when you type a URL". The Kubernetes interview has this:

"What happens when you run kubectl apply -f deployment.yaml?"

A strong answer has that last sentence in it. The point is the pattern: declared state in etcd, and independent control loops converging on it. From there the interviewer will pull on a thread, so be ready for each:

  • Deployment, StatefulSet or DaemonSet? Interchangeable replicas; replicas with stable names and their own storage; one per node (Chapters 12, 15).
  • Liveness, readiness, startup probes? Restart it; stop sending it traffic; give it time before the others begin. A liveness probe should not check dependencies.
  • Requests and limits? Requests are what the scheduler reserves; limits are where the kernel throttles CPU or kills for memory (Chapter 25).
  • How does a Service find pods? By label selector, into a list of ready endpoints (Chapter 12).
  • How does a rolling update avoid downtime? Surge and unavailable limits, readiness gating, and graceful shutdown of old pods (Chapter 17).
  • What happens if the control plane goes down? Running pods keep running; nothing new is scheduled or repaired (Chapter 11).
  • How do you keep a secret out of Git? An external store, synced or injected (Chapters 13, 28).

Incident questions

"Tell me about an incident you were involved in" is asked in almost every loop. It is a behavioural question, and the interviewer is assessing how you think about failure. Give the story a shape:

  • Context, in two sentences: what the system does and what your role was.
  • Detection: how you found out, and how long after it started.
  • Impact, with numbers: who was affected, for how long, how badly.
  • What you did, in order, including the wrong turns. Say "I", for your own actions.
  • Mitigation before diagnosis: what stopped the harm, and when the cause was found.
  • What changed afterwards: the specific fixes, and what you would do differently.

What interviewers listen for is ownership without blame. Describe the conditions that made the mistake possible, yours included, and what was done about them. Stories that fail tend to fail in the same ways: someone else is the villain, the candidate is the lone hero, there are no numbers, or nothing was learnt. If you have never been in a production incident, say so and use the nearest thing, a failed deployment or a broken build that blocked a team, and tell it with the same structure. The hypothetical version, "you are paged at 2 a.m., what do you do?", is answered with the sequence from Chapter 37: confirm, declare, ask what changed, mitigate, communicate, watch, hand over.

How to use the drill.

  
    
    
  • Say the cause aloud before you click, and then say the next command before you read it. That pair, cause and next step, is what an interviewer wants to hear.
    • 
    
  • Six of the sixteen pods never started (two image pulls, two Pending, a missing Secret, a failing init container). For these the application's logs are empty and the answer is in the events. Reaching for kubectl logs first is the commonest wasted minute.
    • 
    
  • Three were killed or crashed (missing configuration, out of memory, evicted). Here the last state, the exit code and --previous logs carry the answer.
    • 
    
  • Six are running and still not serving properly (not Ready, no endpoints, liveness restarts, a DNS name, an idle autoscaler, errors during deploys). Nothing looks broken in get pods, so you follow the request. These are the ones interviewers like most.
    • 
    
  • One is stuck Terminating, and it is the only round where the right answer includes "do not act yet".
    • 
    
  • After a run, the panel lists the ones you missed. Twelve or more on the first attempt is a good score; all sixteen without hesitating is the standard for an on-call engineer.

Trick questions

  
Some statements are offered in interviews to see whether you will agree with them. Each of these sounds reasonable and is wrong, or right only with a condition the interviewer wants to hear.

  

    

      
The statementWhat is wrong with itChapter

      
        
"A container is a lightweight virtual machine."It is an ordinary process with restricted views and limits, sharing the host's kernel. That is why it starts fast and why isolation is weaker7

        
"Kubernetes Secrets are encrypted."They are base64-encoded. Encryption at rest must be switched on, and anyone who can read Secrets or create pods in the namespace can read them13

        
":latest is the newest version."It is whatever was last pushed without a tag, and it can change under you. Pin a version or a digest9

        
"A rolling update means zero downtime."Only with correct readiness probes, enough surge capacity and graceful shutdown. Without them it is a rolling outage12, 17

        
"We have a replica in another zone, so we have backups."A replica copies deletions and corruption within seconds. Backups go back in time; replicas do not32

        
"We should aim for 100% availability."It is unreachable, users cannot tell it from 99.99%, and it leaves no budget for change. Choose a target and spend the error budget1, 24

        
"More alerts mean better monitoring."Pages nobody can act on train people to ignore the pager. Alert on symptoms users feel24

        
"With autoscaling we do not need capacity planning."Autoscaling works inside quotas and reacts after load arrives. Launches and limits still need a plan39

        
"Infrastructure as code means there is no drift."Only if nobody changes things by hand and plans are run. Drift has to be detected19, 18

        
"The liveness probe should check the database."Then a database blip restarts every pod at once. Liveness checks only whether this process is stuck12

        
"The image is signed, so it is safe."A signature proves who built it, not what is in it29

        
"DevOps is the team that handles deployments."It is a way of working in which the people who build software share responsibility for running it. A separate "DevOps team" can be the old wall with a new name1, 35

Use case: the troubleshooting interview as a role-play
Google has described the structure of its Site Reliability Engineering interviews publicly, and the format has been widely copied. Alongside coding and system design there is a round on troubleshooting, and it is run as a role-play. The interviewer describes a symptom, something like "users say the service is slow", and then acts as the system and its tools. The candidate cannot type anything; they say what they would look at, and the interviewer tells them what they see. There is a scripted cause, usually several layers down, and the candidate may or may not reach it in the time. Interviewers who run these rounds are consistent about what they score, and it is not reaching the cause. They watch whether the candidate first establishes what "slow" means and who is affected. They watch whether each question halves the remaining possibilities or just wanders. They note whether the candidate says what result they expect from a check before asking for it, which shows a hypothesis and not a fishing trip. They give credit for noticing when the evidence contradicts a theory and dropping it, and for remembering, somewhere along the way, to ask whether anything can be done for the users before the cause is known. A candidate who methodically narrows the problem and runs out of time scores better than one who guesses the cause at once and cannot say why. The same holds on the job, which is why the round exists.

  


  
⚠️
Common mistakes

    
    
      
  • Answering a vague question without clarifying it.
    • 
      
  • Jumping to a cause. "It's probably DNS" is a joke, not a method.
    • 
      
  • Listing tools in place of reasoning. "I would use Prometheus and Grafana" says nothing about what you would look for.
    • 
      
  • Never mentioning mitigation. In a real outage the users come before the root cause.
    • 
      
  • Silence while thinking. Narrate; the reasoning is what is being assessed.
    • 
      
  • Bluffing. Interviewers pull on confident answers until they hold or break.
    • 
      
  • Agreeing with a trap because the interviewer said it with a straight face.
    • 
      
  • An incident story with a villain, or with no numbers and no lesson.
    • 
      
  • Describing only the happy path in a design: no failure modes, no rollback, no cost.
    • 
      
  • Reciting definitions when asked for experience. One concrete example beats three textbook sentences.

Interview drill

    
Explain it simply: A pod is in CrashLoopBackOff. What do you do?

    
How a strong answer sounds
CrashLoopBackOff means the container starts, exits, and the kubelet is waiting longer and longer between restarts. So the question is why it exits. First, kubectl describe pod for the last state: the exit code and reason. If the reason is OOMKilled, exit code 137, it is hitting its memory limit, and I compare usage with the limit. Otherwise I read the logs of the previous attempt with kubectl logs --previous, because the current container may have only just started. Usually that shows it: a missing environment variable or config file, a dependency it cannot reach at start-up, a bad command or argument, a failed migration. If the logs are empty, I check whether the image runs at all, for instance the wrong architecture or entrypoint. I also check the events for a liveness probe killing it, which looks similar and has a different fix. And I ask what changed: if this started with a new release, I roll back first and investigate after.

    
Deeper: Requests to a service fail intermittently, about one in twenty. Nothing was deployed today. How do you approach it?

    
How a strong answer sounds
Intermittent failures are usually one bad member of a set, so my first aim is to find what the failing requests have in common. I would start with scope: which errors, 5xx or timeouts, from which callers, since when. One in twenty is suggestive: with twenty pods, one bad pod gives exactly that; so does one bad node, one zone, or one of several backends. So I would break the error rate down by pod, by node and by zone. If one pod stands out, I take it out of service by changing its label so it keeps its state for inspection, and the errors should stop, which both mitigates and confirms. If it is one node, I look at that node: disk, memory pressure, a network problem, a noisy neighbour. If errors are spread evenly over pods, the cause is likely downstream or in front: a dependency with one bad replica, a connection pool or database limit being reached at peaks, DNS resolution failing occasionally, or a load balancer still sending to a target that is gone. Tracing a few failed requests end to end shows which hop returns the error. I would also check the time pattern: every few minutes suggests a periodic job or a garbage collection pause; only at peak suggests saturation; since a specific time suggests a change I have not found yet, such as a certificate, a config reload, a node replaced by the autoscaler, or a dependency's deployment, since "nothing was deployed" usually means "nothing of ours". Throughout I would say what I expect each check to show. Once fixed, I would ask why our readiness checks and alerts did not catch a pod or node failing one request in twenty, since that is the lasting fix.

  


  

    
🧠 Key takeaways

    
    
      
  • Know which round you are in: fundamentals, troubleshooting, delivery, design, reliability practice or behaviour.
    • 
      
  • Troubleshooting is a method: clarify, scope, ask what changed, follow the request, test cheaply, mitigate, close the loop.
    • 
      
  • For kubectl apply, tell the story of declared state and independent control loops.
    • 
      
  • A pod's status and last events usually name the problem. Know the common ones on sight.
    • 
      
  • Incident stories need context, detection, impact in numbers, your actions, mitigation and what changed.
    • 
      
  • Do not agree with a statement because it sounds familiar. Say the condition that makes it true.
    • 
      
  • Narrate your reasoning, and say what you do not know.

An interviewer says: "The site is slow. What do you do?" What is the best first response?

      

        
        
        
        
      

      
The question is vague on purpose. Clarifying the symptom and its scope, and asking what changed, is the first step of the method.

    


    

      
A container's last state shows exit code 137 and the reason OOMKilled. What happened?

      

        
        
        
        
      

      
137 is 128 + 9: the process received SIGKILL. With the reason OOMKilled, the cause is the memory limit.

    


    

      
After kubectl apply stores a Deployment, which component decides which node each new pod runs on?

      

        
        
        
        
      

      
Controllers create the ReplicaSet and the pods. The scheduler assigns each unscheduled pod to a node, and the kubelet on that node starts it.

    


    

      
Pods are Running and Ready, but the Service's endpoints list is empty. What is the most likely cause?

      

        
        
        
        
      

      
A Service selects pods by label. Ready pods with different labels are invisible to it. Compare the selector with kubectl get pods --show-labels.

    


    

      
An interviewer says: "Our rolling updates give us zero downtime, since Kubernetes handles that." What is the best reply?

      

        
        
        
        
      

      
The statement is true under conditions. Naming the conditions is the answer the interviewer is looking for.
