Multi-Account Landing Zones

Most companies start in the cloud with one account that holds everything: production, experiments, backups and the administrator's access keys. It works until the day one mistake or one stolen credential reaches all of it at once. The fix that every large cloud user arrives at is the same: many accounts, each a sealed box for one workload in one environment, arranged in a hierarchy with rules that no account can switch off. The prepared environment that new workloads "land" in is called a landing zone. This chapter explains why accounts are the unit of isolation, how to arrange them, how guardrails called service control policies work, and where the logs go. The simulator evaluates requests against a small organisation's guardrails.

Why many accounts

Inside one account, everything is separated only by IAM policies, and policies are easy to get wrong. Between two accounts, the default is the opposite: nothing in one can touch anything in the other unless both sides agree. An account is therefore the boundary for four things at once.

  • Access. An administrator of the development account has no rights at all in production. There is no policy to misconfigure, because there is no path.
  • Blast radius. A deleted resource, a runaway script or a stolen credential is confined to one account.
  • Limits. Service quotas and API rate limits are per account. In a shared account, a load test that exhausts the instance quota or floods an API also stops production from scaling.
  • Cost. The bill is itemised by account with no tagging effort (Chapter 31).

The usual grain is one account per workload per environment: payments-prod, payments-dev, search-prod. That sounds like a great many accounts, and it is; large organisations run thousands. It only works if accounts are cheap to create and uniform, which is what the rest of the chapter provides. Every provider has the same idea under different names.

Organisations and OUs

An organisation joins accounts into a tree. At the top is the management account, which creates the others, pays the bill and owns the organisation's policies. It is the most powerful account you have, so it should contain no workloads and almost no people. Below it, organisational units group accounts that need the same rules. Group by policy, not by the company's org chart: departments are reorganised every year, and "production accounts must not have IAM users" is not.

People do not get a user in each account. They sign in once, through the company's identity provider, and are offered a list of roles in accounts: "ReadOnly in payments-prod", "Developer in payments-dev". Chapter 6 covered why roles with temporary credentials beat users with passwords; with fifty accounts there is no alternative.

Creating an account by hand and remembering to configure it does not scale past a handful. Account vending makes a new account a pull request.

resource "aws_organizations_organizational_unit" "prod" {
  name      = "Prod"
  parent_id = aws_organizations_organizational_unit.workloads.id
}

resource "aws_organizations_account" "payments_prod" {
  name      = "payments-prod"
  email     = "aws+payments-prod@example.com"      # every account needs a unique address: use a shared mailbox
  parent_id = aws_organizations_organizational_unit.prod.id
  tags      = { owner = "team-payments", environment = "prod" }
}

Guardrails (SCPs)

A service control policy is a policy attached to the root, an OU or an account. It looks like an IAM policy and behaves differently in three ways that are worth learning exactly.

  • It grants nothing. An SCP sets the outer limit of what anyone in the account can be allowed. A person still needs an IAM policy that allows the action. The effective permission is the overlap of the two.
  • It binds everyone in the account, including the account's administrators and its root user. This is the point. An account's own administrator can rewrite any IAM policy in it, and cannot touch an SCP.
  • It does not apply to the management account. One more reason to keep that account empty.

Inheritance has a rule that surprises people. An action must be allowed at every level from the root down to the account, and an explicit deny at any level wins. AWS attaches a policy called FullAWSAccess everywhere by default, which allows everything, and there are two ways to use it.

  • Deny list (the common choice): leave FullAWSAccess in place and add policies that deny specific things. New services work without any change.
  • Allow list: remove FullAWSAccess at some level and attach a policy that allows only named services. Everything else is denied without being mentioned. Stricter, and more upkeep. It suits sandboxes.
{
  "Version": "2012-10-17",
  "Statement": [
    { "Sid": "DenyUnapprovedRegions", "Effect": "Deny",
      "NotAction": ["iam:*", "organizations:*", "route53:*", "cloudfront:*", "sts:*", "support:*"],
      "Resource": "*",
      "Condition": { "StringNotEquals": { "aws:RequestedRegion": ["eu-west-1", "eu-west-2"] } } },

    { "Sid": "ProtectAuditTrail", "Effect": "Deny",
      "Action": ["cloudtrail:StopLogging", "cloudtrail:DeleteTrail", "cloudtrail:UpdateTrail"],
      "Resource": "*" },

    { "Sid": "DenyLeavingTheOrganisation", "Effect": "Deny",
      "Action": "organizations:LeaveOrganization", "Resource": "*" }
  ]
}

The first statement uses NotAction to exempt global services, which are not tied to a region and would otherwise break. A good starting set of guardrails is short: approved regions only, the audit trail cannot be stopped, an account cannot leave the organisation, the root user cannot be used, and in production no long-lived IAM users. Two practical cautions. Test a new SCP on a sandbox OU before attaching it higher, because a wrong deny at the root locks out every account at once. And keep a tightly controlled exemption for a "break-glass" role, written as a condition on the principal, for the day an emergency needs something the guardrail forbids.

SCPs are preventive guardrails: the request is refused. The other kind is detective: a rule that inspects what exists and reports what breaks policy, such as a storage bucket that has become public. You want both, since some rules cannot be expressed as a denied API call. Chapter 36 covers writing and testing policies as code.

Central logging and security

Audit logs are only evidence if the person being investigated could not have edited them. So the record of every API call in every account (Chapter 6 introduced CloudTrail) is delivered to a bucket in a separate log-archive account.

$ aws cloudtrail create-trail --name org-trail --s3-bucket-name example-org-audit-logs \
    --is-organization-trail --is-multi-region-trail --enable-log-file-validation

The same pattern, one delegated account that sees all the others, is used for the rest of the security tooling: threat detection, the inventory of resource configurations, and the findings dashboard all report into security-tooling. The security team gets read-only roles into every account from there, and workload teams cannot disable any of it. Backups follow the same logic: copies go to a vault in a different account, so that whoever can delete the database cannot also delete its backups (Chapter 32).

    🗑️
    Use case: Code Spaces, a company deleted in twelve hours

    Code Spaces was a small company that hosted source code repositories for paying customers, and it ran entirely on AWS, in one account. In June 2014 someone obtained access to the company's AWS console and left a message asking for a large payment. When the staff tried to take the account back by changing passwords, they found the intruder had already created extra logins for themselves, and the intruder responded by deleting things: virtual machines, disk volumes, snapshots, storage buckets and machine images. The backups were in the same account as the systems they backed up, so the backups went too. Within about twelve hours most of the company's data, most of its backups and its machine configurations were gone. Code Spaces announced that it could not recover and stopped trading. Nothing about the attack was sophisticated, and no cloud safeguard failed. The design simply had one boundary, and everything the company owned was inside it. With the structure in this chapter the same stolen console login would have opened one workload account. The backups would have sat in a vault in another account that the login had no role in. The intruder's activity would have been written to a log archive they could not reach, and a guardrail would have refused the new IAM users they created to keep their access.

    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Workloads in the management account. Guardrails do not apply there, and it holds the keys to every other account.
    • One account for everything, or one for all of production. A single mistake reaches all of it.
    • Backups and logs in the same account as the thing they protect.
    • Thinking an SCP grants access. It only limits. People wait for permissions that were never given by an IAM policy.
    • Attaching an untested deny at the root. Every account is locked out together. Try it on a sandbox OU first.
    • Forgetting global services in a region restriction, which breaks sign-in, DNS and billing.
    • OUs that copy the org chart. Group accounts by the rules they need.
    • Accounts created by hand. Each one differs slightly, and the differences are where incidents live.
    • An IAM user per person per account. Use single sign-on and roles.
    • Root users with no multi-factor authentication and a mailbox nobody reads. Every account has one; lock them all down.
    • No budget on sandbox accounts. A forgotten experiment is found on the invoice.
    🎤
    Interview drill

    Explain it simply: What is a service control policy, and how is it different from an IAM policy?

    How a strong answer sounds
    An IAM policy is attached to a user or role and grants permissions inside an account. A service control policy is attached to an organisation's root, an OU or an account, and sets the maximum that anyone in those accounts can be granted. It never grants anything by itself: a request has to pass the SCPs and also be allowed by an IAM policy. It applies to every principal in the account, including administrators and the root user, and the account's own administrators cannot change it, which is what makes it a guardrail. Inheritance works by intersection: the action must be allowed at every level down the tree, and an explicit deny at any level wins. It does not apply to the management account. Typical uses are restricting regions, protecting the audit trail and stopping accounts from leaving the organisation.

    Deeper: A forty-engineer company runs everything in one AWS account. How would you move it to a multi-account structure?

    How a strong answer sounds
    I would build the destination first and move workloads into it gradually, because nobody gets a quiet month to do a migration. Step one is an organisation with a new, empty management account, with the existing account invited in as a member so that nothing running has to move yet. Then the foundations: single sign-on from the company's identity provider with roles in place of IAM users, a log-archive account receiving an organisation-wide audit trail, a security-tooling account, and a short set of deny-list guardrails tested on a sandbox OU before going on the root: approved regions, protected audit trail, no leaving the organisation. Next I would write account vending as Terraform, so that a new account arrives with its baseline, budget and access. Now the workloads. New services go into new accounts from day one, which stops the problem growing. Existing ones move in order of value and ease: stateless services first, because moving them is a redeploy to a new account behind the same DNS name; data stores last, using the same replicate, cut over and keep-a-way-back approach as any database migration. Development and staging move before production, to rehearse. Early on I would also move backups to a vault in a separate account, since that is the cheapest large reduction in risk. The old account shrinks until it holds only the things that are too awkward to move, and it gets the production guardrails. I would be honest about cost: cross-account networking, more pipelines and a platform person's time for some months. What the company gets is that one stolen credential or one bad script can no longer reach everything.

    🧠 Key takeaways

    • The account is the strongest boundary for access, blast radius, limits and cost. Use one per workload per environment.
    • The management account creates and governs the rest. Keep workloads and people out of it.
    • OUs group accounts by the rules they need: security, infrastructure, production, development, sandbox.
    • An SCP limits and never grants. It binds administrators and root users, and not the management account.
    • An action must be allowed at every level of the tree, and an explicit deny anywhere wins.
    • Audit logs and backups go to accounts that workload administrators cannot enter.
    • Account vending turns a new, compliant account into a pull request.

    Check your understanding

    An SCP attached to an OU allows all actions. A developer in an account under that OU has no IAM policy allowing s3:PutObject. Can the developer upload a file?

    SCPs set an outer limit. The request must pass the SCPs and be allowed by an identity or resource policy.

    Why are audit logs delivered to a separate log-archive account?

    Evidence must be out of reach of the people it describes. An account boundary achieves that without relying on a policy inside the workload account.

    The root of an organisation has an SCP that denies launching resources outside two regions. An account's own administrator wants a VM in a third region. What can they do inside their account to allow it?

    SCPs bind administrators and root users alike, and can be changed only from the management account. That is what makes them guardrails.

    Why should no workloads run in the organisation's management account?

    It is exempt from guardrails and holds the most power. The fewer people and programs that can act in it, the better.

    A sandbox OU has FullAWSAccess removed and a policy attached that allows only compute, storage and logging services. An administrator in a sandbox account tries to create a managed database. What happens?

    This is the allow-list strategy. Nothing denies the database explicitly; no policy at that level allows it, which is enough.
