Case Study: AWS us-east-1, December 2021

On 7 December 2021 the largest cloud region in the world had a bad day, and a strange one. Virtual machines in us-east-1 kept running. Storage kept answering. Yet for about seven hours companies could not launch servers, sign in to the console, or change a DNS record, delivery drivers could not scan parcels, and video doorbells went blank, including for customers who believed they had nothing in that region. The fault was in a part of AWS that customers never see: an internal network that the provider's own management systems run on. This case study follows AWS's published summary. It is the clearest real illustration of two ideas that earlier chapters stated in the abstract: the difference between a control plane and a data plane, and the danger of a hidden dependency on one place. The simulator asks what your own architecture would have done that morning.

  • Describe what failed inside AWS, and why customers' running servers were unaffected
  • Explain how retries turned a brief surge into hours of congestion
  • Separate control-plane operations from data-plane operations for common services
  • Name the "global" services whose control planes live in one region
  • Design recovery that needs no control-plane call
  • Audit an architecture for hidden dependencies on a single region

What failed

AWS runs two networks in each region. The main network carries customers' traffic: your instances, your load balancers, your requests to storage. A separate internal network hosts the systems AWS uses to run the region: monitoring, internal DNS, authorisation services and parts of the machinery that launches and manages resources. The two are joined by a set of networking devices that pass traffic between them and translate addresses.

At about 7:30 in the morning, Pacific time, an automated process began adding capacity to one of the AWS services in the main network. It was routine. On this occasion it caused a large number of clients inside the internal network to behave in an unexpected way, and together they produced a surge of new connections to the devices between the two networks. The devices were overwhelmed. Communication between the networks became slow and lossy.

The detail that made the day long is the sixth line. The congestion cut the operations teams off from their own real-time monitoring, which lived in the internal network. They had to work out what was happening from logs, cautiously, because the affected devices were carrying whatever traffic was still getting through and a wrong move could make things worse. Moving the internal DNS traffic helped considerably after about two hours. The devices did not fully recover until 2:22 in the afternoon, and some services took a few hours more to catch up.

Why it spread

Two mechanisms turned a surge into an outage, and both are general.

Retries kept the overload alive. When requests across the boundary began to fail, the clients tried again. Well-behaved clients wait longer after each failure, with some randomness, so that load falls when a system is struggling. AWS's summary says its clients had backoff behaviour, and that a latent fault prevented them from backing off adequately in this situation. So each failure produced more attempts, more attempts produced more congestion, and the congestion produced more failures. Such a loop can keep a system overloaded long after its original cause has gone, a state researchers call a metastable failure. The only ways out are to reduce the load from outside or to add capacity, and both were hard to do blind.

Everything shared the same foundation. The internal network was a dependency of a great many services at once, because the things on it, DNS, authorisation and monitoring, are what all the other services use. This is the same pattern as the network plugin in Chapter 43, at the scale of a cloud region. It reached even the tools for telling customers what was happening. The public Service Health Dashboard could not be updated for some time, because the system used to post to it depended on the affected network, and the support centre was impaired in the same way. For a while the page that customers were told to check showed that all was well.

Control plane vs data plane

The most instructive thing about the day is the pattern of what broke and what did not. It follows one line exactly.

  • A service's data plane does its everyday work: an instance runs, a load balancer forwards a request, a DNS server answers a query, a database returns a row.
  • Its control plane changes things: launch an instance, create a load balancer, edit a DNS record, attach a role.

Providers build data planes to keep working without their control planes, and that held. A system that was simply running in us-east-1, with enough capacity already in place, largely sailed through. Systems that needed to change something did not: those scaling up for the morning's traffic, replacing a failed container, or failing over by editing a record.

That explains customers in us-east-1. It does not explain customers elsewhere, and for them the lesson is about the word global. Several AWS services present themselves as having no region: identity management, DNS, the content delivery network, the organisation of accounts. Their data planes really are spread around the world. Their control planes are each hosted in one region, and for most of them that region is us-east-1. The same has long been true of the original, "global" endpoint of the token service, sts.amazonaws.com, which is why AWS recommends the regional endpoints. So a company running entirely in Europe could be affected that day if any part of its path, creating a role, changing a DNS record, signing people in, or fetching a token from the global endpoint, quietly led to Virginia.

# Does our code call the global token endpoint? (It is served from us-east-1.)
$ grep -rn "sts.amazonaws.com" services/ infrastructure/
services/exporter/config.yaml:14:  sts_endpoint: https://sts.amazonaws.com

# Use the regional endpoints instead. One setting, for every SDK and the CLI:
$ export AWS_STS_REGIONAL_ENDPOINTS=regional
$ aws sts get-caller-identity --region eu-west-1 --debug 2>&1 | grep -o "sts\.[a-z0-9.-]*amazonaws.com" | head -1
sts.eu-west-1.amazonaws.com

Lessons for your architecture

None of the lessons is "avoid us-east-1" or "avoid AWS". Every provider has had an incident of this shape. The lessons are about what your recovery depends on.

One caution against over-learning. Going multi-region is not the main lesson, and for many systems it is not worth the cost (Chapter 33). A single-region system with its capacity already provisioned did well that day. A two-region system whose failover needed a DNS API call did badly. What mattered was whether surviving required changing anything.

    Things to try.

    • The starting system is a common one: one region, autoscaling for the peak, a script for DNS, and credentials from the global endpoint. It is down, and the cause is the last of those, not the one people expect.
    • Switch to the regional token endpoint. Now it is degraded: existing instances serve, and the launches the morning peak needed all fail. Switch capacity to "already running" and it is up, in a single region, for all seven hours.
    • Choose two regions, API Gateway, and the DNS script. Degraded, with a healthy second region that cannot be given the traffic. Change steering to health checks: up within a minute or two, and no API was called.
    • Two regions, a load balancer, autoscaling and health checks: still degraded. The region is short of capacity but alive, so its health checks pass. A second region does not replace having enough capacity in the first.
    • Move the workload to eu-west-1 only, with the global token endpoint. Down. Four of the five rows say "n/a" and the fifth is enough.
    • Of the 48 combinations, 14 stay up, 6 are degraded and 28 go down. Of those 28, 24 are every combination that uses the global endpoint, in all three locations. The other 4 are a single region behind API Gateway.

    Use case: Kinesis, a year earlier, and the dashboard that could not say so

    The 2021 incident had a rehearsal. On 25 November 2020, the day before the American Thanksgiving holiday, the same region suffered a long outage that began in a single service, Kinesis, which carries streams of data. Engineers had added a modest amount of capacity to the fleet of servers at the front of the service. Each of those servers keeps a thread open for every other server in the fleet, so adding servers increased the thread count on all of them, and the addition pushed every server over a limit set in the operating system's configuration. They failed together. Bringing the fleet back took most of the day, because the servers had to be restarted slowly to avoid a second collapse. The outage did not stay in Kinesis. Other AWS services were built on it: the sign-in service used it for analytics and, through a fault in how it handled the failure, began refusing logins; the monitoring service used it to ingest metrics, so alarms and autoscaling stopped working; and services that depended on those were affected in turn. And, as would happen again a year later, AWS could not post updates to its Service Health Dashboard at first, because the tool for doing so depended on the sign-in service that was down. AWS's write-up listed the fixes: larger servers so that fewer were needed, alarms on thread counts, isolating parts of the fleet from each other, and removing the dashboard's dependency. The value of reading the two incidents together is the pattern. Routine capacity changes were the trigger both times. A shared internal dependency carried the failure outwards both times. And both times the system for announcing the problem turned out to stand on the thing that had failed.

    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • A recovery plan whose first step is a control-plane call: launch, scale, create, update.
    • Running close to capacity and relying on autoscaling to save you during an incident.
    • Failover by editing DNS records when health-check failover would work without any API.
    • Assuming a "global" service has no home. Its control plane does.
    • Calling the global token endpoint from workloads in other regions.
    • Believing a workload outside us-east-1 has no dependency on it, without checking your suppliers and tools.
    • Trusting the provider's status page as your monitor. It may be the last to know, or unable to say.
    • Your own status page, alerting or runbooks hosted on the infrastructure they report on.
    • Clients that retry immediately and for ever. They turn a blip into an outage, for you and for whoever you call.
    • Concluding "go multi-region" or "leave the cloud" when the cheaper fix is to stop needing changes during a failure.

    Interview drill

    Explain it simply: What is the difference between a control plane and a data plane, and why does it matter in an outage?

    How a strong answer sounds
    The data plane is the part of a service that does the work users depend on moment to moment: serving requests from running instances, forwarding traffic through a load balancer, answering DNS queries. The control plane is the part that makes changes: launching instances, creating load balancers, editing DNS records, changing permissions. Providers design data planes to keep running when control planes fail, and control planes are more complex and fail more often, especially in large incidents when everyone is trying to make changes at once. In AWS's us-east-1 outage in December 2021, running instances were fine, but nothing could be launched and records could not be changed for hours. So it matters for design: a system survives a control-plane outage if it does not need to change anything to keep working. That means capacity already provisioned, failover driven by health checks, and no API calls in the recovery path.

    Deeper: Your company runs only in eu-west-1. During a us-east-1 outage, parts of your product break anyway. How could that happen, and how would you find the causes beforehand?

    How a strong answer sounds
    There are three kinds of route by which that happens. The first is the provider's own global services. Identity management, DNS management, the CDN's configuration and account organisation have control planes hosted in us-east-1, so creating a role, changing a record or updating a distribution fails even though our data plane in Europe is fine. The global token endpoint is the sharpest case: if our services or tools request credentials from it and not from the regional endpoint, they lose access to everything once cached credentials expire. The second route is our suppliers. Our CI system, container registry, identity provider, feature-flag service, paging tool, payment or email provider may run in us-east-1, and each can take away something we need: the ability to deploy, to sign in, to be alerted, to complete a purchase. The third is our own design choices that span regions: an artefact bucket or a secrets store created in us-east-1 years ago, a third-party webhook, replication to a bucket there. To find them beforehand I would do it as an audit with evidence, not from memory. Search the code and infrastructure definitions for region names and global endpoints. Read a sample of DNS resolutions and outbound connections from production to see where traffic really goes. Ask each supplier where they host and what their own dependencies are. Then test it: in a game day, block access to us-east-1 endpoints from a staging environment and see what breaks, which always finds something the audit missed. For each dependency found I would decide whether to remove it, for example by switching to regional endpoints, to tolerate it, by caching or degrading gracefully, or to accept it knowingly and write down what we cannot do during such an event.

    🧠 Key takeaways

    • The December 2021 outage was congestion on AWS's internal network, started by routine scaling and sustained by retries.
    • Data planes kept working: running instances served. Control planes did not: nothing could be launched or changed.
    • Shared internal foundations spread a failure to many services, and to the tools for monitoring and announcing it.
    • "Global" services have control planes with a home region, usually us-east-1.
    • Survive by not needing change: capacity in place, failover by health check, regional endpoints.
    • Audit your suppliers and tools for where they run. Monitor and communicate from outside.
    • Clients that back off properly prevent a surge from becoming a standing overload.

    Health checks and query answering are data plane and kept working. The other three need control-plane calls that were failing.

    A status page that shares a dependency with the thing it reports on goes quiet exactly when it is needed. Host yours elsewhere.
