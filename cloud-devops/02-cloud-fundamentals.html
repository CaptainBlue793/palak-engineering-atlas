Cloud Fundamentals: Regions, Zones & Services

A cloud provider rents out computers, storage and networks that you control through an API and pay for by the hour or the gigabyte. Before any single service makes sense you need four ideas that apply to all of them: where things physically run (regions and availability zones), how much of the stack you manage (IaaS, PaaS, SaaS), which security work stays yours whatever you rent (shared responsibility), and what the meter counts (pricing). The examples use AWS names, with the Azure and Google Cloud equivalents alongside, because the ideas are the same on all three.

What makes it "cloud"

A rented server in someone else's data centre is not new. What changed is how you get one. The usual definition, from the US standards body NIST, lists five properties: you serve yourself without talking to a person (on-demand self-service), over the network (broad network access), from hardware shared with other customers (resource pooling), in amounts you can raise and lower in minutes (rapid elasticity), and you pay for what the meter records (measured service).

For an operator the important consequence is that infrastructure becomes an API. Creating a server is an HTTP request that returns in seconds, so it can be put in a script, reviewed like code and repeated exactly. Everything from Terraform to Kubernetes is built on that fact. The second consequence is financial: you stop buying hardware for the peak three years ahead and instead pay each month for what ran. That is cheaper when load varies and can be dearer when it does not.

Regions and availability zones

A provider's hardware is organised in three levels, and each level is a unit of failure.

  • A region is a geographic area, such as London (eu-west-2 on AWS) or Northern Virginia (us-east-1). Regions are separate from each other on purpose: separate API endpoints, separate capacity, separate copies of most services. Data does not leave a region unless you send it. The large providers each have dozens.
  • An availability zone (AZ) is one or more data centres inside a region with their own power, cooling and network, placed kilometres apart from the region's other zones but close enough for a round trip of a millisecond or two. A region normally has three or more. A fire, a flood or a failed power feed should take out one zone and leave the others running.
  • Edge locations (points of presence) are small sites in many more cities that cache content and terminate connections close to users. They hold copies, not your servers or databases.

You choose the region for every resource you create, and for many resources the zone as well.

Look at the middle column. On AWS the letter in a zone name is assigned per account: your eu-west-2a and another account's eu-west-2a may be different buildings, so that customers do not all crowd into "a". The zone ID (euw2-az2) names the physical zone and is what you compare across accounts.

Services differ in how much of this they handle for you, and it helps to sort them by the scope in which one copy lives.

Things to try.

  • The starting layout is two instances in one zone: $60.74 a month and no spare capacity. Lose an instance and the survivor can serve 150 of the 300 requests a second. Lose the zone and it serves nothing.
  • Add instances to the same zone. With three or four, the loss of an instance goes unnoticed, and the loss of the zone is still a complete outage. More copies in one building protect against a machine failing and nothing bigger.
  • Spread the original two over two zones, one each. The price is the same $60.74, and a zone failure now leaves 150 of 300 instead of zero. That is better, but the service is still overloaded: two zones with exactly enough capacity is the most common half-finished design.
  • Make it survive a zone. Two zones need two instances each: 4 instances, $121.47, twice the capacity the service uses. Three zones need one each: 3 instances, $91.10, half as much again. Three zones is the cheapest layout here that survives a zone, by $30.37 a month, and the saving grows with the size of the service.
  • Fail the region. None of the twelve single-region layouts survives, however many zones and instances you buy. The standby copy does, and it doubles the bill: the three-zone layout goes from $91.10 to $182.21, for hardware that serves nothing on a normal day. That price, and the work of keeping data in two regions, is why most services stop at several zones; Chapter 32 covers the cheaper forms of standby.

Shared responsibility

Moving to a cloud does not move all the security work to the provider. AWS's wording is the easiest to remember: the provider is responsible for security of the cloud, and the customer for security in the cloud. The provider guards the buildings, replaces failed disks, patches the hypervisor and keeps one customer's virtual machine from reading another's memory. What you build on top, and how you configure it, is yours.

The bottom three rows never move. Identities, exposure and data are the customer's at every level, and they are where cloud breaches happen: a storage bucket made public, an access key committed to a repository, a database port open to the world. The provider's durability figures do not cover them either. Object storage that keeps eleven nines of durability will store your accidental deletion just as durably, and replicate it to every zone.

Pricing basics: four meters

A cloud bill has thousands of line items, but nearly all of them come from four meters.

  • Compute time. Per second or per hour that a machine, container or function exists, whether or not it is busy. A stopped virtual machine stops the compute charge; its disk keeps charging.
  • Storage. Per gigabyte per month, by storage class. You pay for what you have provisioned on block volumes and for what you have stored in object storage.
  • Data transfer. Data coming in from the internet is normally free. Data going out to the internet is charged per gigabyte, and so is data crossing between zones and between regions. This is the meter people forget.
  • Requests and managed-service fees. Per million API calls, per load-balancer hour, per gigabyte passing through a NAT gateway, per query.

Compute also comes at three prices. On demand is the list price with no commitment. A commitment of one or three years (reserved instances, savings plans, committed-use discounts) cuts it by a third to two thirds. Spot or preemptible capacity is the provider's spare hardware at a discount of up to about 90%, which it can take back at short notice. Use case: the Strasbourg fire and whose job the backups were

In the early hours of 10 March 2021 a fire destroyed SBG2, one of the data-centre buildings on OVHcloud's site in Strasbourg, and damaged part of a second; the remaining buildings were switched off. Netcraft counted about 3.6 million websites on some 464,000 domains knocked offline. Most came back when the surviving buildings were powered up again, but servers in SBG2 were gone for good, and some customers found that the only backups of those servers had been stored on the same site. Customers who had copies in another location, whether their own or a paid service that placed them elsewhere, restored within days. The event shows both halves of this chapter. A data centre is a building, and a building can be lost in one night, which is why zones are kilometres apart and why "all our servers are in the cloud" says nothing about whether they share a roof. And the provider keeping the hardware running was never the same promise as the customer's data existing somewhere else: that copy was the customer's to arrange.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Everything in one zone. Ten instances in one zone survive the loss of an instance and nothing larger.
  • Two zones with exactly enough capacity. When one zone fails, the other receives double the load. Size each zone so that the survivors can carry everything.
  • Planning to scale up during the failure. Thousands of customers ask for instances in the surviving zones at the same moment, and the APIs you would use may be affected too. Capacity that is already running is the only kind you can count on.
  • Treating a multi-zone design as multi-region. Zones protect against a building failing; they do nothing when a region-wide service fails.
  • Assuming the provider backs up your data. Durable storage keeps what you wrote, including mistakes. Backups, and a copy somewhere else, are yours to set up.
  • Forgetting data transfer. Internet egress, cross-zone chatter and NAT gateway traffic can exceed the cost of the servers.
  • Choosing a region by price alone, then discovering the latency, a data-residency rule or a missing service.
  • Leaving things running. Nothing in the cloud stops charging because you stopped using it.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is the difference between a region and an availability zone?

How a strong answer sounds
A region is a geographic area, such as London, that is isolated from the provider's other regions: its own endpoints, its own capacity, and data stays in it unless I move it. An availability zone is one or more data centres inside a region with independent power, cooling and networking, a few kilometres from the other zones and connected to them by fast private links. Zones exist so that a local event such as a fire or a power failure takes out part of a region, not all of it. So I use several zones in one region for high availability, with a load balancer across them, and I use a second region for disaster recovery, for data-residency reasons or to be closer to users elsewhere. Latency between zones is a millisecond or two, so a database can replicate synchronously across them; between regions it is tens of milliseconds, so replication is usually asynchronous.

Deeper: A service runs on two instances in one zone and needs both to carry its peak. The business wants it to survive the loss of a data centre at the lowest cost. What do you change, and what do you check?

How a strong answer sounds
Two instances in two zones is not enough: after a zone fails, one instance faces the load of two. I need the survivors to hold full capacity, so the cheapest layout is three instances across three zones, one each. Losing a zone leaves two, which is what the peak needs, and I pay for 50% more than I use. Two zones would need two instances in each, which is 100% more. Then I check everything that is not the instances. The load balancer must be enabled in all three zones with health checks that remove dead targets quickly. The data must survive too: a managed database with a standby in another zone, and no state kept on an instance's own disk. I would keep the spare capacity running and not rely on autoscaling to add it during the failure, because that is when capacity in the surviving zones is hardest to get. I would look for single-zone dependencies I did not create on purpose, such as one NAT gateway, and note that traffic between zones is charged. Finally I would test it: take one zone's instances out of service at peak and watch the error rate.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Regions are isolated geographic areas; availability zones are separate data centres inside a region; edge locations only cache.
  • To survive losing one of n zones, the other n − 1 must hold the full load: 100% spare with two zones, 50% with three.
  • Zonal resources are yours to spread; regional services are spread for you; global services often have one home region.
  • IaaS, PaaS and SaaS differ in how much you operate. Rent the highest level that meets the requirement.
  • The provider secures the cloud; you secure what you put in it. Identities, network exposure and data are always yours.
  • Four meters: compute time, storage, data transfer, and requests or service fees. Data out and NAT are the ones that surprise.

Check your understanding

A service runs on four instances, all in us-east-1a. That zone loses power. What happens?

A virtual machine is a zonal resource. More instances in the same zone protect against one instance failing, not against the zone failing, and nothing moves them for you.

A service needs four instances at peak. It is spread evenly over three zones and must keep full capacity if one zone is lost. How many instances must run in total?

The two surviving zones must hold four instances, so each zone needs two: six in total, 50% more than the peak needs. With two zones it would be eight.

A company's customer records leak because a storage bucket was configured to allow public reads. Under the shared-responsibility model, whose failure is this?

The provider secures the service itself. Who may read a bucket is configuration, and identities, exposure and data stay with the customer at every service level.

You hand a platform your application's code, and it builds it, runs it, patches the operating system and adds copies under load. Which service model is this?

In PaaS you bring the code and the data and the provider runs everything below. In IaaS you would patch the OS yourself; in SaaS the application is the provider's too.

Which of these is normally free on the large cloud providers?

Inbound data is free. Outbound data is charged per gigabyte, a stopped machine's volume still bills for storage, and cross-zone traffic is charged in both directions.
