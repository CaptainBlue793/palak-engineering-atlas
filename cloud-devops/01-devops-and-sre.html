DevOps & SRE

DevOps is not a tool and not really a job title. It is an answer to one question: how does a team change a running system often without breaking it? This chapter explains the problem that the DevOps movement set out to fix, how Google's Site Reliability Engineering turns the same idea into rules with numbers, and the four DORA metrics that tell you whether a team is good at delivery. A simulator lets you run twelve weeks of releases under different habits and read the result. The chapter ends with what a DevOps or SRE interview really tests, and where this course covers each part.

The problem: two teams with opposite goals

For a long time, most companies split software work between two departments. Development wrote the code and was judged on features delivered. Operations ran the servers and was judged on uptime. Every change is a risk to uptime, so the two goals pull against each other: one team is paid to change the system and the other to keep it the same.

The usual result looked like this. Developers finished work for months and then handed a large release to operations, often with a document of manual steps. Operations, who had not seen the code before, installed it late at night on servers that had been configured by hand and differed from the developers' machines. When it broke, each side blamed the other. Because releases hurt, the company did them less often, which made each one larger and more likely to hurt. That loop is the thing DevOps was invented to break.

Two events in 2009 gave the idea a shape and a name. At the Velocity conference, John Allspaw and Paul Hammond of Flickr gave a talk called "10+ Deploys per Day: Dev and Ops Cooperation at Flickr", at a time when most companies deployed a few times a year. Later that year Patrick Debois organised the first DevOpsDays in Ghent, and the name stuck.

DevOps as a practice

DevOps means that the people who build a service and the people who run it share one goal and one process, with as much of that process automated as possible. Werner Vogels, Amazon's CTO, put the ownership part in six words in 2006: "You build it, you run it." In practice the idea turns into a small set of habits, and the rest of this course is those habits one at a time:

SRE: operations treated as a software problem

Site Reliability Engineering started at Google in 2003, when Ben Treynor Sloss was asked to run a production team and staffed it with software engineers. His description is the shortest definition there is: SRE is what happens when you ask a software engineer to design an operations team. Google later described the relationship to DevOps with a line of code: class SRE implements DevOps. DevOps says what to aim for; SRE is one specific, written-down way of doing it. Its main rules are these.

  • Reliability gets a number. A service has a service level objective (SLO): a target such as "99.9% of requests succeed, measured over 28 days". Not 100%, because 100% is impossible to reach and nobody's phone or network is that reliable anyway.
  • The gap is a budget. If the target is 99.9%, the service is allowed to fail 0.1% of the time. That allowance is the error budget, and it is there to be spent on releases, experiments and maintenance.
  • The budget decides the argument. While budget remains, the team ships. When it is used up, feature releases stop and the team works on reliability until the service is back inside its target. Development and operations no longer argue from opinion about whether a release is "too risky": they read the same number.
  • Toil has a cap. Toil is manual, repetitive operational work that grows with the size of the service. Google's rule is that an SRE spends at most half their time on it; the rest goes to engineering that removes the toil.
  • Postmortems are blameless. After an incident the write-up asks what in the system allowed it, not who did it, because people who fear blame hide the facts you need.

The arithmetic of an error budget is one multiplication. Over 28 days there are 28 × 24 × 60 = 40,320 minutes, so a 99.9% target leaves 40,320 × 0.001 = 40.3 minutes in which the service may be failing.

DORA metrics: four numbers that describe delivery

How do you know whether a team is good at delivering software? The best-known answer comes from DORA (DevOps Research and Assessment), a research programme started by Nicole Forsgren, Jez Humble and Gene Kim that has surveyed tens of thousands of engineers in its yearly State of DevOps reports and is now part of Google Cloud. Their book Accelerate (2018) reduced delivery performance to four measurements. Two measure speed and two measure stability.

MetricWhat it measuresHow to measure itStrongWeak
Deployment frequencyHow often you release to productionCount production deployments per day or weekOn demand, several a dayLess than once a month
Lead time for changesHow long a commit waits before users have itDeploy time minus commit time, medianUnder a dayMore than a month
Change failure rateThe share of deployments that cause a failure needing a rollback, a hotfix or a patchFailed deployments ÷ all deploymentsAbout 5%Tens of percent
Time to restore (now "failed deployment recovery time")How long users are affected when a deployment failsRecovery time minus failure start, medianUnder an hourDays or longer

The "strong" and "weak" columns are rough: the reports redraw the bands every year, and later editions add a fifth measure. The finding that has held up year after year is the important one: speed and stability are not a trade-off. The teams that deploy most often are also the ones with the lowest failure rates and the quickest recoveries. That contradicts the instinct of the old operations department, which was to make releases rarer and add approvals. The simulator below shows why the instinct is wrong.

Two cautions. Measure per service, not per person, because these are properties of a process. And do not make any of them a target on its own: a team told to raise deployment frequency can deploy empty changes all day.

All four can be read from one file that your pipeline already could write: a line per production deployment.

# deployments.jsonl: the pipeline appends one line per production deployment
{"sha":"9f2c1ab","deployed_at":"2026-09-28T10:42:07Z","status":"ok"}
{"sha":"41d07e3","deployed_at":"2026-09-28T15:03:51Z","status":"failed","restored_at":"2026-09-28T15:21:40Z"}

# Deployment frequency: deployments per day
$ jq -r '.deployed_at[:10]' deployments.jsonl | sort | uniq -c

# Change failure rate
$ jq -s '(map(select(.status == "failed")) | length) as $bad
         | {deploys: length, failed: $bad, cfr_pct: (100 * $bad / length | round)}' deployments.jsonl

# Time to restore, in minutes, for each failed deployment
$ jq -r 'select(.status == "failed")
         | ((.restored_at | fromdate) - (.deployed_at | fromdate)) / 60 | floor' deployments.jsonl

# Lead time for changes: commit time (from Git) to deploy time, median in minutes
jq -r '[.sha, .deployed_at] | @tsv' deployments.jsonl |
while read -r sha at; do
  commit="$(git show -s --format=%ct "$sha")"      # committer time, Unix seconds
  deploy="$(date -u -d "$at" +%s)"                # GNU date
  echo $(( (deploy - commit) / 60 ))
done | sort -n | awk '{ a[NR] = $1 } END { print a[int((NR + 1) / 2)] }'

The lead-time script measures from the commit that was deployed. A deployment that carries forty commits has forty lead times, the oldest of them much longer; a real tool walks every commit between two deployed versions.

Simulator: twelve weeks of releases

One team merges four changes every working day for twelve weeks: 240 changes. A small share of them carry a defect that the team's checks do not catch. You choose how the team releases, how it tests, who approves a release and what it does when a release fails. The simulator replays the same twelve weeks under your choices and reports the four DORA metrics and how much of a 99.9% error budget the team's users paid for.

The model: changes merge at 10:00, 12:00, 14:00 and 16:00 on working days. A change escapes with a defect 5% of the time without automated tests and 1.5% with them. Scheduled releases are cut on Wednesday at 17:00 (daily ones every working day at 17:00) and the pipeline takes 30 minutes. The change board meets on Thursday at 14:00 and a release waits for it. A failure is noticed after 15 minutes. Finding a defect takes 30 minutes × (1 + log₂ of the number of changes in the release) and fixing it another hour; a rollback takes 5 minutes. Every failed release counts as bad time for all users until it is restored, which is harsher than real life: Chapter 17 shows how a canary limits the damage to a few of them.

Things to try. The numbers are from the first twelve weeks, the run the panel opens with.

  • The starting point is the old way: a release every four weeks, no automated tests, a change board, and debugging in production. Three releases carry up to 80 changes each and all three fail, because 13 defects are spread across them. The median failure lasts 23.7 hours and users get 20.8 hours of bad service per 28 days: 31 times the budget. Notice that 23.7 hours still counts as "High" on the DORA scale, which only compares teams with each other. The error budget is the stricter judge, because it is what users were promised.
  • Change only the approval to peer review. Lead time falls from 14.1 to 13.2 days and nothing else moves: the same three releases fail. The board added a day of waiting and caught nothing, which is what the DORA research found about outside approvals. Now put the board back and choose "Every merge": the board turns it into one release a week of up to 20 changes.
  • Shrink the release, still debugging in production (peer review, no tests). Four-weekly: 31 times over budget. Weekly: 27 times. Daily: 21 times. Every merge: 14.5 times, with a median failure of 2.3 hours instead of 23.7. The same 13 defects reach production every time; what changes is that a failed release of one change points straight at its cause. The change failure rate falls from 100% to 5%.
  • Add automated tests. The defects that reach production fall from 13 to 3, and with a release on every merge the failure rate is 1%. Bad time is still 135 minutes per 28 days, 3.3 times the budget, because each of the three failures is debugged for over two hours in front of users.
  • Roll back first. Each failure now costs users 20 minutes whatever its cause: 20 minutes per 28 days, half the budget. All four metrics are in the top band: 4 deployments a day, 30 minutes of lead time, a 1% failure rate and 20 minutes to restore. Over 40 other random twelve-week runs, these habits stayed inside the budget in 37.
  • One result that may surprise you. Go back to four-weekly releases and keep the rollback: bad time is low as well (13 minutes per 28 days with tests), because defects that arrive together cause one incident. The price is paid elsewhere. Two of the three releases fail, each rollback withdraws up to 80 changes to remove one or two bad ones, and the median change waits 13.5 days. The model is also generous here: it lets a release of 80 changes roll back as easily as a release of one. Real large releases, with their schema changes and several services updated together, are exactly the ones that cannot be rolled back cleanly, which is how teams end up debugging in production.

DevOps engineer, SRE, platform engineer

DevOps began as a way of working, but it is now also three job titles, and job adverts use them loosely. What differs is what each role is mainly responsible for.

Three habits help in every round. Say what you would check before you say what is wrong: interviewers mark the method, and a correct guess with no method scores badly. Mitigate first: in any "it is broken" question, the first aim is to stop the harm (roll back, shift traffic, scale up), and the cause comes second. Give numbers: "we deployed about ten times a day, with a failure rate near 4%" is a stronger sentence than "we did CI/CD". Chapter 40 works through full questions of each kind.

Use case: Knight Capital and the server that was missed

On 1 August 2012 the trading firm Knight Capital released new order-routing code to its eight production servers. The release was done by hand, and the new code was not copied to one of the eight; nobody reviewed the result. The new code reused a configuration flag that had once switched on an old test feature, and the old feature's code was still present on the eighth server. When the market opened, that server began sending a flood of unintended orders. The system had sent emails warning of a problem before the open, but they were not treated as alerts. During the incident the team removed the new code from the seven healthy servers, which made all eight misbehave. The US Securities and Exchange Commission's order on the case records that it lasted about 45 minutes and cost the firm more than $460 million; the firm was sold within months. Every missing practice in that story is a chapter of this course: an automated, repeatable deployment, a check that every server runs the same version, alerts that reach a person, and a rehearsed rollback.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Calling a team "DevOps" and changing nothing else. If developers still hand releases to another team through tickets, the wall is still there with a new name on it.
  • Thinking DevOps is a list of tools. Kubernetes with a release every quarter is the old process on new machines.
  • Releasing less often to be safer. Rare releases are large, and large releases fail more often and take longer to diagnose.
  • Aiming for 100% availability. It cannot be reached, it leaves no room to release anything, and each extra nine costs far more than the one before.
  • Debugging in production while users are affected. Restore service first; find the cause afterwards.
  • Turning a metric into a target. Deployment frequency can be gamed in a day. Read the four together.
  • Blaming a person in the postmortem. The next person will make the same mistake, and will hide it.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is the difference between DevOps and SRE?

How a strong answer sounds
DevOps is a set of principles: the people who build a service and the people who run it share one goal, work in small changes, automate the path to production and learn from failures without blame. SRE is one concrete way of putting those principles into practice, which came from Google: reliability gets a numeric target, the gap between the target and 100% is an error budget that is spent on releases, manual operational work is capped so engineers have time to automate it, and operations problems are solved with software. So the two do not compete. A company can follow DevOps principles with or without a team called SRE. As roles, a DevOps engineer usually spends more time on pipelines and infrastructure as code, and an SRE more on SLOs, alerting, incidents and capacity.

Deeper: Your team releases once a month through a change board, and about half the releases cause an incident. Management wants to add a second approval step. What do you say?

How a strong answer sounds
I would start from the numbers. A monthly release carries perhaps a hundred changes, so even a low defect rate per change makes a failure of the whole release likely, and when it fails nobody knows which of the hundred changes is to blame. An approver reading a summary of a hundred changes cannot find the defect either, so a second approval adds waiting and does not lower the failure rate; the DORA research found the same for external approval boards. What does help is working on both parts of the cost of a failure. To lower the chance, make releases smaller and more frequent and put automated tests in the pipeline, so each release carries few changes and each change has been checked by a machine. To lower the cost, make rollback one action and practise it, and release to a small share of traffic first. I would propose a trial on one service: weekly releases with a tested rollback, measuring change failure rate and time to restore before and after, and keep peer review of each change as the approval. If the service has an SLO, its error budget gives management the control they want: releases continue while there is budget and stop when it is spent.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • DevOps removes the wall between building and running: one team, one goal, small changes, an automated path to production.
  • SRE is a concrete version of it: SLOs, an error budget that decides when to ship, a cap on toil, blameless postmortems.
  • A 99.9% target over 28 days allows 40.3 minutes of bad service.
  • The four DORA metrics: deployment frequency and lead time for speed, change failure rate and time to restore for stability.
  • Speed and stability improve together: small releases fail less often and are quicker to diagnose.
  • When a release fails, restore service first and investigate afterwards.
  • In interviews, show your method, mitigate before diagnosing, and use numbers.

Check your understanding

A service has an SLO of 99.9% availability over 28 days. Roughly how long may it be unavailable in that window?

28 days is 40,320 minutes, and 0.1% of that is 40.3 minutes. Four minutes is the budget at 99.99%, and about 7 hours is the budget at 99%.

Which pair of DORA metrics measures stability rather than speed?

Deployment frequency and lead time describe how fast changes flow. Change failure rate and time to restore describe how often a change breaks something and how long users are affected.

A team's error budget for the month is used up after a bad release. Under an error-budget policy, what happens next?

The budget is the agreed limit on risk. While it lasts the team ships; when it is spent, effort moves to reliability. Lowering the target to escape the rule, or blaming a person, defeats its purpose.

Why does releasing every change on its own usually give a lower change failure rate than releasing 80 changes together?

With a 1.5% chance per change, a release of one fails 1.5% of the time and a release of 80 fails about 70% of the time (1 − 0.985⁸⁰). The large release is also harder to diagnose.

A deployment at 14:00 sends the error rate up, and the alert fires at 14:15. What should the on-call engineer do first?

Mitigate, then diagnose. A rollback ends the harm to users in minutes; the investigation can then take as long as it needs without an outage running.
