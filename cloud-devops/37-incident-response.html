Incident Response & Postmortems

Everything in this course so far reduces how often things break. This chapter is about the times they break anyway. An incident is the one situation in which a team's speed is limited less by its tools than by its organisation: who decides, who acts, who tells the customers, and what gets done first. Good teams do not improvise these under pressure. They use a small set of practised habits, borrowed largely from firefighters, that turn a crowd of anxious experts into a response. This chapter covers how to classify an incident, the roles people take, why stopping the damage comes before understanding it, how to communicate while it is happening, and how to learn from it afterwards without blaming anyone. The simulator replays one incident under different habits.

  • Classify an incident by severity, from its effect on customers
  • Name the incident roles and say what the incident commander does and does not do
  • Mitigate first: choose the action that stops the harm before finding the cause
  • Write a status update that is honest, useful and on schedule
  • Run a blameless postmortem and write action items that get done
  • See where the minutes of an incident actually go

Severity levels

An incident is any unplanned event that needs an urgent, coordinated response. The first decision is how big it is, because that decides who is woken and how much is dropped. Severity levels make that decision quick and consistent, by defining size in terms of what customers experience.

LevelMeaningExampleResponse
SEV1A core function is unavailable or badly degraded for many customers, or data or security is at riskCheckout is failing; customer data exposedPage immediately, at any hour. Incident commander, full roles, status page, leadership told. Updates every 30 minutes
SEV2An important function is degraded, or a core one for a minoritySearch is slow; one region is failingPage the on-call engineer. Incident commander. Status page
SEV3Minor impact, with a workaroundReport exports failA ticket, handled in working hours
SEV4No customer impact yetA redundant component has failedA ticket

Two rules make the scale work. Classify by impact, not by cause. You know the impact in the first minute and the cause perhaps an hour later. When unsure, go higher. Declaring a SEV1 that turns out to be a SEV2 costs a few people half an hour, and you downgrade it with thanks. Treating a SEV1 as a SEV3 means the right people learn of it from an angry customer. Anyone should be able to declare an incident, and doing so should never be held against them.

Incident roles

In the 1970s, after a series of Californian wildfires in which well-equipped agencies got in each other's way, fire services designed the Incident Command System: a small set of roles with clear authority that any responder can step into. Software teams adopted it almost unchanged, because they had the same problem. Ten skilled engineers in a channel, each investigating and changing things independently, are slower and more dangerous than three with assigned jobs.

RoleDoesDoes not
Incident commanderOwns the response. Assigns roles, keeps one picture of what is known, decides what is tried next and when to escalateDebug or type commands. The moment the commander opens a terminal, nobody is in command
Operations leadWorks on the system: investigates, proposes and carries out changes, with whichever experts are pulled inMake changes without telling the commander
Communications leadWrites the status page and the updates to support, sales and leadership, on a fixed scheduleInterrupt the people fixing it. Takes updates from the commander
ScribeKeeps a timestamped record of what was seen, decided and doneFilter: records wrong turns too. The postmortem depends on it

In a small incident one person may hold several roles, with one exception: whoever is commanding should not also be the one fixing. The roles are jobs for the hour and not ranks. A junior engineer who is commanding directs a principal engineer who is operating. Handovers are said out loud ("Priya, you have command"; "I have command"), because the worst state is two commanders or none.

Mitigate, then diagnose

Engineers are trained to understand a problem before fixing it. During an incident that instinct is expensive. Customers are being harmed now, and the fastest way to stop the harm rarely requires knowing why it started. So the order is reversed: mitigate first, diagnose afterwards. A mitigation is any action that reduces the impact, whether or not it addresses the cause.

Some habits keep a mitigation from becoming a second incident. Make one change at a time, announced to the commander first, so you can tell what helped. Prefer reversible actions. Be wary of the restart: it destroys evidence, and if the cause is a bad release the fault simply restarts with the pod. Put a time limit on each line of investigation, and when it expires say so and try another, since people under stress hold on to their first theory. And if the system depends on you to keep it limping, ask for relief: long incidents are run in shifts.

Communication

An incident has two audiences who are not in the room: customers, and everyone inside the company who is being asked by customers. If they are told nothing they fill the gap themselves, with support tickets, social media posts and direct messages to the engineers who are trying to work. A status page and a regular internal update exist to absorb that pressure.

Investigating  14:06 UTC
Some customers are unable to complete checkout. Browsing and existing orders are not affected.
We are investigating and will update by 14:35.

Identified     14:08 UTC
A change released at 14:00 is causing errors during checkout. We are reverting it.

Monitoring     14:11 UTC
The change has been reverted and checkout is working again. We are watching to confirm.
Orders that failed were not charged; please place them again.

Resolved       14:41 UTC
Checkout has been stable for 30 minutes. We will publish a full review within five working days.

Good updates share a few properties. They say what the customer experiences, in the customer's words, and what still works. They say what the customer should do. They commit to the time of the next update and keep to it, even when the update is "no change". They do not speculate about causes or promise a time for the fix. And they are written by the communications lead, so that the people repairing the system are not also composing prose. A status page hosted on the same infrastructure as the product will be down when it is needed, which is why they are run elsewhere.

Blameless postmortems

When the incident is over the most valuable part begins. A postmortem (also called an incident review) is a written account of what happened and what will change. Its purpose is learning, and learning needs the truth, which people only tell when it is safe to.

Blameless is the working rule that makes it safe. It starts from an assumption: everyone involved acted reasonably given what they knew, the tools they had and the pressure they were under. If an engineer ran a command that took down production, the useful questions are about the system. Why was that command possible? Why did it look safe? What did the tooling show them? "Human error" is where an investigation starts and never where it ends, because the next person in the same position will do the same thing. Blameless does not mean without accountability. It moves accountability from "who did it" to "who will fix the conditions that allowed it".

# Postmortem: checkout errors, 7 October

Summary               Two sentences a newcomer could understand.
Impact                Who was affected, for how long, how badly: 9,000 failed checkouts over 10 minutes.
Timeline              Timestamped, from the scribe's record: what was seen, decided and done.
Detection             How did we find out? How long after it began? Could it have been sooner?
Response              What helped and what slowed us down.
Contributing factors  Several, usually. Not one "root cause". What made it possible, likely and worse?
What went well        Worth protecting: the alert fired, the rollback worked.
Where we got lucky    It was 14:00 on a Tuesday. At 03:00 on a Sunday?
Action items          Each with one owner, a date and a ticket. Few enough to be finished.

The section that decides whether any of it matters is the last one. Action items that are vague ("be more careful", "improve monitoring") or ownerless are not done. Good ones are specific and checkable: "add a canary stage to the checkout pipeline with automatic rollback on error rate; owner Dana; by 21 October". Track them to completion, publish the review to the whole engineering organisation, and read other teams' reviews, since their incident this month is yours next quarter.

    The shop takes 50 checkouts a second, so a 30% failure rate loses 900 a minute. An alert fires after 2 minutes; customer reports reach an engineer after 15. Declaring takes 3 minutes. A SEV3 pages nobody, and 20 minutes pass before the scale is noticed. With nobody in command, two engineers' changes collide, costing 10 minutes. Diagnosing first takes 25 minutes; a restart costs 8 and changes nothing. Without updates, responders lose a quarter of their working time to questions.

    Things to try.

    • The starting team has none of the habits. The bug that a five-minute rollback fixes affects customers for 93 minutes and fails 83,700 checkouts. Read the timeline: 83 of those minutes have nothing to do with the bug.
    • Switch on all five habits. The same bug costs 10 minutes and 9,000 checkouts: two to detect, three to declare, five to roll back.
    • From that best case, remove one habit at a time. Diagnosing before mitigating gives 35 minutes. Calling it a SEV3 gives 30. Waiting for customers to report it gives 23. Having nobody in command gives 20. Restarting first gives 18. Skipping communication gives 12.
    • "Find the root cause first" is the costliest single habit, and the one that feels most like good engineering. The cause was found either way. In one version the customers waited while it was found.
    • Communication looks cheap to skip in the best case, at 2 minutes. Skip it in the starting case and it costs 15, because its cost grows with everything else that is going wrong.
    • Across the 48 combinations the incident lasts between 10 and 93 minutes, with a median of 47. The technical fix is 5 minutes in every one of them.
    🌐
    Use case: Fastly, 8 June 2021, and a forty-nine-minute timeline

    Fastly runs one of the large content delivery networks, and on the morning of 8 June 2021 a large part of the web went with it: news sites, shops, government services and forums returned errors across the world. The company's account of the day is worth reading as a response, not only as a failure. The outage began at 09:47 UTC, and Fastly's monitoring detected it within a minute. The cause was unusual. A software release nearly a month earlier had contained a dormant bug, and that morning one customer made a perfectly valid change to their own configuration that happened to trigger it, causing most of the network to return errors. The engineers did what this chapter recommends: they asked what had changed. Forty minutes in they had identified the customer configuration as the trigger and disabled it, which was a mitigation and not a fix, since the bug was still in the software. Services began recovering at once, and Fastly reported that within 49 minutes of the start, 95% of its network was operating normally. The permanent fix, a corrected release, began rolling out some hours later, after the pressure was off. That same day the company published a plain summary with times, the cause, an apology and the steps it would take, including a review of why the bug was not caught in testing. Detection in one minute, mitigation before repair, and a prompt, specific, public account: the three things were what turned a global outage into a story that mostly ended the same morning.

    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Diagnosing while customers are still affected. Roll back first; the logs will still be there.
    • Under-declaring. "Let's see if it gets worse" is a decision to respond later with fewer people.
    • No one in command, or a commander who is also debugging.
    • Everyone changing things at once, so nobody knows what helped and some changes undo others.
    • Restarting first. It erases evidence and often brings the same fault straight back.
    • Silence. No update is read as "they do not know" or "they do not care", and the questions go to the responders.
    • Promising a time for the fix, or guessing at the cause in public.
    • Heroes. One exhausted expert for eight hours makes worse decisions than two fresh people in shifts.
    • A postmortem that names a person as the cause. The next review will be less honest.
    • Stopping at "human error" or at a single root cause. Incidents have several contributing factors.
    • Action items with no owner or date, or twenty of them. Three finished ones are worth more.
    • Declaring it over the moment the graph recovers, without watching, cleaning up or telling customers.
    🎤
    Interview drill

    Explain it simply: What does an incident commander do?

    How a strong answer sounds
    The incident commander owns the response as a whole. They declare or confirm the severity, assign the other roles, operations, communications, scribe, and keep a single shared picture of what is known and what is being tried. They decide what happens next: approve the rollback, call in another team, escalate, hand over when people tire. Just as important is what they do not do: they do not debug or run commands themselves, because the moment they are heads-down in a terminal nobody is coordinating. It is a role for the duration of the incident and not a rank, so a junior engineer can command senior people. A good commander is calm, asks short questions, repeats decisions back so they are unambiguous, and keeps asking "what is the fastest way to reduce customer impact?" ahead of "why did this happen?".

    Deeper: You are on call. At 02:10 you are paged: the checkout error rate is 30%. Talk me through the next thirty minutes.

    How a strong answer sounds
    I acknowledge the page so it does not escalate, and take about two minutes to confirm it is real: the error-rate graph, whether it is all regions and all users, and whether real orders are failing, not only a health check. Thirty per cent of checkouts failing is a SEV1 by impact, so I declare it straight away, open the incident channel and page for an incident commander and a communications lead. Until they arrive I say in the channel that I am commanding and operating, and I hand command over explicitly when someone joins. My first question is what changed. I check the deploy log and the GitOps history for the last few hours, feature-flag changes and any infrastructure changes. If there was a release shortly before the errors began, I roll it back at once, without waiting to prove it is the cause, because a rollback is quick, reversible and usually right. I say what I am about to do before I do it, and I make that one change and watch the graph. If nothing changed on our side, I look at where the errors come from, one dependency, one zone, one kind of request, and reach for the matching generic mitigation: fail over the database, drain the zone, shed load. I avoid restarting things blindly, and before removing a broken pod I take it out of service and keep it for later. Meanwhile the communications lead has posted a first status update within ten minutes, in terms of what customers see, with a time for the next one. Once errors are back to normal I do not close the incident: I watch for fifteen to thirty minutes, check for side effects such as stuck orders or a queue backlog, and make sure the update says what customers should do about failed orders. Then I write down what I know while it is fresh, hand anything outstanding to the day team, and go back to bed. The root cause and the postmortem happen in daylight.

    🧠 Key takeaways

    • Classify by customer impact. When unsure, declare the higher severity and downgrade later.
    • Give people roles: commander, operations, communications, scribe. The commander coordinates and does not debug.
    • Mitigate, then diagnose. Ask what changed, and undo it before you are certain.
    • Make one announced change at a time, prefer reversible actions, and keep the evidence.
    • Update customers on a schedule, in their terms, with the time of the next update.
    • Blameless postmortems ask how the system made the mistake possible, and end in a few owned, dated actions.
    • Most minutes in an incident are lost to organisation, not to the fix.

    Check your understanding

    A release went out ten minutes ago and error rates have jumped. Nobody yet knows why. What should happen first?

    Mitigate before diagnosing. A rollback is fast and reversible, and the cause can be found afterwards with customers no longer affected.

    Why should the incident commander not be the person fixing the system?

    Command is a full-time job during an incident. When the commander starts debugging, nobody holds the overall picture.

    You are unsure whether a problem is a SEV1 or a SEV2. What is the better choice, and why?

    Severity can be lowered in a minute. Time lost before the right people join cannot be recovered.

    A postmortem concludes: "Root cause: an engineer ran the wrong command." What is wrong with that?

    "Human error" is the starting point of the analysis. The fixes are in the system that allowed one mistake to cause an outage.

    Which status-page update is best?

    It states the impact in the customer's terms, says what still works, and commits to the time of the next update without guessing at cause or fix time.
