Case Study: A Cloud-Cost Blowout

An outage announces itself. A cost incident does not. Nothing is slow, no alert fires, customers are happy, and the system is quietly spending three times what it should, every hour, for a month. The first person to notice is someone in finance opening an invoice. This case study follows a mid-sized online business whose monthly cloud bill went from $42,000 to $119,100 with no change in traffic. It is a composite of patterns that recur constantly, and it had three causes, none of which appeared on any architecture diagram: bytes passing through a NAT gateway, a log level, and an experiment nobody switched off. We follow the investigation from the invoice down to the three lines of configuration, and then ask what would have caught it on day five and not day thirty-three. The simulator contains the bill.

  • Investigate a cost increase by comparing periods and choosing the right grouping
  • Explain why grouping by service or by team can hide a cause that grouping by usage type reveals
  • Trace NAT gateway charges to the workload that produced them
  • Recognise the logging, data-transfer and forgotten-resource traps
  • Compare budget alerts, forecast alerts and anomaly detection by how early each fires
  • Treat a cost incident like any other incident: detect, mitigate, review

The bill

The company sells prints and photo books online. Its cloud bill had been close to $42,000 a month for most of a year: a couple of application services, a database, a large bucket of customers' photographs, and the usual supporting pieces. On the third of the month the finance manager forwarded the new invoice to the head of engineering with one line: "Is this right?" It was for $119,100.

Traffic had not grown. Nothing had been launched. There had been no outage and no complaints. The first reactions in the engineering channel were the usual ones, and all were wrong: that it must be a billing error, that someone had bought a commitment, that it was the database. The head of engineering did the useful thing and treated it as an incident. One person was put in charge of finding out, with the same questions as for any other incident (Chapter 37): what changed, and how do we stop it today, before we fully understand it?

That second question matters more for cost than people expect. A cost incident has no natural end. Every day spent on a careful analysis was costing about $2,500 more than it should.

Finding the cause

The method is short. Compare this period with the last normal one. Group the difference until it is concentrated in a few lines. Drill into those lines until each one names a resource and an owner.

Grouped by service, the increase fell in three places: "EC2 - Other" up $36,400, the monitoring service up $20,900, and compute up $19,800. Grouped by usage type, each of those became specific.

NAT gateway, gigabytes processed: up $36,400. A NAT gateway charges about $0.045 for every gigabyte that passes through it, so this was some 800 terabytes. The gateway cannot say who sent the traffic, but the network's flow logs can.

The addresses belonged to a new image-resizing service, deployed on the fifth of the month, and the destinations were object storage. A job in that service was re-reading the entire photo library to regenerate thumbnails, in a loop, and because its pods were in private subnets with no gateway endpoint for storage, every byte went out through the NAT gateway and was charged for.

Log ingestion: up $20,900. Log services charge about $0.50 per gigabyte taken in, so this was roughly 42 terabytes. One log group accounted for almost all of it.

On the twelfth there had been a brief incident in the payments service. To diagnose it, an engineer had set the log level to debug, which logged every request and response in full. The incident was closed in an hour. The log level was never changed back.

GPU instance-hours: up $19,800. Three large GPU instances had been running since the ninth, in the main production account, with no tags. The audit trail showed who had launched them: a data scientist testing a recommendation model, who had finished the experiment the same afternoon and gone on holiday.

Three causes, with three different owners, starting on three different days. That is typical. A bill that has tripled has rarely done so for one reason.

Egress and logging traps

The mitigations took one afternoon. The job was stopped, the log level reset and the instances terminated. The lasting fixes took a week, and each is an instance of a general trap.

resource "aws_vpc_endpoint" "s3" {
  vpc_id            = aws_vpc.main.id
  service_name      = "com.amazonaws.eu-west-2.s3"
  vpc_endpoint_type = "Gateway"
  route_table_ids   = aws_route_table.private[*].id       # private subnets now reach storage without the NAT gateway
}

What these have in common is that the system works. Charges that accompany a failure get noticed with the failure. These accompany success: the thumbnails were generated, the payments were logged, the experiment ran.

Budgets and anomaly alerts

The review asked the usual last question: why did nothing tell us? The company had no cost alerts at all. The first signal was the invoice, 33 days after the first cause began. There are three levels of alert, and they differ mainly in how early they fire.

  • A budget alert on actual spend fires when the month's total passes a threshold. It is simple and late: by the time a monthly figure is exceeded, most of the damage is done.
  • A budget alert on forecast spend fires when the projected month-end total passes the threshold. It reacts within days of a change in the rate of spending.
  • Anomaly detection compares each day's spend, per service, with recent history and alerts on a jump. It fires on the day the cause begins, and it says which service moved.
resource "aws_budgets_budget" "monthly" {
  name         = "production-monthly"
  budget_type  = "COST"
  limit_amount = "50000"
  limit_unit   = "USD"
  time_unit    = "MONTHLY"
  notification {
    comparison_operator        = "GREATER_THAN"
    threshold                  = 100
    threshold_type             = "PERCENTAGE"
    notification_type          = "FORECASTED"              # projected, not yet spent
    subscriber_sns_topic_arns  = [aws_sns_topic.cost_alerts.arn]   # to the engineering channel, not to a finance inbox
  }
}

resource "aws_ce_anomaly_monitor" "by_service" {
  name              = "by-service"
  monitor_type      = "DIMENSIONAL"
  monitor_dimension = "SERVICE"
}

Two details decide whether such alerts work. They must go to the people who can act, the engineers, in the channel where they already look, with enough detail to start: which service, which account, since when. And billing data is delayed, commonly by several hours and sometimes a day, so cost alerts are a second line. For things that can run away in hours, the first line is an ordinary operational alert on the cause itself: bytes through the NAT gateway, bytes ingested per log group, instances running without an owner tag.

Clouds, in general, will not stop you spending. A budget is a notification, not a limit. Where a hard stop is wanted, as in a sandbox, it has to be built: a budget action that removes permissions or stops instances, a quota set deliberately low, an automatic shutdown at night. The company's final list was short: a gateway endpoint in every network, by default, in the Terraform module; debug logging that turns itself off after an hour; experiments only in sandbox accounts with a budget and a nightly shutdown; a forecast alert and anomaly detection on every account; and the daily cost chart on the engineering dashboard, next to latency and errors.

    Things to try.

    • Start with the team tag, which is how most people first look at a bill. One row changed: "untagged", up by the whole $77,100. Four teams have unchanged lines and a clear conscience.
    • Group by service. Three rows rise: EC2 - Other by $36,400, Monitoring by $20,900 and EC2 compute by $19,800. You know where to look and not yet what for.
    • Group by usage type. The same three amounts now read "NAT gateway, GB processed", "Log ingestion, GB" and "Instance-hours, GPU". Of the 13 usage types on the bill, 10 did not move at all.
    • Group by resource and each has a name you can go and look at.
    • Now the alerting. With none, all 26 days from the fifth onwards are red, and $77,100 is wasted before anyone reads the invoice.
    • A budget on actual spend fires once, on day 16, after $29,500 has gone. It saves the second half of the month.
    • A budget on forecast spend fires on days 5, 9 and 12, and only $3,400 is wasted. Note the warning under it: on day 9 the projection was $50,600 against a limit of $50,000.
    • Anomaly detection fires on the same three days for the same $3,400, with more margin, and it names the service. Either of the last two turns a $119,100 month into a $45,400 one.

    Use case: $72,000 in a few hours, on a free tier

    In 2020 the founder of a small start-up called Milkie Way published an account of the night his company nearly ended. The team was testing a new feature on Google Cloud: a service that crawled web pages and stored what it found in the Firestore database. They were on the free tier, with a billing budget of seven dollars set as a reminder. They deployed a test version in the evening and went to bed. The code had a flaw: each page it processed queued further requests to itself, so the number of running copies multiplied, and the platform, doing exactly what a serverless platform is for, scaled out to meet the demand, to a thousand instances at once. Each instance read and wrote the database continuously. By the time the founders understood the emails from the billing system, the service had performed over a hundred billion database reads and the charges stood at about $72,000. The budget alert had fired, but a budget is a notification and not a limit, and billing figures lag behind usage, so the first alert described a small amount when the real total was already in the thousands. The spending stopped when the project's free-tier billing account was automatically upgraded and then suspended for non-payment. Google later waived the bill as a one-off. The founder's own list of lessons matches this chapter's closely: set limits on how far a service may scale, because the default is "as far as needed"; do not rely on billing alerts for anything that can run away in minutes; test with small limits first; and understand that the cloud will, by default, sell you as much as your code asks for.

    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Finding out from the invoice. With no alerts, detection time is a month.
    • Assuming a billing error, and waiting for the provider to reply while the meter runs.
    • Analysing before stopping. Mitigate first: a cost incident has no natural end.
    • Grouping only by service. "EC2 - Other" and "data transfer" hide the cause; usage type shows it.
    • Relying on tags to find the culprit. The surprise is usually in something untagged or shared.
    • Looking for one cause. A tripled bill often has several, with different owners.
    • Private subnets with no gateway endpoint for storage.
    • Debug logging switched on by hand and off by memory.
    • Experiments in the production account, with no owner tag, expiry or budget.
    • Treating a budget as a limit. It only sends a message.
    • Cost alerts that go to finance, who cannot tell whether a number is wrong or act on it.
    • No limit on how far a serverless service or an autoscaler may scale.

    Interview drill

    Explain it simply: Why can a NAT gateway produce a surprisingly large bill?

    How a strong answer sounds
    A NAT gateway is charged two ways: an hourly fee, which is small, and a fee for every gigabyte it processes, about four and a half cents. Workloads in private subnets send all their outbound traffic through it by default, and that includes traffic to the provider's own services such as object storage and the container registry. So a job that reads a large bucket, or nodes that pull big images frequently, can push hundreds of terabytes through the gateway without anyone having decided to. Nothing fails, so nothing draws attention to it. The fix for storage is a gateway endpoint, which is free and routes that traffic privately; interface endpoints or a registry mirror do the same for other services. To find the culprit you use VPC flow logs to see which sources and destinations account for the bytes, since the bill only names the gateway.

    Deeper: How would you set up cost monitoring for a company so that a runaway cost is caught in a day and not a month?

    How a strong answer sounds
    I would build it in layers, fastest first, because billing data itself is late. The first layer is operational alerts on the things that cause runaway cost, using ordinary metrics that are available in minutes: bytes through each NAT gateway, bytes ingested per log group, the number of running instances by type, concurrent executions of functions, and resources without an owner tag. Those have thresholds based on normal behaviour and go to the owning team. The second layer is the provider's cost anomaly detection, per service and per account, which catches whatever I did not think to alert on, usually within a day. The third is budgets on forecast spend for every account, as a backstop. All of these go to engineering channels with the service, account and start time attached, since a figure sent to a finance inbox cannot be acted on. Then visibility: a daily cost chart on the same dashboards as latency and errors, broken down by team, with unit cost so that growth is distinguishable from waste. For that to work, attribution has to be sound: an account per workload and environment, required tags enforced in the pipeline, and an explicit owner for shared things such as gateways. I would also put limits where a hard stop is acceptable: sandbox accounts with budget actions and nightly shutdown, maximum instance counts on autoscalers and functions, quotas kept deliberately modest. Finally I would treat a cost spike as an incident with a short review, because the lasting improvements, such as a gateway endpoint by default in the network module or debug logging that expires, come out of asking why the last one was possible.

    🧠 Key takeaways

    • A cost incident is silent: the system works and nothing alerts. Treat it as an incident and stop the spend first.
    • Investigate by comparing periods, then grouping by usage type, resource and day.
    • Team tags do not find the cause when the cause is untagged, and it usually is.
    • NAT gateway bytes, log ingestion and forgotten experiments are the three commonest surprises.
    • A gateway endpoint for storage is free and removes the largest of them.
    • Anomaly detection and forecast alerts fire in days; a budget on actual spend fires late; an invoice fires last.
    • A budget is a message, not a limit. Build hard stops where you want them.

    Check your understanding

    A bill has risen sharply and the increase is under "EC2 - Other". What is the most useful next step?

    "EC2 - Other" combines NAT processing, data transfer, disks and more. The usage type separates them and usually names the cause.

    Pods in private subnets read 500 TB from object storage in the same region through a NAT gateway. Roughly what does the NAT processing cost at $0.045 per GB, and what removes it?

    500,000 GB × $0.045 = $22,500. A gateway endpoint routes storage traffic privately at no charge.

    Which alert fires earliest when daily spending suddenly doubles on the fifth of the month?

    Anomaly detection compares each day with recent days, so it fires on the day of the jump. A budget on actual spend waits for the month's total.

    A team sets a monthly budget of $50,000 and assumes spending cannot go above it. What is wrong?

    A hard stop has to be built: a budget action that stops or restricts resources, low quotas, or scheduled shutdown.

    Why did grouping the bill by team tag fail to find the causes in this case?

    Everything unexpected landed in "untagged". Attribution needs owners for shared resources and tags enforced when things are created.
