Capacity Planning

Capacity planning is making sure the system has enough of everything before it is needed, without paying for much more than that. Autoscaling has made the day-to-day version of the problem easier and has not removed it. Clouds have quotas. Databases do not scale out at the press of a button. A new machine type may simply not be available in the quantity you want. And no autoscaler knows that marketing has booked a television advert for Thursday. This chapter covers how to forecast demand, how much spare capacity to keep and why, how to find out by load testing what your system can really carry, and how to prepare for the peaks that arrive on a known date. The simulator runs a year of growth against a capacity plan.

Demand forecasting

A forecast starts with choosing what to forecast. CPU is a poor choice, because it is an effect. Pick the driver, the business quantity that causes load, such as orders per minute, active users or messages sent, and measure how much of each resource one unit of the driver consumes. Then a forecast of the business becomes a forecast of capacity, and people outside engineering can check it.

Three properties of demand shape every forecast.

  • Peaks, not averages. A shop that averages 1,000 requests a second and peaks at 4,000 needs capacity for 4,000. Always plan from the peak of the driver.
  • Seasonality. Demand repeats by day, by week and by year. Compare this Monday with last Monday, and this December with last December, or ordinary cycles look like growth and decline.
  • Compounding. Growth of 6% a month is not 72% a year. It is 101%: the service doubles. Small errors in the monthly rate become large errors a year out.
# Short horizon: will this disk fill within four hours, on the trend of the last six?  (Chapter 21)
predict_linear(node_filesystem_avail_bytes{mountpoint="/data"}[6h], 4 * 3600) < 0

# Longer horizon: the weekly peak of the driver, to fit a growth rate to
max_over_time(sum(rate(orders_total[5m]))[7d:5m])

# Resource per unit of demand: CPU cores used for each order per second
sum(rate(container_cpu_usage_seconds_total{namespace="shop"}[5m])) / sum(rate(orders_total[5m]))

A forecast should be a range: "between 9,000 and 12,000 requests a second by December". The width of the range is information, and it is what headroom has to cover. Forecast as far ahead as your longest lead time and no further. And talk to the people who know what is coming: the product roadmap and the marketing calendar predict step changes that no trend line can.

Headroom

Headroom is capacity above the expected peak. It is not waste; each part of it is there for a reason.

Headroom forWhy it is neededHow much
Forecast errorGrowth is faster than predictedGrows with lead time: the longer capacity takes to arrive, the more wrong the forecast can become
FailureA zone or region is lost and the rest must carry its loadWith N equal parts, run each below (N − 1)/N (Chapter 33)
SpikesTraffic jumps faster than scaling reactsEnough for the minutes it takes new capacity to start (Chapter 25)
DeploymentsRolling updates run extra pods, and new code may be slowerThe surge setting, plus a margin for regressions

The first row holds the key relationship. Lead time is the delay between deciding you need capacity and having it. Whatever capacity you have today was decided that long ago, on a forecast made then. If growth has been faster since, the gap between forecast and reality has had the whole lead time to widen, and headroom is the only thing covering it.

In a data centre, lead time is months: order, deliver, rack. In a cloud it is often minutes, which is the cloud's great gift to capacity planners. But the minutes apply only inside limits that have long lead times of their own:

  • Quotas. Every account has limits on instances, addresses and much else, and a large increase is a support request that can take days or weeks.
  • Availability. The provider may not have the instance type you want in the zone you want. Specialised hardware such as GPUs can be short for months.
  • Things that do not scale out: the primary database, a shard's size, a third party's rate limit, a licence count.
  • Address space. A subnet sized for 250 addresses cannot hold 400 pods (Chapter 5).
  • Money. A budget approval is a lead time.

Headroom costs money every hour (Chapter 31). Too little costs outages. The honest way to set it is to work out, for each resource, the lead time and the plausible forecast error over that time, and to keep enough to cover it.

Load testing

Every capacity plan contains the number "one instance can handle X". If that number came from a guess, a vendor's brochure or last year's test, the whole plan inherits its error. A load test measures it: send controlled traffic at the system, increase it in steps, and watch where the service stops meeting its SLO.

// k6: raise the arrival rate in steps and stop trusting the system when the SLO breaks
import http from 'k6/http';

export const options = {
  scenarios: {
    steps: {
      executor: 'ramping-arrival-rate',          // requests arrive at a set rate, however slow the replies
      startRate: 100, timeUnit: '1s', preAllocatedVUs: 500, maxVUs: 5000,
      stages: [
        { target: 200, duration: '5m' }, { target: 300, duration: '5m' },
        { target: 400, duration: '5m' }, { target: 500, duration: '5m' },
      ],
    },
  },
  thresholds: { http_req_duration: ['p(99)<400'], http_req_failed: ['rate<0.001'] },
};

export default function () { http.get('https://staging.example.com/api/products?page=1'); }

That table carries the main lessons. Capacity is where the SLO breaks, not where CPU reaches 100%. Someone reading only the CPU column would have said 500. Latency does not rise in a straight line: it stays flat and then turns sharply upwards at a knee, and the usable capacity is just before it. And the result is specific to this version of the code on this size of machine, so the test has to be repeated when either changes; many teams run a short one in the pipeline to catch performance regressions.

Different shapes of test answer different questions. A load test checks behaviour at the expected peak. A stress test keeps going until something breaks, to find out what breaks first and how. A spike test jumps suddenly, to test scaling and queues. A soak test holds a steady load for hours, to find leaks. Two cautions apply to all of them. Use an open load model, in which requests arrive at a fixed rate whether or not earlier ones have finished: a tool that waits for each reply before sending the next slows down politely when the system does, and hides exactly the collapse you are looking for. And test the whole path. The first bottleneck is rarely the service you were thinking about. It is the database connection limit, the login service, or a third party that rate-limits you.

Organic vs event-driven growth

Demand rises in two quite different ways, and a plan that handles one can be helpless against the other.

Organic growthEvent-driven peak
ShapeSmooth and compounding: a few per cent a monthA step: two to fifty times normal, within minutes
ExamplesMore customers, more usage eachA launch, a sale, an advert, a ticket release, a news story
Predictable from history?Yes, from the trendNo. From a calendar, if someone tells you
Handled byAutoscaling, with a regular review of limits and bottlenecksPreparing in advance: autoscaling reacts too late and too small
Usual failureA limit reached quietly: a quota, a disk, a connection poolNobody told engineering, or nobody tested at that scale

For organic growth the work is a routine: each quarter, extend the forecast, compare it with every limit, and act on any that will be reached within its lead time. For an event the work is a project with a date.

Capacity for month m is the demand observed at month m minus the lead time, grown forward at the planned 5% for the lead time, plus the headroom. A planned event is provisioned for; a surprise is not. If the per-instance figure was never tested, every instance carries 350 where the plan counted 500, so real capacity is 70% of what the plan believes. In a short month, the share of requests that cannot be served is the gap between demand and capacity.

Things to try.

  • The starting year grows at 8% a month against a planned 5%, with a six-month lead time and 15% headroom. Demand overtakes capacity in month 5 and stays ahead for the rest of the year. The shortfall is small, 2.9% at worst, and permanent. The plan needed 18% of headroom.
  • Fix it two ways. Raise headroom to 30% and the year is clean, at the cost of 13% of capacity idle. Or keep 15% and cut the lead time to 3 months: also clean, because a three-month-old forecast needs only 8.8%, and a one-month-old one 2.9%. Shortening lead time is worth as much as buying headroom.
  • Set growth to "as forecast", lead time to 1 month, headroom to 30%, and choose "never tested". The forecast is perfect, and the service is short in all 13 months, by 9%. An instance that carries 350 where the plan says 500 needs 43% of headroom just to break even.
  • A surprise event with 50% headroom and everything else right: one month is short, and in it a quarter of requests are not served. Switch the event to "in the plan" and the year is clean with only 15%.
  • Growth of 3% with a six-month lead and 50% headroom: never short, and 39% of capacity idle on average. Over-forecasting has a price too; it is just a quieter one.
  • Of the 162 settings, 66 are clean and 33 are short only on the day of the surprise event. The other 63 are short in ordinary months, and 60 of those 63 are the plans built on a capacity figure nobody tested.

Use case: HealthCare.gov, launch day

On 1 October 2013 the United States government opened HealthCare.gov, the website through which millions of people were to buy health insurance under a new law. The date had been fixed in legislation years before, which makes this one of the best-documented examples of an event-driven peak. According to officials' later statements and congressional testimony, the site had been built to handle around 50,000 to 60,000 simultaneous users. On the first day some 250,000 arrived at once, roughly five times the plan, and several million visited within the first days. The site was almost unusable for weeks. Documents released afterwards recorded that six people managed to complete an enrolment on the first day. The capacity estimate was only part of it. Accounts from the investigations describe end-to-end testing that began only a couple of weeks before launch, and a test shortly before opening in which the system failed with a few hundred simulated users. A late design decision made every visitor create an account before they could look at prices, which sent the whole crowd through the one registration component that was least able to take it, when most of them only wanted to browse. And there was no way to make excess visitors wait: the system simply fell over. A small team of experienced engineers was brought in, added monitoring that had not existed, found and removed the bottlenecks one at a time and put a waiting-room page in front, and by December the site was carrying its intended load. Every step of this chapter's event checklist is visible in what was missing: a realistic number, a load test of the whole path with time to act on it, a core path protected from optional work, and a queue at the door.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Planning from averages. The system has to survive the peak.
  • Treating 5% a month as "about 60% a year". It compounds to 80%, and 8% a month to 152%.
  • Believing autoscaling removes capacity planning. It works inside quotas, availability and the limits of things that do not scale out.
  • A capacity figure nobody measured, or measured two years and forty releases ago.
  • Reading capacity off the CPU graph. Latency breaks the SLO well before CPU is full.
  • Load testing one service in isolation, when the bottleneck is the database, the login service or a third party.
  • A load tool that waits for each reply, and therefore eases off just as the system starts to struggle.
  • Finding out about the launch from the press release.
  • Scaling up when the event starts. New capacity takes minutes; the crowd arrives in seconds.
  • No way to say "please wait". Without a queue or load shedding, extra demand becomes an outage for everyone.
  • Forgetting to scale back down afterwards, and to record what the real peak was for next year.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: If we have autoscaling, why do we still need capacity planning?

How a strong answer sounds
Autoscaling adjusts capacity within limits, over minutes. Capacity planning is about the limits themselves and about what autoscaling cannot see. Account quotas, the availability of an instance type, subnet address space, the size of the primary database and third-party rate limits do not scale automatically, and changing them takes days or weeks. Autoscaling also reacts to load that has already arrived, so it is too slow for a step change like a launch or a sale, which has to be scaled for in advance from a calendar. And it scales what it is told to scale, on the assumption that each instance can carry a certain load, which someone has to have measured. So the planning work is forecasting the driver of demand, checking that forecast against every limit and its lead time, load testing to know real per-unit capacity, and preparing explicitly for known events.

Deeper: Marketing tells you that in three weeks a television campaign will send "a lot" of traffic to the site. What do you do?

How a strong answer sounds
First I turn "a lot" into numbers, with marketing: when the adverts air, how many viewers, what happened on previous campaigns, and which page they will land on. From that I set a planning figure and deliberately choose a multiple of their estimate, because the cost of being too high is a few hours of spare capacity and the cost of being too low is the campaign failing in public. Second, I find out what the system can carry now. I load test the real user journey from the landing page through sign-up to checkout, at the planning figure, with an open arrival-rate model, in an environment as close to production as I can get. I expect something unexpected to break first, typically the database connection pool, a login or payment dependency, or a cache that is cold, which is exactly why this happens in week one and not week three. Third, the long-lead items: cloud quota increases, a larger database instance or read replicas, and a conversation with any third party about their rate limits. Fourth, the plan for the day. Capacity is scaled up beforehand on a schedule, with caches warmed. I agree with product which optional features can be switched off under pressure, and I put a waiting room or rate limit in front so that overflow waits. I ask for a change freeze around the window and make sure people are watching dashboards built for it, with a named decision-maker. Then a rehearsal: a final load test with all of that in place. Afterwards I scale down, and I record the actual peak against the estimate, because the next campaign's forecast should start from data.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Forecast the business driver at its peak, allow for seasonality and compounding, and state a range.
  • Headroom covers forecast error, failures, spikes and deployments. Its size follows from lead time.
  • Cloud lead times are short only inside quotas, availability, address space and the parts that do not scale out.
  • Load test to learn real capacity: the highest rate that still meets the SLO, found on the whole path with an open model.
  • An untested capacity figure can consume all of your headroom by itself.
  • Organic growth is a routine review. An event is a project: get the number, test, raise limits, pre-scale, shed and queue.

Check your understanding

A load test of one pod shows p99 latency of 140 ms at 300 requests a second, 390 ms at 350 and 1,900 ms at 400. CPU at 400 is 88%. The SLO is a p99 of 400 ms. What is the pod's capacity?

Capacity is the highest load that still meets the SLO. At 400 the latency is far past it, even though CPU is not full.

Why does the headroom you need grow with lead time?

If growth beats the forecast, the gap compounds every month until new capacity can arrive. Headroom is what covers that gap.

A service grows 6% a month. Roughly how much larger is it after twelve months?

1.06 to the power 12 is about 2.01. Growth compounds, so adding the monthly rates under-estimates it.

Why is autoscaling by itself a poor defence against a product launch announced for 09:00?

Known events are scaled for in advance, with limits raised beforehand and a queue for the overflow.

A load-testing tool sends a request, waits for the reply, then sends the next. Why can this hide an overload?

Real users do not wait for other users' requests to finish. An open, arrival-rate model keeps sending at the set rate and exposes queueing.
