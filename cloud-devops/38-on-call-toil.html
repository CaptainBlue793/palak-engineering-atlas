On-Call & Toil Reduction

A service that runs all night needs somebody who will answer when it breaks at night. How a team arranges that says a great deal about it. Done badly, on-call is a pager that fires forty times a week for things nobody can act on, carried by the same three people until they leave. Done well, it is a rare, fairly shared and well-supported duty, and every page teaches the team something that makes the next one less likely. The same thinking applies to the daytime version of the pager: toil, the repetitive manual work that keeps a system running and never improves it. This chapter covers how to design a rotation people can sustain, how to write runbooks that work at 3 a.m., what counts as toil, and how to decide what to automate first. The simulator gives you a quarter's engineering hours and eight chores.

  • Design an on-call rotation, and work out how many people it needs
  • Set limits that keep on-call sustainable, and act when they are exceeded
  • Write a runbook that a tired engineer who did not build the service can follow
  • Define toil precisely, and tell it apart from overhead and project work
  • Rank automation by payback, risk and interruption cost
  • Prefer removing the cause of a chore to automating the chore

Rotations

Being on call means being reachable and able to respond within an agreed time, typically five to fifteen minutes, for an agreed period. A rotation shares that duty among a group. The design questions are who is in it, how long a shift lasts, and who backs them up.

ShapeHow it worksGood forCost
Weekly, one siteOne person is primary for a week, day and night; another is secondary and is paged if the primary does not answerMost teamsNight pages. Needs enough people that a turn comes round rarely
Follow the sunTwo or three sites in different time zones each cover their own daytime and hand overCompanies with engineers on several continentsHandovers; each site must know the whole system
Business hours onlyPages wait until morningInternal tools and low-tier services with an SLO that allows itNone, which is the point: not everything deserves a night page
TieredA first line handles known problems from runbooks and escalates the rest to the owning teamVery large estatesThe first line can become a ticket-passing layer that learns nothing

The arithmetic of a rotation is simple and often ignored. With a weekly shift and N people, each person is primary one week in N, and with a secondary as well, on call two weeks in N. Google's SRE teams use two rules of thumb that are a reasonable starting point for anyone. An engineer should spend no more than a quarter of their time on call, which for a primary-and-secondary weekly rotation at one site means at least eight people. And a shift should see no more than about two incidents, because each one, properly handled, is hours of work once the follow-up and the review are counted. A team of three carrying a pager around the clock is not a rotation. It is a countdown to someone resigning.

Beyond the numbers, a few practices make the difference between a duty and a burden:

  • The people who build the service are in its rotation. Nothing improves reliability like the author of the code being woken by it.
  • Every page is reviewed. Each week, look at every page from the last shift: was it real, was it actionable, was there a runbook? Delete or fix the alerts that fail the test.
  • Handover is a ritual: a short written note on what happened, what is still fragile and what is in progress.
  • On-call is paid for, in money or time off, and a disturbed night means a late start.
  • Escalating is never held against anyone. The secondary and the incident commander exist to be called.
  • Nobody starts alone. New engineers shadow a shift, then lead one with a shadow, and practise on tabletop exercises first.

Runbooks

A runbook is the written procedure for one alert or one operational task. Its reader is a competent engineer who did not build this service, was asleep four minutes ago and has never seen this alert before. Write for that person.

# Runbook: CheckoutQueueBacklog

What this alert means   The payment-events queue has more than 10,000 messages waiting for over 10 minutes.
Customer impact         Order confirmation emails are delayed. Checkout itself still works.
Severity                SEV3 if the backlog is steady or shrinking. SEV2 if it is growing: confirmations will be hours late.

First checks            (two minutes)
  $ kubectl -n shop get pods -l app=payment-worker          # are the workers running? restarting?
  $ kubectl -n shop logs deploy/payment-worker --tail=50     # the same error repeated?
  Dashboard: https://grafana.example.com/d/payment-worker    # did consumption stop, or did arrivals jump?

If workers are crash-looping after a deploy
  Roll back:  argocd app rollback payment-worker             # then see "After mitigation"
If workers are healthy and arrivals jumped
  Scale out:  kubectl -n shop scale deploy/payment-worker --replicas=12     # safe up to 20; the database allows 200 connections
If one message is failing repeatedly
  It should move to the dead-letter queue after 5 attempts. If it has not, see "Poison message" below.

Escalate to             #team-payments, or page payments-secondary, if not improving after 20 minutes.
After mitigation        Note what you did in the incident channel. Do not delete the dead-letter queue.
Last used / reviewed    2026-09-18, by the on-call engineer during a real page.

The useful properties are all visible there. It starts with meaning and impact, so the reader can judge urgency. Its commands can be copied, with safe limits stated. It branches on what you observe, not on what the cause might be. It says when to stop and call someone. And it is dated by use. The alert itself should link to it, so nobody searches a wiki at 3 a.m.:

- alert: CheckoutQueueBacklog
  expr: queue_messages_ready{queue="payment-events"} > 10000
  for: 10m
  labels: { severity: ticket, team: payments }
  annotations:
    summary: "payment-events backlog is {{ $value }} messages"
    runbook_url: "https://runbooks.example.com/payments/checkout-queue-backlog"

Toil defined

"Toil" is used loosely to mean any work people dislike. The SRE definition is narrower and more useful. Toil is operational work that has most of these properties:

  • Manual: a person does it by hand.
  • Repetitive: it has been done before and will be done again.
  • Automatable: a machine could do it; no human judgement is really needed.
  • Reactive: it arrives as an interruption, not as a plan.
  • Without lasting value: afterwards the system is no better than it was.
  • Growing with the service: twice the customers means twice as much of it.
ToilNot toil
Restarting a worker each time an alert firesWorking out why the worker hangs, and fixing it
Creating accounts for each new joiner by handBuilding the self-service access request
Clicking "approve" on a deploy that always passesReviewing a risky migration
Copying numbers into a weekly reportMeetings, planning, hiring: that is overhead, a different problem
Renewing a certificate from a calendar reminderDiagnosing a new kind of incident: it needs judgement

The last property is why toil matters so much. Work that grows in step with the service means the team must grow in step with the service, or drown. Google's rule is that an SRE should spend at most half their time on toil, keeping the other half for engineering that reduces future toil. The number matters less than having one, measuring against it, and treating a breach as a signal to stop and invest. Measure simply: tag tickets, or ask each on-call engineer at handover to estimate hours by category. Some toil is fine, even pleasant. The danger is the slow kind of failure, in which a team is too busy bailing to fix the leak.

Automation priorities

There is always more toil than time to remove it, so the question is the order. The basic sum is payback: the hours it costs to automate a task, divided by the hours a week it saves. A task that takes six hours a week and twenty hours to automate pays for itself in under four weeks. One that takes two hours a week and forty to automate needs twenty. Shortest payback first is a good default, and three other things adjust it.

  • Risk. A rare manual task that causes an outage when forgotten or mistyped, certificate renewal being the classic, deserves automation long before its hours justify it.
  • Interruption. A ten-minute task that arrives twelve times a week costs far more than two hours, because each arrival breaks someone's concentration.
  • Elimination. Before automating a chore, ask why it exists. An automatic restart for a hung worker is good. Fixing the bug that hangs it is better, and leaves no automation to maintain.

Automation is software, with the costs of software. It needs tests, an owner and maintenance, and it fails in its own way: a script does the wrong thing to a thousand machines as fast as it does the right thing. Give remediation the same guard rails as a rollout (Chapter 17): act on a few targets at a time, stop when the result is unexpected, and cap how much it may do in an hour. And do not automate a process that is itself wrong. Automating a pointless approval step makes the pointlessness permanent.

    Use case: Facebook's FBAR, two engineers doing the work of two hundred

    By 2011 Facebook ran tens of thousands of servers, and at that scale something is always broken: a failed disk, a hung process, a machine that will not reboot. Each fault raised an alert, and each alert had a known response that an engineer carried out by hand. It was a perfect example of toil: manual, repetitive, without lasting value and growing with the fleet. Facebook's answer, which it described publicly that year, was a system called FBAR, for Facebook Auto-Remediation. When an alert fired, FBAR picked it up before any person did, ran the same diagnostic steps an engineer would, and if the problem matched a known pattern applied the known fix: restart the service, reimage the machine, or take it out of rotation and file a repair request for the data-centre staff. Engineers taught it new cases by writing small remediation routines, so each kind of fault needed a human only the first time. It was built with the caution this chapter recommends. It worked on a limited number of machines at once, so that a mistaken diagnosis could not take down a cluster, and it handed anything it did not recognise to a person. Facebook reported that FBAR was maintained by two engineers and was doing work equivalent to about two hundred full-time administrators. The lasting point is not the ratio. The engineers on call stopped being woken for the thousandth disk failure, and were free to work on the failures nobody had seen before.

    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • A rotation of two or three people, on call every other week for ever.
    • Pages that need no action. Each one teaches the on-call engineer to ignore the pager.
    • Nobody reviews the pages. The same false alarm fires weekly for a year.
    • On-call as an unpaid extra, with a full day's work expected after a broken night.
    • Runbooks that explain the architecture and never say what to type.
    • Runbooks nobody has followed since they were written.
    • Calling everything unpleasant "toil". Meetings are overhead; a hard investigation is engineering.
    • Not measuring toil, so nobody notices it has reached 70% of the team's week.
    • Automating the biggest chore first when three small ones would pay back sooner.
    • Automating a symptom for ever when the cause could be fixed once.
    • Automation with no limits, able to restart or delete everything at once.
    • Heroics as culture: praising the person who was up all night, and not asking why the system needed them.
    🎤
    Interview drill

    Explain it simply: What is toil, and why does it matter?

    How a strong answer sounds
    Toil is operational work that is manual, repetitive, automatable, reactive, leaves nothing better behind, and grows in proportion to the service. Restarting a stuck worker every time an alert fires is toil. Finding and fixing why it gets stuck is engineering. It matters because of that last property: if the manual work scales with the service, the team has to scale with it too, or it spends all its time keeping things running and none improving them. That is why SRE teams cap toil, at Google at half of each engineer's time, and use the rest to automate or remove it. It is not the same as overhead such as meetings, and not all operational work is toil: handling a new kind of incident needs judgement. Some toil is acceptable. The aim is to keep it measured and bounded.

    Deeper: You join a team of four whose on-call gets about forty pages a week. Two people have already left. What do you do?

    How a strong answer sounds
    I would treat it as an emergency for the team and start with data, because forty pages is a number, not a diagnosis. I would export the last month of pages and sort them: by alert, by time of day, by whether any action was taken, and by what the action was. In my experience that list is very uneven: a handful of alerts produce most of the pages, and many pages needed no action or the same trivial action each time. That gives three piles. Pages where nothing was done are deleted or turned into tickets or dashboards straight away, and that alone often halves the load in a week. Pages with the same manual fix each time get a runbook now, then a script, then automatic remediation with limits, and where possible I fix the underlying cause so the alert never fires. Pages that were real and different each time are the genuine on-call work, and they become the reliability backlog. In parallel I would fix the human side, because the team is close to breaking. Four people around the clock is too few, so I would ask for help: borrow engineers from the product teams who own the services, make low-tier services business-hours only, add a secondary so nobody is alone, and make sure broken nights are compensated. I would also agree a limit with management, say two actionable pages per shift, with an understanding that when we exceed it, reliability work takes priority over features until we are back under. Then I would put the weekly page review in the calendar permanently and report the page count as a team metric. The message to the team has to be believable: the load is the system's fault, here is the plan, and here is the number going down.

    🧠 Key takeaways

    • A sustainable rotation needs enough people: no one on call more than about a quarter of the time, around eight for a primary and secondary at one site.
    • Limit incidents per shift, review every page, and remove alerts nobody can act on.
    • Runbooks are for a tired stranger: meaning, impact, commands to copy, when to escalate. Keep them current by using them.
    • Toil is manual, repetitive, automatable, reactive, valueless afterwards and grows with the service. Cap it and measure it.
    • Climb the ladder: runbook, script, button, automatic, and best of all unnecessary.
    • Rank automation by payback, then adjust for risk and interruption.
    • Give automation limits, and let it hand unusual cases to a person.

    Check your understanding

    A team runs a weekly rotation with a primary and a secondary, and wants nobody to be on call more than a quarter of the time. What is the smallest team that manages it?

    Each week uses two people, so with N people each is on call 2 weeks in N. A quarter of the time means 2/N ≤ 1/4, so N is at least 8.

    Which of these is toil?

    It is manual, repetitive, automatable, reactive and leaves the system no better. The retention policy is engineering, the meeting is overhead, and the investigation needs judgement.

    Task A takes 6 hours a week and 20 hours to automate. Task B takes 10 hours a week and 60 hours to automate. Which pays back sooner?

    Payback is cost divided by weekly saving: 20/6 ≈ 3.3 weeks and 60/10 = 6 weeks.

    What should a runbook begin with?

    The reader must judge urgency first. Then come quick checks, copyable mitigations, and when to escalate.

    An auto-remediation system restarts a hung service on any machine that reports it. Why should it have a limit on how many machines it acts on per hour?

    Routine volume is handled automatically. Unusual volume is a signal, so the automation stops and pages a person.
