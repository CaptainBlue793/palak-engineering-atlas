Supply-Chain Security

Very little of what you ship is code you wrote. A typical service is a few thousand lines of your own on top of hundreds of open-source packages, a base image, a compiler, a handful of third-party CI actions and a build machine. Each of those is a way in, and attackers have noticed: it is often easier to poison something a thousand companies install than to break into one of them. This chapter maps the software supply chain and its attacks, then covers the four defences that matter: knowing what is inside your artefacts (SBOMs), proving where they came from (signing and provenance), fixing your inputs so that they cannot change under you (pinning), and the SLSA framework that ties these together. The simulator runs eight real kinds of attack against the defences you choose.

The attack surface

The software supply chain is everything that goes into turning an idea into a running program: source code, the dependencies it pulls in, the tools and machines that build it, the place the built artefact is stored, and the step that deploys it. An attacker who controls any one link controls what runs in production.

LinkIncidentWhat happened
SourcePHP, 2021Two commits adding a backdoor were pushed to PHP's self-hosted Git server under the names of core developers. Reviewers caught them within hours
Dependencyevent-stream, 2018The tired maintainer of a popular npm package handed it to a volunteer, who added a dependency that stole from one specific Bitcoin wallet application
Dependencyxz, 2024A contributor spent about two years earning maintainer rights on a compression library, then hid a backdoor aimed at SSH servers in its release archives. An engineer found it while investigating logins that were half a second slow

SBOMs

A software bill of materials (SBOM) is a machine-readable list of every component inside an artefact: each package, its version, its licence and where it came from, including the dependencies of dependencies. The two common formats are SPDX and CycloneDX. You generate one at build time and store it with the image.

$ syft registry.example.com/checkout@sha256:9b2c… -o cyclonedx-json > checkout.sbom.json
 ✔ Cataloged contents   [312 packages]

$ grype sbom:checkout.sbom.json --fail-on high         # compare the inventory with vulnerability databases
NAME        INSTALLED  FIXED-IN  TYPE          VULNERABILITY   SEVERITY
log4j-core  2.14.1     2.15.0    java-archive  CVE-2021-44228  Critical

# Or have the build produce and attach both documents itself
$ docker buildx build --sbom=true --provenance=mode=max -t registry.example.com/checkout:1.8.2 --push .

An SBOM does not make anything safer by existing. Its value is that it turns a question into a query. On the day Log4Shell was announced, the question in every company was "which of our services contain log4j-core older than 2.15, and where are they running?" Teams with SBOMs for their deployed images answered in minutes. Teams without them spent days unpacking archives and asking around. Scanning the SBOM continuously, and not only at build time, matters for the same reason: the image that was clean when you built it in March has a known critical flaw in December, and nothing about the image has changed.

Two cautions. A scanner reports every known flaw in every package, and most are not reachable in your program, so a team needs a rule for what blocks a release (commonly: critical and high severity with a fix available) or the reports are ignored. And an SBOM lists known components with known flaws. It says nothing about a backdoor nobody has discovered yet.

Signing and verification

A signature on an image is a statement, checkable by anyone, that a particular identity vouches for a particular digest. The traditional difficulty was the private key: a long-lived signing key is one more secret to leak (Chapter 28). Sigstore, with its tool cosign, removes the key. This is called keyless signing.

$ cosign sign registry.example.com/checkout@sha256:9b2c…          # in CI: always sign a digest, never a tag

$ cosign verify registry.example.com/checkout@sha256:9b2c… \
    --certificate-identity "https://github.com/example/checkout/.github/workflows/release.yml@refs/heads/main" \
    --certificate-oidc-issuer "https://token.actions.githubusercontent.com"
Verification for registry.example.com/checkout@sha256:9b2c… --
The following checks were performed on each of these signatures:
  - The cosign claims were validated
  - Existence of the claims in the transparency log was verified offline
  - The code-signing certificate was verified using trusted certificate authority certificates

Two points decide whether signing is worth anything. First, a signature nobody checks is decoration. The check belongs at admission to the cluster, where a policy rejects any image without a valid signature from the expected identity (Chapter 36 shows the policy). Second, be exact about what is proved. A signature says this pipeline produced this digest. It does not say the contents are good. If the pipeline was fed poisoned code, it signs the poison faithfully.

Pinning actions and images

A build is only repeatable if its inputs cannot change behind your back. Pinning means naming each input by its content and not by a label that someone else can move.

# .github/workflows/release.yml
permissions: {}                                   # start from nothing; grant per job
jobs:
  release:
    runs-on: ubuntu-24.04
    permissions:
      contents: read
      packages: write
      id-token: write                              # for keyless signing and provenance
      attestations: write
    steps:
      # A tag such as @v4 is a label its owner, or a thief, can move. A commit hash cannot be moved.
      # Look hashes up in the action's own repository; never copy one from a tutorial, this one included.
      - uses: actions/checkout@11bd71901bbe5b1630ceea73d27597364c9af683   # v4.2.2
      - uses: docker/build-push-action@<full 40-character commit hash>     # v6.x
        id: build
        with: { push: true, tags: "registry.example.com/checkout:${{ github.sha }}" }
      - uses: actions/attest-build-provenance@<full commit hash>
        with:
          subject-name: registry.example.com/checkout
          subject-digest: ${{ steps.build.outputs.digest }}
          push-to-registry: true
# Dockerfile: the base image by digest, with the tag kept for the reader
FROM python:3.13-slim@sha256:4c2c…e91a

# Dependencies: install exactly what the lockfile records, and check each file's hash
$ npm ci                                          # fails if package-lock.json and package.json disagree
$ pip install --require-hashes -r requirements.txt
$ go mod verify
  • Actions by commit hash. When the tags of a popular action were moved to a malicious commit in 2025, every workflow that used @v45 ran the attacker's code on its next run, and every workflow pinned to a hash did not. Chapter 48 is that story.
  • Images by digest, both the base image in the Dockerfile and the image named in the deployment (Chapter 9).
  • Packages by lockfile with hashes. A lockfile records the exact version and checksum of every package, direct or not. A new upstream release changes nothing until you choose to update.
  • Internal package names need a namespace or a registry setting that never falls back to the public registry, which is the fix for dependency confusion.

Pinning has a cost: pinned things do not receive fixes. So pinning comes with an update robot (Dependabot or Renovate) that opens a pull request for each new version, with the change visible and the tests run. Updates then arrive as reviewed changes on your schedule, not as silent changes on somebody else's. Many teams add a waiting period of a few days for new releases, because malicious versions are usually reported and removed within days.

SLSA

Signing proves who produced an artefact. Provenance says how: a signed document, generated by the build platform and not by the build script, recording which source repository and commit were built, by which builder, with which workflow and inputs. With provenance a cluster can enforce a much stronger rule than "is it signed?", namely "was this image built from our repository's main branch by our release workflow on the hosted builder?"

SLSA (pronounced "salsa", Supply-chain Levels for Software Artifacts) is a framework, started at Google and now run under the Open Source Security Foundation, that grades how much you can trust a build.

Build levelRequirementWhat it defends against
L1Provenance exists, describing how the artefact was builtMistakes: you can tell what a given artefact is. Easy to forge
L2The build runs on a hosted platform that generates and signs the provenanceTampering after the build, and artefacts built on someone's laptop
L3The platform is hardened: each build is isolated and short-lived, and build steps cannot reach the signing materialTampering during the build, including by another build or a compromised step
 
$ gh attestation verify oci://registry.example.com/checkout@sha256:9b2c… --repo example/checkout
✓ Verification succeeded!
  - Build repo:      example/checkout
  - Build workflow:  .github/workflows/release.yml@refs/heads/main
  - Signer workflow: .github/workflows/release.yml@refs/heads/main

The practical reading: build on a hosted, short-lived runner and not on a long-lived machine that somebody can log in to; let the platform issue provenance; verify it before deploying. The level numbers are useful as a shared vocabulary, with suppliers and auditors especially, and less important than doing those three things.

Which attacks get through?Interactive
    An attack is stopped if at least one ticked defence prevents it, and limited if none prevents it but at least one reduces it. The rules are simplified to the main effect of each control: real attackers chain several of these steps, and a real control can be misconfigured.

    Things to try.

    • Nothing ticked: all 8 get through. Read the "would stop it" line of each: it is the map of this chapter.
    • Signing only: 2 stopped, 6 through. Signing with verification stops the swapped image and the image that never came from your pipeline, and nothing that happens before or during the build.
    • Pinning only (lockfile, action hashes, image digests): 3 stopped, 1 limited, 4 through. Pinning protects your inputs. It does nothing about your own build machine, your own repository or your own cluster.
    • One defence at a time. Provenance from an isolated builder is the strongest single control, stopping 3. Digests and signing stop 2 each, action hashes and branch protection 1 each. The lockfile, SBOMs and least-privilege tokens stop nothing by themselves: they only limit.
    • Four defences stop every attack that can be stopped: action hashes, image digests, verified provenance and a protected branch give 6 of 8. No other set of four does, and all 16 of the 256 combinations that reach 6 contain those four.
    • Add SBOMs with scanning to those four and nothing gets through unopposed: 6 stopped, 2 limited. That is the smallest set, five controls, with a zero in the "get through" box.
    • Everything ticked is still 6 stopped and 2 limited. In none of the 256 combinations is the malicious dependency or the newly announced flaw stopped. For those two the work is making the damage small and the discovery fast.
    • Tick signing and branch protection, and read the build-machine row. It gets through, with a note on why neither helps. That row is SolarWinds.

    Use case: SolarWinds, and the signature that was genuine

    SolarWinds made Orion, a network monitoring product installed deep inside thousands of companies and government agencies. In 2019 attackers got into the company's build environment and placed a small implant on the build servers. It did one thing. When the build of Orion started, it replaced a single source file with a version containing a backdoor, let the compiler run, and put the original back. The source repository stayed clean, so code review had nothing to see. The build produced an official release, and the release process signed it with SolarWinds' real certificate. From March 2020 the backdoored updates went out through the normal update channel, and roughly 18,000 customers installed one. The attackers then chose a much smaller number of them, including US government departments, to break into further. It was discovered in December 2020, when the security firm FireEye investigated a theft of its own tools and traced it back to its Orion server. The lesson shaped everything in this chapter. The customers did everything they had been told: they installed signed updates from their vendor. The signature was valid, because signing proves who published a thing and nothing about what happened inside the build. Provenance from an isolated, short-lived builder, builds that can be reproduced and compared, and SLSA itself are the industry's answer to that one swapped file.

    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Third-party actions referenced by tag or branch. You run whatever that label points to today.
    • Signing images and never verifying them. Without an admission check a signature changes nothing.
    • Verifying "is it signed?" without checking by whom. Anyone can sign an image with their own identity.
    • Treating a signature as proof of safety. It is proof of origin.
    • Pinning everything and never updating. Pins without an update robot become a museum of known flaws.
    • Generating SBOMs and filing them. Their use is the query on the day a flaw is announced, and the continuous scan before it.
    • Installing without the lockfile (npm install in CI where npm ci was meant), so the build quietly takes newer versions.
    • A long-lived build server that people log in to. It is the most valuable machine you own and the least watched.
    • CI jobs with broad, long-lived credentials. A hostile dependency runs with whatever the job can reach.
    • Merging the update robot's pull requests unread, which turns it into a delivery service for whatever was published upstream.

    Interview drill

    Explain it simply: What does signing a container image prove, and what does it not?

    How a strong answer sounds
    A signature binds an identity to an image digest. If the cluster verifies it at admission and checks which identity signed, then only images produced by our release pipeline can run, which stops a swapped image in the registry and stops someone deploying an image built elsewhere. It does not prove the image is safe. The pipeline signs whatever it built, so a malicious dependency, a compromised action or tampering during the build all end up with a valid signature. SolarWinds' backdoored updates were correctly signed. That gap is what provenance and hardened builders address: they record and constrain how the artefact was built, not just who published it. And a signature that nothing verifies proves nothing to anyone.

    Deeper: A critical vulnerability is announced in a widely used library. Walk through your first day.

    How a strong answer sounds
    The first question is where we are exposed, and the answer should come from data, not from asking teams. I query the SBOMs of the images that are actually running, not just the latest builds, for the affected package and version range, including indirect dependencies. That gives a list of services, which I sort by exposure: internet-facing and handling untrusted input first. While that runs, I look for a mitigation that does not need a rebuild, such as a configuration flag, a WAF rule or blocking outbound connections, because it can be applied in minutes. Next, the fix: bump the version in the lockfile, rebuild, and let the normal pipeline test and roll it out, fastest for the exposed services. I also check vendors' products and base images, which my lockfiles do not cover, and I check whether we were already attacked, by searching logs for the exploit's signature from the time the flaw became public or earlier. Then I add the scanner rule so that no new build reintroduces the old version, and I track the long tail, because the last five per cent of services take most of the time. Afterwards the honest review question is how long the inventory step took. If it took days, the action item is SBOMs generated at build time and scanned continuously against what is deployed.

    🧠 Key takeaways

    • The chain has five links: source, dependencies, build, artefact, deploy. Each needs its own control.
    • An SBOM is an inventory. It makes "where do we run this?" a query, and feeds continuous scanning.
    • Keyless signing ties an image digest to a CI identity with no stored key. Verify at admission, and check the identity.
    • A signature proves origin, not safety. Poison fed to the pipeline is signed faithfully.
    • Pin actions by commit hash, images by digest and packages by lockfile, and let a robot propose updates.
    • Provenance records how an artefact was built. SLSA levels grade how hard that record is to fake.
    • No control stops a malicious dependency outright. Limit it: lockfiles, waiting periods, scanning and CI jobs with little to steal.

    Check your understanding

    A workflow uses some-org/deploy-action@v3. Why is this a risk?

    A tag is a movable label. Pinning to the full commit hash fixes the exact code, and an update robot proposes new hashes for review.

    Malware on a build server changes a source file during compilation and restores it afterwards. The release is signed as usual. What does the signature tell a customer?

    This is the SolarWinds case. The signature was genuine. Provenance from an isolated, short-lived builder is the control aimed at tampering during the build.

    What is the main practical use of an SBOM?

    It is an inventory. When a flaw is announced, the inventory answers "where do we run it?" in minutes, and continuous scanning asks that question every day.

    In keyless signing with Sigstore, what replaces the long-lived private key?

    The job proves its identity, receives a short-lived certificate, signs with a one-off key and discards it. Verification checks the identity and the transparency log.

    A team pins every dependency, action and base image, and has no automated updates. What happens over the next year?

    Pinning stops surprise changes, good and bad. It has to be paired with a robot that proposes each update as a reviewable pull request.
