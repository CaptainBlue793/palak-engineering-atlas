Operating a Service Mesh

A service mesh puts a proxy next to every service and routes all traffic between services through those proxies. In exchange you get encryption and verified identity on every connection, the same metrics for every call, and control over retries, timeouts and routing, none of which needs a change to application code. You also get a new distributed system to run, one that sits in the path of every request you serve. How a mesh works on the wire is covered in the Networks course. This chapter is about living with one: whether to adopt it, how to turn on mutual TLS without breaking production, how to set traffic policy, how to debug a request that now passes through two extra proxies, and what it all costs.

  • Say what a mesh provides and judge whether a system needs one
  • Roll out mutual TLS in stages, from permissive to strict, without an outage
  • Predict which calls succeed, fail or stay unencrypted during a partial rollout
  • Set timeouts, retries, outlier detection and traffic splits as mesh policy
  • Debug failures through sidecars using proxy status, access-log flags and configuration dumps
  • Estimate the resource, latency and operational cost of a mesh

Why a mesh

Without a mesh, every service implements its own TLS, its own retries and timeouts, and its own metrics, in its own language and with its own bugs. A mesh moves those concerns out of the services and into a uniform layer. It has two parts. The data plane is the proxies: one per pod as a sidecar, or shared per node in the newer sidecarless designs. The control plane (istiod in Istio, the destination and identity controllers in Linkerd) issues certificates to the proxies and pushes them their configuration.

A mesh gives youWithout one you wouldWorth it when
Mutual TLS between all services, with certificates issued and rotated automaticallyManage certificates per service, or leave internal traffic in plain textCompliance or a zero-trust policy requires encryption in transit and verified service identity

The honest question is whether those are problems you have. Ten services written by one team in one language, with a good HTTP library and network policies, do not need a mesh, and adopting one will slow that team down. A hundred services across fifteen teams and four languages, with an auditor asking how internal traffic is encrypted, probably do. Start from a requirement, adopt the smallest part of the mesh that meets it (mutual TLS is the usual first), and expand only when the next requirement appears.

mTLS rollout

Mutual TLS is enforced by the server's proxy, and a mesh lets you set how strict it is.

  • PERMISSIVE: the proxy accepts both mutual TLS and plain text. Meshed clients are encrypted; clients without a proxy still work.
  • STRICT: the proxy accepts only mutual TLS. A client that cannot present a mesh certificate is refused.

On the client side nothing needs configuring: a meshed client's proxy uses mutual TLS automatically when the destination has a proxy, and plain text when it does not. Two facts follow that decide every rollout. A service without a proxy is outside the policy altogether: it sends and accepts plain text whatever the namespace says. And a client without a proxy can talk to a meshed server only while that server is permissive. The rollout is therefore a sequence, and its order is the whole skill.

# Step 5 for one namespace: accept only mutual TLS
apiVersion: security.istio.io/v1
kind: PeerAuthentication
metadata: { name: default, namespace: shop }
spec:
  mtls: { mode: STRICT }
---
# Then: only the checkout service's identity may call payments, and only these paths
apiVersion: security.istio.io/v1
kind: AuthorizationPolicy
metadata: { name: payments-callers, namespace: shop }
spec:
  selector: { matchLabels: { app: payments } }
  action: ALLOW
  rules:
    - from: [{ source: { principals: ["cluster.local/ns/shop/sa/checkout"] } }]
      to:   [{ operation: { methods: ["POST"], paths: ["/v1/charges*"] } }]

The principal in that policy is the caller's Kubernetes service account, proved by its certificate. A caller that arrives in plain text has no identity at all, so an identity-based policy rejects it even where plain text is otherwise accepted.

    The rules are Istio's defaults. A proxy on the client uses mutual TLS when the destination has a proxy, and plain text otherwise. A proxy on the server accepts both in PERMISSIVE mode and only mutual TLS in STRICT mode. A server with no proxy enforces nothing. An authorisation policy that names an identity is enforced by the server's proxy and can only be satisfied by a caller that arrived over mutual TLS.

    Things to try. Follow a rollout from the beginning.

    • No proxies. All three calls work in plain text, and the mode switch does nothing: there is no proxy to enforce it.
    • Give only payments a proxy, permissive. Still three plain-text calls, all working. Now switch to STRICT: two of the three calls break at once, because neither caller can offer a certificate. This is what "enable mTLS on the important service first" does.
    • Proxies on web, checkout and payments, permissive. Two calls are encrypted and one is not: the nightly batch job. Nothing is broken, and the plain-text tile is telling you who is left.
    • Switch that to STRICT. The two meshed calls are unaffected and legacy-batch → payments is refused. In production this is the outage that arrives the night after the change, when the job runs.
    • Bring legacy-batch into the mesh. Three of three encrypted, and STRICT is safe.
    • Add the authorisation policy. The batch job is denied again, this time with a 403: it proved who it is, and it is not checkout. Take its proxy away in PERMISSIVE mode and it is still denied, for a different reason: a plain-text caller has no identity to check.
    • Remove the proxy from payments under STRICT. Nothing breaks, and nothing is protected: both calls to payments are plain text. Of the sixteen ways to place the four proxies, STRICT breaks nothing in seven, and in six of those that is only because payments itself has no proxy.

    Traffic policies

    Once every call passes through proxies, the behaviour of calls becomes configuration. The settings below are the ones that earn their keep; the Networks course explains what each does on the wire.

    apiVersion: networking.istio.io/v1
kind: VirtualService
metadata: { name: payments, namespace: shop }
spec:
  hosts: [payments]
  http:
    - timeout: 2s                           # the caller never waits longer than this, retries included
      retries:
        attempts: 2
        perTryTimeout: 800ms
        retryOn: connect-failure,refused-stream,503   # never retry what may already have happened
      route:
        - { destination: { host: payments, subset: stable }, weight: 95 }
        - { destination: { host: payments, subset: canary }, weight: 5 }
---
apiVersion: networking.istio.io/v1
kind: DestinationRule
metadata: { name: payments, namespace: shop }
spec:
  host: payments
  subsets:
    - { name: stable, labels: { version: stable } }
    - { name: canary, labels: { version: canary } }
  trafficPolicy:
    connectionPool: { http: { http2MaxRequests: 200, maxRequestsPerConnection: 100 } }
    outlierDetection:                       # stop sending to a pod that keeps failing
      consecutive5xxErrors: 5
      interval: 10s
      baseEjectionTime: 30s
      maxEjectionPercent: 50

    Three cautions go with this power.

    • Retries multiply. Two attempts at each of four layers is up to sixteen requests at the bottom for one at the top, arriving exactly when the bottom is struggling. Retry at one layer, usually the outermost, cap the total with a timeout, and retry only failures that are safe to repeat.
    • Mesh retries and application retries stack. If the client library already retries three times, the mesh's two turn that into six. Decide which layer owns the behaviour and switch the other off.
    • Defaults differ between meshes and versions. Know what yours does when you configure nothing, because that is what most of your services are running with.

    Debugging through sidecars

    A request from web to checkout now makes four hops: the application, its own proxy, the destination's proxy, the destination application. When it fails, the first job is to find which hop failed, and the proxies record enough to tell you.

    $ istioctl proxy-status                                  # has every proxy received the current configuration?
NAME                              CDS      LDS      EDS      RDS      ISTIOD
checkout-7c9d8b6f4-x2kfp.shop     SYNCED   SYNCED   SYNCED   SYNCED   istiod-6f9c
payments-5f6b9c8d7-m8qzt.shop     SYNCED   SYNCED   STALE    SYNCED   istiod-6f9c   # this one is behind

$ istioctl analyze -n shop                               # configuration that cannot work: a route to a subset that does not exist
$ istioctl x describe pod checkout-7c9d8b6f4-x2kfp -n shop   # which policies and routes apply to this pod
$ istioctl proxy-config clusters deploy/web -n shop | grep checkout   # what this proxy believes about a destination
$ kubectl logs deploy/web -n shop -c istio-proxy | tail -3            # the proxy's access log
[2026-10-07T10:42:07Z] "GET /cart HTTP/1.1" 503 UF upstream_reset_before_response_started{connection_failure} - 0 91 3004 - "checkout.shop.svc.cluster.local" "10.244.2.9:8080"
    Response flagMeaningUsual cause
    UFUpstream connection failureThe destination refused or reset: a strict-mode mismatch, the application not listening, a pod going away
    UHNo healthy upstreamNo ready endpoints, or all of them ejected by outlier detection
    NRNo route configuredA VirtualService that matches nothing, or a destination this proxy has no configuration for
    UTUpstream request timeoutThe timeout in the policy is shorter than the service's real latency
    UOUpstream overflowThe connection pool's circuit breaker is open
    DCDownstream connection terminatedThe caller gave up first: its own timeout was shorter
    URXRetry limit exceededEvery attempt failed

    Two failures are particular to sidecars and worth recognising. At start-up, an application that makes calls before its proxy is ready sees connection errors for a second or two; at shutdown, a proxy that exits before the application has finished drops its last requests. And a Job whose proxy keeps running never completes. Current Kubernetes versions fix both by running the proxy as a native sidecar, a container that starts before the application and stops after it. Sidecarless meshes avoid the question by not having one.

    Cost

    CostRough sizeWhat drives it
    MemoryTens of megabytes per proxy: 500 pods at 60 MB is 30 GB of the cluster doing nothing but proxyingThe number of pods, and how much of the mesh's configuration each proxy is sent
    CPUA fraction of a core per proxy per thousand requests a secondTraffic, TLS, and the telemetry you ask for
    LatencyA few milliseconds per call at the 99th percentile, twice per hopTwo proxies per call; a request that crosses five services pays it five times
    UpgradesEvery pod restarted to get a new proxy version, several times a yearThe mesh's release cycle and its security fixes
    Failure modesNew ones: stale configuration, expired certificates, a control plane outageProxies keep working with their last configuration if the control plane is down, and nothing new can start
    PeopleThe largest costSomeone has to own it, understand it and be on call for it

    You can shrink the first rows: restrict each proxy's configuration to the services it actually calls, and consider a sidecarless data plane, which shares one proxy per node. The last rows do not shrink. A mesh is in the request path of everything, so a mistake in it is an outage of everything.

    🚗
    Use case: Lyft and the proxy that became Envoy

    The proxy inside most service meshes was written at Lyft, for reasons that had little to do with security. Around 2015 Lyft was partway through splitting a large PHP application into services written in several languages, and its engineers were, by their own account, afraid of the network. Calls between services failed intermittently and nobody could say where. Each language had its own HTTP client with its own retry and timeout behaviour, or none. A slow dependency could exhaust its callers. Matt Klein and his colleagues decided to solve the problem once, outside the applications: a small, fast proxy running beside every service, through which all traffic in and out would pass, so that load balancing, retries, timeouts, circuit breaking and, above all, consistent statistics and tracing were the same for every service whatever it was written in. Lyft released it as open source in 2016 under the name Envoy. The stated design goal has been quoted ever since: the network should be transparent to applications, and when problems do occur it should be easy to work out where. Istio, and most meshes after it, were built by adding a control plane to that proxy. It is worth remembering what the original problem was. The first value of a mesh at Lyft was not encryption or policy. It was being able to see what every service was doing to every other.

    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Adopting a mesh with no requirement in mind, then paying its costs for features nobody uses.
    • Switching to STRICT before checking who still sends plain text. The forgotten client breaks in production.
    • Believing STRICT covers everything in the namespace. A pod without a proxy is not enforced at all.
    • Retries at every layer. One slow service receives many times its normal load at the worst moment.
    • Mesh timeouts shorter than the application's work, or longer than the caller's own timeout.
    • Every proxy receiving the whole mesh's configuration. Memory and propagation time grow with the square of the cluster.
    • Reading only the application's logs. The answer to "which hop failed" is in the proxies' access logs.
    • Treating mesh upgrades as optional. The proxy is security-critical software in every pod.
    • No owner. A mesh installed by a team that then moved on is the worst thing to be on call for.

    Interview drill

    Explain it simply: What does a service mesh do, and when would you not use one?

    How a strong answer sounds
    A service mesh routes all service-to-service traffic through proxies, one beside each workload, managed by a control plane. That gives mutual TLS with automatically rotated certificates and a verified identity for every caller, uniform metrics and traces for every call, and traffic policy such as timeouts, retries, outlier ejection and weighted routing, all without changing application code. I would not use one when I do not have the problems it solves. A small number of services owned by one team, in one language with a decent client library, get little from it and pay for it in memory per pod, added latency, a regular upgrade burden and a new component that can take everything down. I would adopt it for a concrete requirement, typically encryption and identity between services across many teams, and start with only that.

    Deeper: You are asked to enforce mutual TLS for every service in a production cluster of 200 services. How do you do it without an outage?

    How a strong answer sounds
    In stages, with measurement between them. First I set the mesh-wide policy to permissive, so that injecting proxies changes nothing about who can connect. Then I inject sidecars namespace by namespace, starting with low-risk ones, through ordinary rolling restarts, watching error rates and latency, because the proxies themselves can introduce problems such as start-up ordering or protocol detection. Once a namespace is meshed, its proxies report whether each inbound connection used mutual TLS, so I can list exactly which sources still arrive in plain text. Those are the ones that would break: jobs and cron jobs, workloads in namespaces not yet meshed, virtual machines, ingress controllers, monitoring scrapers, health checkers. Each is either brought into the mesh, routed through a gateway that originates mutual TLS, or given a narrow, documented exception on a specific port. Only when a namespace shows no plain-text inbound traffic for a full business cycle, including the monthly batch runs, do I switch that namespace to strict, again starting where a mistake is cheapest, with a one-line rollback ready. After all namespaces, the mesh-wide default becomes strict. Then I add identity-based authorisation policies, first in audit or dry-run mode. Throughout, I would alert on plain-text connections and on certificate expiry, and remember that strict mode only binds workloads that have a proxy, so an admission policy should require injection.

    🧠 Key takeaways

    • A mesh moves TLS, identity, telemetry and traffic policy out of services into proxies and a control plane.
    • Adopt it for a requirement you have, and begin with the smallest part that meets it.
    • Roll out mutual TLS in order: permissive, inject, find the plain-text clients, fix or exempt them, then strict by namespace.
    • Strict mode is enforced by the server's proxy. Workloads without a proxy are outside it, and clients without one are refused.
    • Identity-based authorisation needs mutual TLS: a plain-text caller has no identity.
    • Retries multiply across layers. Give one layer the job and bound it with a timeout.
    • Debug by hop: proxy status, the access-log response flag in the right proxy, then the proxy's configuration.
    • The mesh is in the path of every request. Its largest cost is the people who must understand it.

    Check your understanding

    A namespace is switched to STRICT mutual TLS. A cron job without a sidecar calls a meshed service in that namespace. What happens?

    Strict mode is enforced on the server side. A client with no proxy has no certificate to offer, so its plain-text connection is rejected.

    During a migration, why is PERMISSIVE mode used first?

    Permissive accepts both. It lets proxies be rolled out gradually and exposes the remaining plain-text callers before anything is enforced.

    A pod in a STRICT namespace has no sidecar. Which statement is true about traffic to that pod?

    The policy is carried out by proxies. A workload without one is simply outside the mesh, whatever the namespace's policy says.

    Each of four layers of services retries a failed call twice (three attempts in total). The bottom service starts failing. How many requests can it receive for one request at the top?

    Three attempts at each of four layers multiply: 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 = 81. That is why retries belong at one layer, with a budget.

    The calling proxy's access log shows a 503 with the flag UH. Where do you look?

    UH is "no healthy upstream". The proxy had a route and nowhere healthy to send the request.
