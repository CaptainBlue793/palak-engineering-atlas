Case Study: A Kubernetes Outage

On 14 March 2023 Reddit was unreachable for 314 minutes, a figure that earned the incident its nickname, the Pi-Day outage, since the date in American form is 3/14. Nothing dramatic had been attempted. Engineers were carrying out a routine Kubernetes version upgrade of a kind they had done many times. The upgrade itself worked. What failed was a single line of network configuration written four years earlier, which depended on a label that the new version no longer applied. This case study follows Reddit's own published account: the change, the way one missing label became a total outage, why it was so hard to diagnose, the difficult recovery, and what the company changed afterwards. The simulator lets you run the same upgrade with different guardrails in place.

The change

Reddit ran its main site on a number of Kubernetes clusters. The one in this story was among the oldest: built by hand about four years before, grown and patched ever since, and unlike any other the company had. Engineers call such a system a pet: it has a history and quirks, and it cannot simply be recreated from a description. The newer clusters were built from standard definitions; this one predated them.

The work planned for 14 March was to upgrade that cluster from Kubernetes 1.23 to 1.24. Kubernetes releases a new minor version about three times a year and supports each for about a year, so upgrades are routine and unavoidable. The team had a procedure and had used it for previous versions. The control-plane nodes were upgraded, and within minutes the site began to fail, and then went down altogether.

Version 1.24 contained many changes, and one of them was the end of a long deprecation. For years the nodes that run the control plane had carried the label node-role.kubernetes.io/master. In version 1.20 the project introduced a replacement, node-role.kubernetes.io/control-plane, and applied both. In 1.24 the old label was removed.

$ kubectl get nodes -l node-role.kubernetes.io/master          # on 1.23
NAME     STATUS   ROLES                  VERSION
cp-1     Ready    control-plane,master   v1.23.17
cp-2     Ready    control-plane,master   v1.23.17
cp-3     Ready    control-plane,master   v1.23.17

$ kubectl get nodes -l node-role.kubernetes.io/master          # after the upgrade to 1.24
No resources found

Nothing in the upgrade reported a problem, because from Kubernetes' point of view there was none. Every component it manages had been told about the change years in advance. The label was removed exactly as documented.

The cascade

The cluster's pod network was provided by Calico, in a mode where nodes tell one another which pod addresses they host by exchanging routes over BGP. In a large cluster it is impractical for every node to talk to every other, so a few nodes act as route reflectors: every node peers with them, and they pass everyone's routes on. In this cluster the route reflectors were the control-plane nodes, and the configuration found them by label.

# Calico configuration, written when the cluster was built
apiVersion: projectcalico.org/v3
kind: BGPPeer
metadata: { name: peer-with-route-reflectors }
spec:
  nodeSelector: all()
  peerSelector: has(node-role.kubernetes.io/master)        # "the route reflectors are the master nodes"

When the label disappeared, that selector matched nothing. As far as Calico was concerned, the cluster no longer had any route reflectors, and it did what its configuration said.

The shape of this failure is worth naming, because it recurs. A piece of configuration held a hidden dependency on something its authors did not own: a label that belonged to Kubernetes and that Kubernetes was free to change. The dependency was in a system, the network plugin's configuration, that the upgrade procedure did not look at. And the thing that broke was shared by everything, so the failure was total at once.

Diagnosis

From the outside this incident looks simple, since the cause fits in a sentence. From inside it did not, and understanding why is the most useful part of the story.

What the responders sawWhat it suggestedWhat was true

When a shared foundation fails, every layer above it reports its own kind of error, and each team naturally investigates its own layer. The configuration that mattered had been written years earlier by people who had moved on, lived outside the upgrade's scope, and referred to a label by a name that had only just stopped existing. Nobody had a reason to look there.

This is where the rule from Chapter 37 applies, and where the Reddit team made the decision that ended the outage. They asked what had changed, and the answer was the upgrade. They could not find why the upgrade had broken things. After roughly two hours without a diagnosis, they stopped trying to understand and chose to undo: to restore the cluster to its state before the upgrade. Mitigation did not need the root cause. In fact the cause was only worked out afterwards, once the site was back.

Recovery

"Undo the upgrade" is easily said. Kubernetes does not support downgrading a control plane in place, so undoing meant restoring from backup: rebuilding the control-plane nodes on the old version and loading the cluster's state from a snapshot of etcd taken before the change.

Backups existed. The procedure to restore them did too, on paper. What did not exist was any recent experience of doing it on this cluster. The documented steps had been written for an earlier state of the system and no longer matched it. The responders met mismatched certificates, host details that had changed since the instructions were written, and components that had to be brought back in a particular order which had to be rediscovered under pressure. Each obstacle was small. Together they turned a restore into several hours of careful improvisation, on a production cluster, with the site down. When it was finished the cluster was back on 1.23, the old label was back on its nodes, Calico found its route reflectors, and the network returned. Total time: 314 minutes.

Two things deserve notice. First, the team got its service back by a route that did not depend on understanding the failure, which is exactly why you keep such a route. Second, that route had never been walked. An untested restore procedure is a guess, and here it cost most of the outage. The diagnosis took about two hours; the recovery, once chosen, took longer.

Guardrails added

Reddit's published review is frank about its conclusions, and they generalise well beyond one company.

# Before upgrading: what does the new version remove, and do we mention it anywhere?
$ grep -rn "node-role.kubernetes.io/master" infrastructure/ cluster-addons/ helm-values/
cluster-addons/calico/bgp-peer.yaml:7:  peerSelector: has(node-role.kubernetes.io/master)

$ pluto detect-all-in-cluster          # finds API versions that the next release removes
$ kubectl get bgppeers,ippools -o yaml | grep -n "node-role"    # and look inside the add-ons' own configuration

Tools such as pluto and kubent catch one class of upgrade problem, the use of API versions that are about to be removed. They would not have caught this one, because a label inside a network plugin's selector is not an API version. No tool replaces reading the release notes with your own configuration open beside them.

    Things to try.

    • The starting settings are the real incident: the old label, every control-plane node at once, a staging cluster with a simpler network, and a restore never tried. Route reflectors go from 3 to 0 and the site is down for 314 minutes.
    • Change only the restore to "practised": 180 minutes. Nothing was prevented, and 134 minutes were saved. That is what a restore drill is worth here.
    • Change only the method to "one node at a time": the upgrade stops at 2 of 3 route reflectors with the network working, and nobody outside the team knows.
    • Change only the method to "a new cluster": the new cluster never works and never receives traffic. With "tested first on nothing", this is still zero minutes of outage.
    • Change only the rehearsal to "the same network setup": the failure happens there. The staging cluster with a simpler network gave a clean rehearsal and no protection.
    • Change only the selector: nothing happens, with either replacement. Read the two explanations: one of them is still borrowing a name from Kubernetes.
    • Of the 54 combinations, 36 use a selector that survives the upgrade. Of the 18 that do not, 6 are caught in rehearsal, 4 on the new cluster, 4 after the first node, and only 4 reach users. An outage like this one needs every guardrail to be missing at the same time.

    Use case: Datadog, the same month, and an update nobody scheduled

    Six days before Reddit's outage, on 8 March 2023, the monitoring company Datadog suffered the largest incident in its history, and the mechanism was a close relative. Datadog ran tens of thousands of Kubernetes nodes across several cloud providers and regions. The nodes' operating system was configured, by default, to install security updates automatically. That morning an update to systemd, the Linux service manager, reached the fleet, and as part of applying it the network service on each machine was restarted. On restart it removed network routes that it did not recognise as its own, and those included the routes that the cluster's network plugin had installed to let pods talk to one another. Node after node dropped off its cluster's network. Because the update arrived on a schedule tied to the machines and not to any deployment process, it struck across regions and providers within about an hour, defeating the redundancy that separate regions were meant to give. Recovery took more than a day, partly because the clusters' own control planes ran on the affected nodes, and the company later put the cost in lost revenue at around five million dollars. Datadog's public review reached conclusions very like Reddit's. The trigger was a change that no engineer had made or reviewed. It broke a shared foundation, the node network, through an interaction between two components that each behaved correctly. And the fix was to bring that class of change under the same staged, observable rollout as everything else: automatic updates were switched off, and base-image changes now travel region by region.

    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Reading release notes for new features and skipping the removals.
    • Checking the workloads' manifests and forgetting the add-ons: network plugin, ingress controller, storage driver, their own custom resources.
    • Depending on labels, annotations or names that another project owns.
    • A hand-built cluster that nobody could rebuild. It will be upgraded rarely, differently and fearfully.
    • A staging cluster that differs from production in architecture, so a clean rehearsal means nothing.
    • Upgrading every control-plane node in one step.
    • No health check of the network between upgrade steps, only of the component being upgraded.
    • Assuming a downgrade is possible. For a control plane it is a restore.
    • Backups without restore drills.
    • Diagnosing indefinitely. Set a time limit, and when it passes, take the route back.
    • Automatic operating-system updates on production nodes, outside any staged rollout.

    Interview drill

    Explain it simply: How do you upgrade a Kubernetes cluster safely?

    How a strong answer sounds
    I prepare, rehearse, and then go in small reversible steps. Preparation means reading the release notes for removals and behaviour changes, and checking them against everything in the cluster: workloads for removed API versions, with a tool such as pluto, and the add-ons and their configuration by hand, because the network plugin, ingress controller and storage driver are where surprises hide. I rehearse on a cluster that really resembles production, including its network setup. Before starting I take an etcd snapshot and make sure the restore has been practised. Then I upgrade one minor version at a time, control plane first, one node at a time, checking cluster health between each, including the network and DNS, not only the upgraded component. After that the worker nodes, by replacing them in batches with pods drained gracefully and disruption budgets respected. If I have the choice, I prefer building a new cluster on the new version and shifting traffic to it gradually, because the old cluster stays untouched as the way back. With a managed control plane some of this is done for me, but the add-ons and the node upgrade remain mine.

    Deeper: Right after a control-plane upgrade, services across the cluster start timing out and DNS lookups fail. Pods show as Running. What do you do?

    How a strong answer sounds
    The timing says the upgrade is the trigger even if I cannot see how, so I treat that as known and start two tracks. The first is mitigation: what is my way back, and how long does it take? A control plane cannot be downgraded in place, so the way back is a restore from the pre-upgrade snapshot, or moving traffic to another cluster if we have one. I would set a time limit for diagnosis now, something like thirty minutes for an outage this size, and prepare the way back in parallel so that it is ready when the limit passes. The second track is narrowing the fault. Widespread timeouts with DNS failing and pods Running points below the applications, at pod networking. I would test it directly: from a debug pod, can I reach a pod on the same node, and one on another node, by IP address? If same-node works and cross-node does not, the pod network's routing is broken and DNS is only a victim. Then I look at the network plugin itself: are its pods healthy, what do its logs say, and for a BGP-based setup, are the sessions established and are routes present on the nodes? I would then ask what the upgrade changed that the plugin could depend on: node labels, taints, API versions of its custom resources, kube-proxy or CNI configuration. Comparing a node's labels before and after the upgrade is quick, and searching the plugin's configuration for anything that selects by label would find a case like Reddit's. If I find it, the fix may be a one-line change that is faster than the restore. If the time limit passes first, I stop and restore. Afterwards the review has two questions: how did a dependency like that go unnoticed, and why was the way back not faster.

    🧠 Key takeaways

    • An upgrade can break things it never touches, through configuration that depends on what it changes.
    • Failures in a shared foundation show up as different symptoms in every layer above. Look low.
    • Do not depend on labels and names you do not own. Read every release's removals against all your configuration, add-ons included.
    • Rehearse on a true copy, upgrade in small steps with health checks, or replace the cluster and shift traffic.
    • Pet clusters are upgraded rarely and dangerously. Build clusters from a definition.
    • Limit the time spent diagnosing. Mitigation does not need the root cause.
    • A restore that has never been practised is the slowest part of an outage.

    Check your understanding

    In the Pi-Day outage, what did the Kubernetes upgrade actually do wrong?

    The upgrade behaved as designed. The fault was a hidden dependency in configuration that nobody had checked against the release's removals.

    Pods are Running, DNS lookups fail across the cluster, and a pod can reach other pods on its own node but not on other nodes. Where is the fault most likely to be?

    Same-node traffic working and cross-node traffic failing isolates the routing between nodes. DNS fails because most pods cannot reach it.

    Why did the recovery take longer than the diagnosis?

    Backups existed. The steps to restore them had to be rediscovered under pressure, which is what restore drills prevent.

    Which practice would have kept this failure away from users even with the faulty configuration in place?

    A new cluster with a broken network would never have passed its checks or received traffic, and the old cluster would have carried on serving.

    What is the general lesson about selecting nodes by the label node-role.kubernetes.io/master?

    A label you define, such as one marking route reflectors, changes only when you change it.
