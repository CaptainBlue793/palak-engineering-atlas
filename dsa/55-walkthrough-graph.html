Walkthrough: A Hard Graph Problem

This chapter takes everything from the graph chapters and applies it to one problem, starting from scratch. The problem is Swim in Rising Water. It was chosen on purpose: the obvious tool is almost right, three quite different solutions all work, and the trap is a single word in what you are asked to minimise. The point is not the answer. It is watching the reasoning unfold at the pace it would in a real interview.

The problem

Swim in Rising Water. You are given an N × N grid, where grid[i][j] is the height (elevation) of that square. Rain starts, and at time t the water level everywhere is t. You start at (0,0) and want to reach (N-1, N-1). You can move up, down, left or right, and swimming takes no time at all. But you can only be on a square at time t if grid[i][j] ≤ t, and you can only move between two squares if both are under water. Return the earliest time you can reach the destination. Limits: N ≤ 50, and the elevations are a permutation of 0 … N²−1 (every value appears exactly once).

Stage 1 — Read the structure, not the story

1
Strip the narrative and restate it

The rain and the swimming are just decoration. What is left: a grid you can move around in four directions, and a path whose cost is the highest cell value along it. We want the path that makes that highest value as small as possible. Everything else in the statement is flavour.

Say two observations out loud, because both matter later. First, waiting never helps. Swimming is instant and the water only rises. So if you can reach a square at all, you can reach it the moment the water covers the highest square on your route. That means time does not need to be part of the state: the answer depends only on the path. Second, the goal is to minimise the maximum, not the sum. That phrase is the whole problem.

Restated: over all paths from (0,0) to (N-1,N-1) that move up, down, left or right, find the smallest possible value of the highest elevation on the path. This is sometimes called the bottleneck shortest path or the minimax path. Think of a hiking route where you only care about the highest point you have to climb, not how far you walk.

A tiny example makes the difference between "fewest steps", "smallest sum" and "smallest maximum" concrete. Suppose two routes lead from start to goal:

RouteElevations visitedStepsSumHighest point
A (short, over a peak)0 → 7 → 42117
B (long, through low ground)0 → 1 → 2 → 3 → 5 → 45155

BFS picks route A because it has fewer steps. Ordinary Dijkstra (sum of elevations) also picks A, because 11 < 15. But in this problem you have to wait until the water covers every square on your route, so route A costs time 7 and route B costs time 5. The right answer is 5. Each tool answers a slightly different question, and only one of them is the question being asked.

🚫
Why the two reflex answers fail
  • BFS is wrong. BFS finds the path with the fewest steps, and the number of steps has nothing to do with the highest elevation. A long detour through low ground often beats a short hop over a peak. (Worth saying: BFS is only correct when every edge costs the same, and here the cost is not even something you add up.)
  • Grid DP is wrong. You can move in all four directions, so cells depend on each other in circles, and no fill order exists (Chapter 42). If you find yourself wanting to rerun the DP loops until nothing changes, that is Bellman-Ford, and it means you have a graph problem.
  • Plain Dijkstra is almost right, and this is the interesting one. Dijkstra minimises a sum of edge weights. Here we want to minimise a maximum. The good news: the greedy reasoning behind Dijkstra does not actually rely on addition. It only needs one thing: making a path longer never makes its cost smaller. Both sum and max have that property, so Dijkstra works with one line changed.

Stage 2 — The brute force, and the redundancy

2
State the O(N⁴) version first

Once you have restated the problem, the brute force is easy: for each possible time t from 0 upwards, flood-fill from (0,0) using only cells with elevation ≤ t, and stop at the first t that reaches the destination. Since the elevations are a permutation of 0 … N²−1, there are N² candidate times, and each flood fill costs O(N²). So this is O(N⁴). At N = 50 there are 2,500 cells, so that is 2,500 flood fills of up to 2,500 cells each: about 6 million operations, which actually passes (a typical judge allows roughly 10⁸ simple operations per second). Say so: the brute force is already fast enough. The rest of the discussion is about a cleaner solution, and about what happens if N grows.

Now name the repeated work. Each flood fill explores the whole area it explored last time, plus a little more. There are two separate ways to remove that:

  • The answer is monotone. If you can finish by time t, you can also finish by t+1. So binary search the answer instead of trying every value: O(N² log N²). At N = 50 that is about 12 flood fills (log₂ 2,500 ≈ 11.3) instead of 2,500. Why is it monotone? Raising the water never removes a usable cell, it only adds them, so a route that worked at t still works at t+1.
  • Or never start over at all. Add cells in order of increasing elevation and keep track of what is connected as you go (union-find). Or always explore the lowest cell on the edge of what you have reached so far (Dijkstra).

Solution A — binary search on the answer

binary search the time, flood fill to checkO(N² log N)O(N²)
// Feasibility is MONOTONE in t: if the destination is reachable using only
// cells with elevation <= t, it is also reachable for any larger t, because
// raising t only ever ADDS usable cells. That monotonicity is exactly the
// precondition binary search needs — say it out loud, do not assume it.
bool canReach(int t,vector<vector<int>> &grid){
	int N=grid.size();

	if(grid[0][0]>t)return false;   // cannot even start

	vector<vector<bool>> visited(N,vector<bool>(N,false));
	queue<pair<int,int>> q;

	q.push({0,0});
	visited[0][0]=true;            // mark on ENQUEUE, always

	int dr[]={1,-1,0,0};
	int dc[]={0,0,1,-1};

	while(!q.empty()){
		auto [r,c]=q.front();

		q.pop();

		if(r==N-1 and c==N-1)return true;

		for(int d=0;d<4;d++){
			int nr=r+dr[d];
			int nc=c+dc[d];

			if(nr<0 or nr>=N or nc<0 or nc>=N)continue;
			if(visited[nr][nc])continue;
			if(grid[nr][nc]>t)continue;     // still above water

			visited[nr][nc]=true;
			q.push({nr,nc});
		}
	}

	return false;
}

int swimInWater(vector<vector<int>> &grid){
	int N=grid.size();

	// the answer is at least the higher of the two endpoints, and at most
	// the largest elevation in the grid
	int low=max(grid[0][0],grid[N-1][N-1]);
	int high=N*N-1;

	while(low<high){                   // find the SMALLEST feasible t
		int mid=low+(high-low)/2;

		if(canReach(mid,grid))high=mid;   // feasible -> try smaller
		else low=mid+1;                  // infeasible -> must go higher
	}

	return low;
}
The loop is the "smallest value that works" template from Chapter 7: while(low<high), high=mid when it works, low=mid+1 when it does not, then return low. It has no off-by-one risk because it never needs a final comparison. The raised lower bound is a small but real improvement worth mentioning. No route's highest point can be below either endpoint's own elevation, because both endpoints are always on the path. The cost is O(N²) per check times log(N²) checks, so O(N² log N). (log(N²) = 2 log N, and the constant 2 disappears in big-O.)

A dry run. Take this 3×3 grid, with the goal at the bottom right:

0 5 2
1 7 3
6 8 4

There are three sensible routes: along the top (0,5,2,3,4, highest point 5), through the middle (0,1,7,3,4, highest 7) and down the left (0,1,6,8,4, highest 8). So the answer should be 5. The search runs like this:

  1. low = max(0, 4) = 4, high = 8. mid = 6: the cells ≤ 6 include the whole top route, so it is reachable. Set high = 6.
  2. mid = 5: still reachable along the top. Set high = 5.
  3. mid = 4: the usable cells are 0, 1, 2, 3, 4. From (0,0) you can step down to 1, but every cell next to those two is above 4. The flood fill gets stuck, so set low = 5.
  4. low == high == 5, so the loop ends and returns 5.

Notice that the check at t = 4 failed even though both endpoints were under water. Being able to stand on the start and the goal is not enough; the cells in between must be connected.

Solution B — Dijkstra with one line changed

bottleneck Dijkstra — max instead of plusO(N² log N)O(N²)
// dist[r][c] = the minimum achievable MAXIMUM elevation on any path from
//              (0,0) to (r,c).
// Dijkstra's correctness needs only that extending a path never lowers its
// cost. max(...) satisfies that just as + does, so the algorithm carries over
// unchanged — except for the relaxation itself.
int swimInWater(vector<vector<int>> &grid){
	int N=grid.size();
	vector<vector<int>> dist(N,vector<int>(N,INT_MAX));

	// min-heap of {cost so far, row, col} — SORT KEY FIRST
	priority_queue<tuple<int,int,int>,
	               vector<tuple<int,int,int>>,
	               greater<tuple<int,int,int>>> pq;

	dist[0][0]=grid[0][0];
	pq.push({grid[0][0],0,0});

	int dr[]={1,-1,0,0};
	int dc[]={0,0,1,-1};

	while(!pq.empty()){
		auto [d,r,c]=pq.top();

		pq.pop();

		if(d>dist[r][c])continue;        // a stale heap entry
		if(r==N-1 and c==N-1)return d;  // first pop of the goal is optimal

		for(int k=0;k<4;k++){
			int nr=r+dr[k];
			int nc=c+dc[k];

			if(nr<0 or nr>=N or nc<0 or nc>=N)continue;

			// the ONLY change from textbook Dijkstra:
			int cost=max(d,grid[nr][nc]);   // not d + weight

			if(cost<dist[nr][nc]){
				dist[nr][nc]=cost;
				pq.push({cost,nr,nc});
			}
		}
	}

	return -1;                          // unreachable; cannot happen on a full grid
}
This is the cleanest of the three, and the one I would write. Four details carry over from Chapter 29 and are worth saying:
  • The cost comes first in the tuple, so the default comparison orders by cost.
  • greater makes it a min-heap.
  • The d > dist[r][c] check throws away out-of-date entries. This "lazy deletion" trick means you never need to lower a value already in the heap.
  • The first time the destination is popped, its cost is already the best, so you can return immediately instead of emptying the heap.
The bigger idea is worth naming too. Replace + with any way of combining costs that never goes down as the path grows, and you get "widest path", "most reliable path" (with min, and multiplying probabilities), and this bottleneck version. They are all the same algorithm.

The same 3×3 grid, run through Dijkstra. Heap entries are written (cost, row, col).

  1. Pop (0,0,0). Right neighbour: max(0,5) = 5. Down neighbour: max(0,1) = 1.
  2. Pop (1,1,0), the cheapest. Its neighbours get max(1,7) = 7 (middle) and max(1,6) = 6 (bottom left).
  3. Pop (5,0,1). Right: max(5,2) = 5. The middle would be max(5,7) = 7, no better than what it has.
  4. Pop (5,0,2), then (5,1,2). Each passes cost 5 on, because max(5,3) and max(5,4) are both 5.
  5. Pop (5,2,2). That is the goal, so return 5. The cells with 6, 7 and 8 were never settled.

Step 4 shows what makes max different from +. Once a route has climbed to 5, walking over lower cells costs nothing extra. With a sum, every extra cell would add to the cost, which is why a sum-based Dijkstra prefers short routes and a max-based one does not care about length at all.

Solution C — union-find, adding cells in elevation order

incremental connectivityO(N²·α)O(N²)
// Because the elevations are a permutation of 0 .. N*N-1, "raise the water to
// t" means exactly "activate the cell whose elevation is t". So activate cells
// in increasing elevation order, union each with its already-active
// neighbours, and the answer is the first t at which start and goal are joined.
// Nothing is ever recomputed — that is the redundancy removed completely.
class DSU{
	vector<int> parent,rank;

public:
	DSU(int n){
		parent.resize(n);
		rank.assign(n,0);

		for(int i=0;i<n;i++)
		parent[i]=i;
	}

	int find(int x){
		if(parent[x]==x)return x;

		return parent[x]=find(parent[x]);   // path compression
	}

	bool unite(int a,int b){
		a=find(a);
		b=find(b);

		if(a==b)return false;

		if(rank[a]<rank[b])swap(a,b);

		parent[b]=a;

		if(rank[a]==rank[b])rank[a]++;

		return true;
	}
};

int swimInWater(vector<vector<int>> &grid){
	int N=grid.size();
	vector<int> whereIs(N*N);            // elevation -> flattened index

	for(int r=0;r<N;r++)
	for(int c=0;c<N;c++)
	whereIs[grid[r][c]]=r*N+c;

	DSU dsu(N*N);
	vector<bool> active(N*N,false);
	int dr[]={1,-1,0,0};
	int dc[]={0,0,1,-1};

	for(int t=0;t<N*N;t++){
		int id=whereIs[t];
		int r=id/N;
		int c=id%N;

		active[id]=true;

		for(int k=0;k<4;k++){
			int nr=r+dr[k];
			int nc=c+dc[k];

			if(nr<0 or nr>=N or nc<0 or nc>=N)continue;
			if(!active[nr*N+nc])continue;   // neighbour still dry

			dsu.unite(id,nr*N+nc);
		}

		if(dsu.find(0)==dsu.find(N*N-1))return t;
	}

	return -1;
}
This is the fastest of the three, and it takes advantage of the permutation rule. The key insight being tested is reading "elevations are a permutation of 0 … N²−1" and realising it means "cells switch on one at a time, in an order you already know". Without it, you would have to sort the cells by elevation first. That costs O(N² log N) and puts you back level with the other two.

Note that the connectivity check comes after the unions for time t, so the t you return is the first moment start and goal are joined. This is also the shape of Kruskal's algorithm: process edges from lightest to heaviest and join them. That is no coincidence. The bottleneck path between two nodes always lies on the minimum spanning tree.

What the α means. α is the inverse Ackermann function, the cost of one find with path compression and union by rank. It grows so slowly that it is at most 4 for any input that fits in a computer, so O(N²·α) is "linear in the number of cells" for every practical purpose.

At N = 50 all three finish instantly, so the table is really about what happens when the input grows. At N = 1,000 there are a million cells. The O(N⁴) brute force would need about 10¹² operations, which is far too slow. The three good solutions each need roughly 10⁶ to 2 × 10⁷ operations, which is fine. Past that point the choice between them rests on code clarity and follow-ups, not speed.

I would write the Dijkstra. It is the shortest. It does not rely on the permutation rule, so it survives the follow-up "what if elevations can repeat, or are any 64-bit values?" without changes. And the one-line difference from textbook Dijkstra is easy to explain. I would mention the other two: union-find as the fastest in theory given the permutation, and binary search as the fallback whose correctness is hardest to doubt.

Then check it by tracing. Take the 2×2 grid [[0,2],[1,3]]. Start with dist[0][0]=0. Pop (0,0,0): update right to max(0,2)=2 and down to max(0,1)=1. Pop (1,1,0): update right to max(1,3)=3. Pop (2,0,1): down gives max(2,3)=3, which is no better. Pop (3,1,1): that is the goal, so return 3. Correct, because every route must step on the destination itself, whose elevation is 3.

The tests to volunteer
  • N = 1. The answer is grid[0][0]. Does your code handle it correctly? (In the Dijkstra, (0,0) is the goal and is popped first, so yes.)
  • The destination is the highest cell. [[0,1],[2,3]] → 3. The answer can never be below either endpoint.
  • The start is the highest cell. [[3,0],[1,2]] → 3. This catches the bug of starting with dist[0][0]=0.
  • A forced detour. A grid where the direct route crosses a peak but a longer route stays low. This is the test that breaks a BFS-based attempt, so write it first.
  • Full size, N = 50. Check the running time, and check that no maths is done on INT_MAX.
  • Stress test. Compare against the O(N⁴) brute force on random 3×3 and 4×4 permutation grids. That settles it in seconds (Chapter 53).
🔮
The follow-ups you would actually get
  • "What if elevations can be any values, with repeats?" Dijkstra does not change. Binary search must search over the sorted distinct values instead of 0…N²−1. Union-find must sort the cells first, which costs O(N² log N).
  • "What about diagonal moves too (eight directions)?" Extend the direction arrays to eight entries. Nothing else changes, which is a good reason to keep directions in arrays instead of writing four separate branches.
  • "Return the actual path, not just the time." Store a parent for each cell whenever you improve it, then walk backwards from the destination. This is harder in the union-find version, which is a real argument for Dijkstra.
  • "Now minimise the sum of elevations instead." Change max(d, grid[nr][nc]) back to d + grid[nr][nc], and it becomes ordinary Dijkstra. Neither binary search nor union-find can adapt, because a sum does not behave like a single threshold you can raise. This follow-up is the point of the whole problem.
  • "What if the water can also go down, so timing matters?" Then time becomes part of the state: dist[r][c][t], and the graph gets one layer per time step. This is a good moment to say that adding a dimension is the standard fix when the state does not capture enough (Chapter 38).
  • "Many (start, goal) queries on the same grid?" Build the minimum spanning tree once. The bottleneck between any two nodes is the heaviest edge on the one tree path between them, which binary lifting answers in O(log N). Naming this is enough.

Tips & tricks

The things that are muscle memory rather than theory — what to check first, and the line that is wrong the first time you write it.

💡 STRIP THE STORY
Rain and swimming are decoration; "minimise the maximum along a path" is the problem. Restate in your own words before choosing a tool.
💡 MONOTONE COMBINER
Dijkstra needs only that extending a path never lowers its cost. Swap + for max and you have bottleneck paths; min gives widest path.
💡 PICK FOR FOLLOW-UPS
Three solutions were correct; choose the one that survives "what if elevations repeat?" or "now minimise the sum". That judgement is what is actually being assessed.
⚠️
Common mistakes on this problem
  • Using BFS and expecting the fewest steps to matter. The goal is the highest elevation, not the path length.
  • Using grid DP. Moving in four directions makes cells depend on each other in circles (Chapter 42).
  • Keeping d + grid[nr][nc] in the Dijkstra. That solves a different problem and returns a larger number.
  • Starting with dist[0][0] = 0 instead of grid[0][0]. You start standing on a cell, and its elevation counts.
  • Putting the coordinates before the cost in the heap tuple, so it sorts by row instead of by cost.
  • Forgetting greater<>, which gives a max-heap and a completely wrong visiting order.
  • Leaving out the out-of-date entry check if(d > dist[r][c]) continue;. The answer is still right but slower, and it hides the invariant.
  • Marking visited when taking a cell out of the queue in the BFS check (Chapter 53, bug 1).
  • Binary searching from low = 0 when the endpoints force a higher minimum. Harmless but sloppy, and worth saying you noticed.
  • Checking connectivity before doing the unions in the union-find version, which returns t+1.
  • Not asking whether elevations are a permutation. The answer changes which solution is best.

Interview drill

Warm-up: Present this problem end to end as you would in a round, in about four minutes of speech.

How a strong answer sounds
Let me restate it: I have an N×N grid of elevations, I move four-directionally from the top-left to the bottom-right, and I can only occupy a cell once the water level reaches its elevation. Since swimming is instantaneous and the water only rises, waiting never helps — so the arrival time is just the maximum elevation along whichever path I take, and I want to minimise that maximum. Stripped of the story, this is the bottleneck shortest path on a grid graph. Two quick clarifications: are elevations a permutation of 0 to N²−1, and how large is N? Given N ≤ 50 and a permutation, that is 2,500 cells. The brute force is: for each candidate time t, flood fill from the origin using only cells at or below t, and take the first t that reaches the goal. That is O(N⁴), about 6 million operations, which actually passes — but it re-explores the same region every time, and there are two ways to remove that. First, feasibility is monotone in t, since raising the water only adds usable cells, so I can binary search t instead of scanning it: O(N² log N). Second and better, I can avoid restarting at all with a modified Dijkstra. I define dist[r][c] as the minimum achievable maximum elevation on any path to that cell, and relax with max(d, grid[nr][nc]) instead of d + weight. That is legitimate because Dijkstra's greedy argument needs only that extending a path never lowers its cost, and max satisfies that just as addition does. dist[0][0] starts at grid[0][0], not 0, because I begin standing on that cell. O(N² log N), and the first pop of the destination is already optimal so I can return immediately. There is also a union-find solution that is asymptotically better, O(N²·α): because the elevations are a permutation, "raise the water to t" means exactly "activate the cell with elevation t", so I activate cells in order, union each with its active neighbours, and return the first t at which the start and goal share a root. I would write the Dijkstra, because it is shortest and it survives the follow-up where elevations are arbitrary with duplicates, whereas union-find would then need a sort. Shall I code it?

Deeper: Justify that Dijkstra with max is actually correct. The interviewer is not satisfied by "it works".

How a strong answer sounds
Dijkstra's correctness rests on one property of the cost function, and it is not addition. The invariant is: when a node is popped from the min-heap, its recorded cost is final. The proof is by contradiction — suppose node u is popped with cost d but some cheaper path to u exists. That path must leave the settled set at some point, through a node v still in the heap. Since the heap is a min-heap and u was popped first, v's current cost is at least d. And the cheaper path continues from v to u, so its total cost is at least the cost of reaching v — which requires only that extending a path never decreases its cost. That gives cost ≥ d, contradicting the assumption that it was cheaper. Notice what the argument used: monotonicity of the combiner, and nothing about addition. So the property I need is that combining a path cost with one more edge is non-decreasing. For sums with non-negative weights, that holds — and the non-negativity is exactly why Dijkstra fails with negative edges, since there the extension can reduce the cost and the settled invariant breaks. For max, it holds unconditionally: max(d, x) ≥ d for any x whatsoever, even negative elevations. So the bottleneck version is if anything on safer ground than the additive one. That reframing also tells me what else I can plug in: min with a maximising heap gives the widest-path problem, and multiplying probabilities in [0,1] with a max-heap gives the most-reliable-path problem, because multiplying by a value at most 1 is also monotone. All the same algorithm, one line different. I would add one honest note: the argument assumes the cost of a path is determined by the sequence of cells, which it is here, and that I am comparing totals rather than anything history-dependent — if the cost depended on, say, how many times I had turned, the state would need augmenting and this would no longer be plain Dijkstra.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Strip the story first. "Minimise the maximum along a path" is the entire problem; rain and swimming are decoration.
  • BFS needs uniform edge cost and grid DP needs an acyclic order — both fail here, and saying why is part of the answer.
  • Dijkstra's proof needs only that extending a path never lowers its cost, so max works exactly like +.
  • Monotone feasibility permits binary search on the answer; a permutation of values permits incremental union-find.
  • Choose the solution that survives the follow-up, not just the one that is asymptotically fastest.

Why is plain BFS wrong for this problem?

BFS is only correct when every edge costs the same. Here the objective is not even additive, which is the deeper reason a queue cannot order the exploration correctly.

Replacing Dijkstra's d + weight with max(d, elevation) is valid because…

That is also exactly why negative edge weights break Dijkstra: there, extending a path can lower the cost, so the "popped means final" invariant fails. The max version needs no non-negativity at all.

What makes binary search on the answer applicable here?

Monotonicity is the precondition, and it should be stated rather than assumed. The check being cheap is what makes it efficient, not what makes it correct.

Which fact makes the union-find solution possible in O(N²·α) with no sorting?

Without the permutation you would have to sort the cells by elevation, which costs O(N² log N) and removes the advantage. Reading that constraint is the point.

The follow-up changes the objective to minimising the sum of elevations. What happens?

This is why Dijkstra is the choice to code: it survives the follow-up with one character changed, and being able to say that in advance is exactly the judgement being assessed.
