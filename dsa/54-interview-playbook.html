The Interview Playbook

A coding interview is not really a test of whether you can solve the problem. It is a test of how you solve problems in front of another engineer. Two people can both reach the best solution and get very different results, depending on how they got there. This chapter gives you a minute-by-minute plan for the round, the things worth saying out loud, and what the interviewer is actually writing down.

  • Run a 45-minute round to a reliable structure
  • Ask the clarifying questions that matter, and skip the ones that do not
  • Narrate a derivation instead of announcing an answer
  • Recover gracefully when you are stuck or wrong
  • Self-score against the rubric interviewers actually use

The 45-minute structure

Why have a fixed structure at all? Because under pressure, people skip the steps that feel slow, such as asking questions, checking an example, or agreeing a plan. Those are exactly the steps that stop a twenty-minute mistake. A plan decided in advance means you never have to decide what to do next while you are nervous. You just move to the next box. The times below are for a 45-minute round with one problem. For a 30-minute round, shrink every step in proportion. If a round has two problems, spend less time on extensions for the first one and keep at least 20 minutes for the second.

0:00 – 0:03 · UNDERSTAND
Restate the problem, then ask about the input

Say the problem back in your own words. It catches misreadings straight away and costs fifteen seconds. Then ask only the questions whose answers change your code: how big is the input, can it contain duplicates or negative numbers, can it be empty, may you modify it, and what should you return when there is no answer?

Say: "So I'm given an array of integers and a target, and I need to return the indices of two numbers that sum to it. Before I start — can the array contain duplicates or negatives? Is it sorted? Is there always exactly one answer, and what should I return if there is none? And roughly how large is n?"
0:03 – 0:06 · EXAMPLE
Work one small example by hand, including an edge case

Write down a real input and the answer it should give. Then pick one edge case (an empty input, a single element, all values the same) and say what it should produce. This is where you catch an ambiguity the interviewer never mentioned, and it gives you a test to walk through later.

Say: "So [2, 7, 11, 15] with target 9 gives [0, 1]. And with [3, 3] and target 6 — that should be [0, 1], using the same value twice at different indices but not the same index twice. Let me confirm that is allowed."
0:06 – 0:12 · APPROACH
Brute force out loud, then name the redundancy

Say the slow, obvious solution and its complexity, even when you already know the best answer. Then say what work it repeats, and let the improvement follow from that. This is the most valuable habit in the whole round. It shows you can work a solution out rather than recall it, and that is the part that still holds when a constraint changes.

Say: "The brute force checks every pair, so O(n²). The wasted work is the searching: for each element I scan the rest of the array again to ask 'is target − x in here?'. That is just a 'have I seen this value?' question, so a hash set answers it in O(1). That makes the whole thing O(n) time and O(n) space. Shall I code that?"
0:12 – 0:15 · AGREE
State the plan and get a nod before writing code

Say which data structures you will use, how the loops will be arranged, and the complexity you expect. Then stop and wait. If the interviewer wants you to go a different way, finding out now costs you three minutes instead of twenty. A good interviewer will steer you here if you are heading somewhere that will not work.

Say: "The plan: one pass, a hash map from value to index, and for each element check whether the complement is already in the map. O(n) time, O(n) space, and it handles duplicates because I look up before inserting. Does that sound right to you?"
0:15 – 0:32 · CODE
Write it cleanly, narrating only the non-obvious lines

Use real names. Handle the special cases at the top. Use a helper function plus a driver if the logic has to carry state around. Explain why a line is there, not what it does. The interviewer can read i++ for themselves. If you realise halfway through that the approach is wrong, say so straight away instead of pushing on. Catching your own mistake reads far better than being caught.

Say: "I'm checking the map before inserting the current element — that is deliberate, so the same index can't be used twice. And I'm returning the indices rather than the values, as we agreed."
0:32 – 0:40 · VERIFY
Trace the example by hand, then the edge cases

Walk through your actual code, line by line, on the real example. Do not just claim it works. Then run the edge cases you named in step 2, and offer any others you would add. Finding your own bug during this pass counts in your favour, not against you.

Say: "Let me trace [2,7,11,15], target 9. i=0: complement 7, map empty, insert 2→0. i=1: complement 2, found at 0, return [0,1]. Correct. Now n=1: the loop runs once, nothing found, we fall through to the not-found return. And an empty array returns the same. I'd also want a test where the complement equals the element itself."
0:40 – 0:45 · EXTEND
State the complexity, the trade-offs, and one improvement

Give the final time and space complexity. Then name one trade-off you thought about and decided against, and one thing you would do with more time. Finish by asking a real question about the team or the work. It is the last thing they remember, so make it count.

Say: "O(n) time and O(n) space. If the array were sorted I'd use two pointers for O(1) extra space instead, and if it needed to handle a stream I'd keep the map and drop the indices. With more time I'd extend it to the three-sum variant, which is the sorted-plus-two-pointers version wrapped in an outer loop."

Which clarifying questions actually change the code? A question is worth asking only if different answers lead to different code. Some examples:

QuestionHow the answer changes your plan
Can values be negative?Negatives break a sliding window on sums, because adding an element can make the sum smaller. You then need prefix sums with a hash map.
Is the input sorted?If yes, two pointers or binary search, often with O(1) extra space. If not, a hash map, or sort first for O(n log n).
How large is n?n ≤ 20 allows trying every subset. n ≤ 10⁵ needs O(n log n) or better.
Can I modify the input?If yes, you can sort in place or mark visited cells in the grid itself. If not, you need a copy or a separate visited array.
What if there is no answer?Decides whether you return −1, an empty list, or throw, and which edge case you test.

"What language should I use?" or "Can I use a hash map?" rarely change anything. Just state your choice.

Why the verify step earns its time. Suppose you had written Two Sum the other way round: insert the current value into the map, then look up its complement. Trace [2, 5] with target 4. At i = 0 you insert 2→0, then look up 4 − 2 = 2, find it at index 0, and return [0, 0]. That uses the same element twice. The sample input [2, 7, 11, 15], target 9, would never have shown this. A two-element trace where the complement equals the value itself shows it in seconds. That is why "a test where the complement equals the element itself" appears in the verify script above.

✍️
My convention: the interviewer should never have to ask "what are you thinking?"

Silence is the most expensive thing in a coding interview. Thinking quietly is not wrong in itself. The problem is that reasoning nobody hears cannot earn you any credit, and an interviewer who cannot see your thinking has nothing to write down except the final code.

So I describe the shape of my thinking even when I have not finished it: "I'm deciding between a heap and sorting here. Sorting is simpler, but the streaming requirement probably rules it out." That one sentence is worth more than two minutes of silent correctness, because it shows a decision being made for the right reasons. And when I really do need to think, I say so: "Give me twenty seconds to work out the recurrence." That is a perfectly good sentence, and it sounds calm rather than stuck.

    📊
    What each dimension actually means
    • Did you understand the problem? Did you say it back, and ask the questions that changed your code? Asking nothing looks weak even when you happen to be right.
    • Did you work the approach out? Was the solution derived, or just announced? A derived O(n²) often scores higher than an announced O(n), because the reasoning is what carries over to other problems.
    • Complexity. Stated correctly, without being asked, for both time and space, including the space your recursion uses.
    • Code quality. Real names, no repeated logic, special cases handled at the top, boundaries right. Would a colleague accept this in a code review?
    • Correctness. Does it work, and did you check it by walking through it rather than just saying it works?
    • Communication. Could the interviewer follow your reasoning without asking? Silence costs more than a wrong turn.
    • Testing instinct. Did you offer edge cases, or wait to be asked for them?
    • Coachability. When given a hint, did you take it, use it, and say where it came from? Arguing with a correct hint is the fastest way to fail a round you were otherwise passing.

    How to practise against the rubric. Knowing the dimensions is not the same as scoring well on them. The only way to improve communication is to practise communicating. A routine that works:

    1. Pick a medium problem you have not seen. Set a 45-minute timer.
    2. Solve it out loud, as if someone were listening. Record yourself on your phone, or better, have a friend play the interviewer.
    3. Play back the first ten minutes. Count the silences longer than about thirty seconds, and check whether you asked a question, worked an example and gave the brute force before you started coding.
    4. Score yourself with the rubric above, and choose your lowest dimension as the one thing to fix next time.

    Most people's first recording is uncomfortable to watch. That is normal, and it is also the fastest feedback you can get. Two or three rounds a week, each followed by a scored review, improves your interview performance faster than solving three times as many problems in silence.

    When you are stuck

    🧭
    A procedure, not a panic

    Getting stuck is expected. Problems are chosen so that most candidates need some help. What is scored is what you do while stuck. In order:

    • Say that you are stuck, and exactly where. "I can see this needs DP, but I can't pin down what the state should be." That is a precise, useful sentence, and it invites the hint you need. Struggling in silence invites nothing.
    • Go back to a smaller case. Solve n = 1, 2, 3 by hand and write the answers down. Most recurrences are found this way, not by staring at the problem.
    • Say what you have ruled out, and why. "Greedy by end time fails here, and here is a counterexample." Ruling things out is progress, and it gets credit.
    • Offer the brute force as a safety net. "Let me code the O(n²) version so we have something working, then improve it." A working slow solution scores far higher than an unfinished fast one.
    • Take the hint and run with it. Interviewers hint on purpose. Using one well counts strongly in your favour, and ignoring one counts strongly against you.

    What not to do: go silent, guess at code, insist your wrong approach is right, or apologise over and over. None of it helps, and the apologising actively hurts. It uses up time and makes you sound fragile.

    The procedure on a real problem: "Make an amount with the fewest coins." You try greedy first: always take the largest coin that fits. Then you check it on coins {1, 3, 4} with amount 6. Greedy gives 4 + 1 + 1, which is three coins. But 3 + 3 is two. So you say: "Greedy by largest coin fails. Here is a counterexample: 6 with {1, 3, 4}." That rules greedy out, and it gets credit. Next, go small. The fewest coins for amount 0 is 0. For amount 1 it is 1. For amount 6, the last coin you add must be 1, 3 or 4. That leaves 5, 3 or 2 still to make, and each of those is the same problem again. So you say: "The state is the amount still to make, and dp[x] = 1 + min(dp[x − c]) over the coins c that fit." You got from stuck to a recurrence in four spoken steps, and the interviewer watched every one of them. That is worth more than having the recurrence memorised.

    Tips & tricks

    The things that are muscle memory rather than theory — what to check first, and the line that is wrong the first time you write it.

    💡 BRUTE FORCE ALOUD
    State the brute force even when you know the optimal answer. It proves you understand the problem, gives you a baseline to test against, and produces the optimisation.
    💡 NARRATE DECISIONS
    "I am choosing between a heap and sorting; sorting is simpler but the streaming requirement rules it out" is worth more than two minutes of silent correctness.
    ⛔ TAKE THE HINT
    Hints are given deliberately. Using one well is a positive signal; arguing with a correct one is the fastest way to fail a round you were passing.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Coding before you have agreed on the approach. That wastes twenty minutes instead of three.
    • Going silent for more than about thirty seconds. Reasoning nobody hears earns no credit.
    • Announcing a memorised solution without showing how you got there. The follow-up question will expose it.
    • Asking no clarifying questions, or asking ones whose answers change nothing.
    • Claiming it works without walking through it. Check it on the real example, line by line.
    • Arguing with a correct hint. The fastest way to fail a round you were passing.
    • Throwing away a working brute force to chase a faster solution you never finish.
    • Giving the complexity only when asked, or leaving out the space complexity.
    • Single-letter names everywhere, or copy-pasted logic you then have to fix in two places.
    • Apologising over and over. It uses up time and makes you sound fragile.
    • Not asking a real question at the end. It is the last impression you leave, and it is easy to do well.
    🎤
    Interview drill

    Warm-up: Ten minutes in you realise your chosen approach cannot work. What do you do?

    How a strong answer sounds
    I say it straight away and precisely: "I've hit a problem with this approach. My greedy rule fails on this input, and here is the counterexample." Naming the exact failure matters far more than admitting it, because it shows I found the problem by reasoning rather than guessing. A counterexample is solid evidence that I understand the problem.

    Then I make the failed approach earn its keep. Knowing that greedy-by-duration fails, and why, usually points at the right structure. In that case, a short choice nearby can block two better ones, which is exactly the sign that the problem needs DP or a different ordering. So my next sentence is: "Given that, I think this needs DP over intervals. Let me work out the state."

    What I would not do is quietly keep coding something I know is wrong and hope the tests are weak. That burns the remaining time, and worse, it suggests I would do the same thing on a real project. I also would not spiral into apologising. One clear sentence is enough.

    If there is very little time left, I change the goal: get the brute force working and correct, describe the better approach in words with its complexity, and say exactly what I would build next. A working slow solution plus an accurate description of the fast one scores well above an unfinished fast attempt. I would say that trade-off out loud, so the interviewer knows it is a deliberate choice and not a retreat.

    Deeper: You solve the problem in fifteen minutes. How do you spend the remaining thirty?

    How a strong answer sounds
    Finishing early is an opportunity, and the worst thing to do with it is sit back.

    First I make sure the solution is really finished, to a standard I would defend in a code review. I trace it line by line on the real example instead of claiming it works. I run the edge cases: empty, single element, duplicates, negatives, and the boundary values. I tidy up the names and structure.

    Then I state the time and space complexity, including the recursion stack if there is one, and I name a trade-off I considered and rejected, because that shows the choice was deliberate.

    Next I offer the extensions myself, since interviewers usually have follow-ups ready and offering first is always better than waiting. The good ones are: what changes if the input arrives as a stream and cannot be stored; what changes if I may only use O(1) extra space; what changes if the input is sorted, or if duplicates are allowed; how I would split the work across several machines; and how I would test it. That last one is where I mention writing a brute force I trust and stress-testing against it on small random inputs.

    If the problem has an obvious harder relative, I say so and offer to try it: "Two-sum extends to three-sum, which is a sort plus a two-pointer scan inside an outer loop, so O(n²). Want me to write it?"

    Finally I spend the last couple of minutes on a real question about the team: what the codebase looks like, how work gets reviewed, what the hardest problem they are facing is. That is the last thing they remember, and a specific question lands very differently from "do you like working here?".

    🧠 Key takeaways

    • Follow the structure: understand, example, approach, agree, code, verify, extend. Do not start coding before you have agreed on the approach.
    • Say the brute force and name the work it repeats. A solution you derive scores higher than one you announce.
    • Describe the shape of your thinking as you go. Silence earns no credit.
    • When stuck, say exactly where, shrink the case, say what you ruled out, and offer the brute force as a safety net.
    • Take hints well. Arguing with a correct hint fails rounds you were otherwise passing.
    • Check your code by walking through it, offer edge cases yourself, and give both time and space complexity without being asked.

    Check your understanding

    You recognise the problem instantly and know the optimal solution. What should you do first?

    A solution you work out shows a skill that carries over to new problems. One you just announce looks memorised and falls apart on the follow-up. Working it out only costs two or three minutes.

    You are genuinely stuck, eight minutes in. The best move is…

    "I can see this needs DP, but I can't pin down the state" is a precise sentence that earns credit and invites exactly the hint you need. Silence invites nothing.

    The interviewer suggests an approach you had not considered. What is the right response?

    Using a hint well counts in your favour. Ignoring or arguing with a correct one counts strongly against you. If you really do disagree, back it up with a concrete counterexample, not a preference.

    With fifteen minutes left, you have a working O(n²) solution and think an O(n log n) one exists. What do you do?

    Never swap a working solution for an unfinished one. Describing the better approach and its complexity earns most of the credit, even if you do not finish coding it.

    Which of these most often separates two candidates who both reach the optimal solution?

    The code is just the end product. The process is what gets scored. Walking through your own code and offering edge cases are the two cheapest ways to score higher.
