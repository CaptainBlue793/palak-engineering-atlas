Failure Arithmetic: Nines & MTBF

A single server that fails once a year sounds reliable. Ten thousand of them fail about 27 times a day. Distributed systems exist because failure is certain at scale, and the main design question is no longer "will it fail?" but "how often, how many at once, and for how long?" This chapter gives you the small amount of probability you need to answer that: availability as nines, how dependencies multiply and replicas add up, MTBF and MTTR, quorum availability, and the assumption that breaks all of it: that failures are independent.

  • Convert between nines and a downtime budget per year and per month
  • Compute availability for components in series and in parallel
  • Use MTBF and MTTR, and explain why faster recovery often beats rarer failure
  • Calculate the availability of a majority quorum of replicas
  • Recognise correlated failures and the failure domains that limit them

Nines and downtime budgets

Availability is the fraction of time (or of requests) a service works. It is quoted in nines because the useful values are all close to 1: "three nines" is 99.9%. The number becomes concrete when you turn it into a downtime budget, the time you are allowed to be down:

99%two3.65 days7.3 hoursA person can fix things by hand, next morning

Read the last column carefully. At four nines, one incident that pages a human, who logs in and investigates, uses up the whole year. That is the practical reason this course spends so long on automatic failure detection, leader election and failover.

Series: dependencies multiply

If a request needs every component on its path, the components are in series, and (assuming independent failures) the availabilities multiply. Each extra dependency lowers the total:

The rule of thumb: you can't be more available than your critical dependencies multiplied together. A service promising 99.99% while synchronously calling four services at 99.9% each has promised something impossible. The fixes are architectural: remove the dependency from the critical path (cache, queue, degrade gracefully), or make it more available.

Parallel: replicas add nines

If any one of n replicas can serve the request, the components are in parallel, and the system is down only when all of them are down: availability = 1 − (1 − a)n. Two independent replicas at 99% each give 1 − 0.01² = 99.99%. That's how unreliable machines make a reliable service.

Consensus systems sit in between. A Raft or Paxos group of n replicas needs a majority to be up: 2 of 3, or 3 of 5. The availability is the probability that at least k of n are up, a binomial sum:

/** P(at least k of n independent replicas are up), each up with probability a. */
static double atLeast(int n, int k, double a) {
	double p = 0;
	for (int up = k; up <= n; up++) p += choose(n, up) * Math.pow(a, up) * Math.pow(1 - a, n - up);
	return p;
}

static Duration downtimePerYear(double availability) {
	return Duration.ofSeconds(Math.round((1 - availability) * 365.25 * 24 * 3600));
}

atLeast(3, 2, 0.99);   // 0.999702: 3 replicas at 99% each, majority of 2
atLeast(5, 3, 0.99);   // 0.99999015: 5 replicas, majority of 3

Three replicas tolerate one failure; five tolerate two. Going from 3 to 5 replicas costs two machines and adds almost two nines, which is why production etcd and ZooKeeper clusters are usually 3 or 5 nodes. Going to 4 buys nothing: a majority of 4 is 3, so 4 nodes also tolerate only one failure (Chapter 12 has the details). Build your own system below:

MTBF and MTTR: how often, and for how long

Availability hides two separate numbers. MTBF, mean time between failures, says how often something breaks. MTTR, mean time to repair (or recover), says how long each failure lasts. For a component that alternates between working and being repaired:

availability = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR)

A server that fails every 1,000 hours and takes 1 hour to come back is available 1000 / 1001 ≈ 99.9% of the time. You can add a nine in two ways: make failures ten times rarer (very hard: better hardware, fewer bugs), or make recovery ten times faster (often much easier: automatic restart, automatic failover to a standby, smaller blast radius). Most of the gains in this course come from attacking MTTR: a failure detector that notices in seconds (Chapter 5) plus an election that takes a few hundred milliseconds (Chapter 17) turns an hour-long outage into a blip.

More machines, more failures

If each machine fails independently with probability p on a given day, the chance that at least one of N fails that day is 1 − (1 − p)N, which grows quickly with N. A machine that fails once every three years has p ≈ 1/1000 per day; with 10,000 of them, you should expect about 10 failures every day. Backblaze, which publishes statistics for hundreds of thousands of drives, reports annualised hard-drive failure rates around 1.5%: in a fleet of 100,000 drives, that's about four drive failures a day. Jeff Dean's well-known list for a new Google cluster's first year included roughly a thousand individual machine failures and thousands of hard-drive failures. At that scale failure is routine work, handled by software, not an emergency.

Correlated failures: the assumption that breaks the maths

Every formula above assumed failures are independent: one replica going down says nothing about the others. Real failures are frequently correlated. Replicas in one rack share a top-of-rack switch and a power feed. Replicas in one availability zone share a building. Replicas running the same binary share every bug, so a poison request or a leap-second bug crashes all of them in the same minute. A configuration push reaches every replica within seconds.

A small correlated term dominates everything else. Three replicas at 99% each give 99.97% if independent, but if a shared switch fails for 5 hours a year, those 5 hours alone cost you more than the replicas saved. That is what the red slider in the panel shows. The defence is to spread replicas across failure domains, separate racks, zones or regions, and to stagger deploys so that a bad build reaches one replica before all of them.

☁️
Use case: one typo, one region

On 28 February 2017, an engineer debugging Amazon S3's billing system in us-east-1 ran a playbook command to remove a few servers. One input was mistyped, and a much larger set of servers was removed, including ones that ran S3's index subsystem. Restarting those subsystems took several hours, and S3 in that region couldn't serve requests. Thousands of websites and many AWS services that depended on S3 in us-east-1 failed with it, including, for a while, the AWS status dashboard. S3 stored every object redundantly across many disks and facilities. None of that helped, because the failure was common-mode: one control-plane action applied to the whole region. Amazon's follow-up added safeguards so that the tool removes capacity more slowly and refuses to take a subsystem below its minimum.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Promising more nines than your dependencies allow. Series availabilities multiply; check the product first.
  • Assuming independent failures for replicas in the same rack, zone, binary or config. The correlated term usually dominates.
  • Running an even number of consensus replicas. Four nodes tolerate one failure, like three, but cost more and need a bigger majority.
  • Optimising only MTBF. Automatic detection and failover (MTTR) is usually the cheaper nine.
  • Measuring availability by uptime of servers instead of the success rate of user requests.
  • Forgetting planned downtime: deploys, migrations and certificate renewals spend the same budget as failures.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Why do etcd and ZooKeeper clusters usually have 3 or 5 nodes, not 4 or 6?

How a strong answer sounds
They need a majority of nodes up to make progress. A majority of 3 is 2, so 3 nodes tolerate 1 failure; a majority of 4 is 3, so 4 nodes still tolerate only 1. The fourth node adds cost, adds one more machine that could fail, and makes every write wait for one more acknowledgement, without improving fault tolerance. Five nodes tolerate two failures, which covers one planned restart plus one unplanned failure at the same time. Beyond 5 or 7, the latency of collecting a majority usually outweighs the gain.

Deeper: Your three database replicas are each 99.9% available. Someone says the database is therefore "nine nines". What's wrong with that?

How a strong answer sounds
That number assumes the replicas fail independently and that any one replica is enough. Neither holds. If it's a consensus database, it needs 2 of 3, which gives about 99.9997%, not nine nines. More importantly, replicas share failure modes: the same software version, the same config, often the same zone or network. A single correlated outage of an hour a year caps the database near 99.99% regardless of replica count. I'd ask which failure domains the replicas span, how deploys and config changes are staged, and I'd measure availability from client success rates rather than computing it.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Turn nines into a downtime budget: three nines ≈ 8.8 hours a year, four nines ≈ 53 minutes.
  • In series, availabilities multiply; you can't beat the product of your critical dependencies.
  • In parallel, replicas add nines: 1 − (1 − a)n; a majority quorum is a binomial "at least k of n".
  • Availability = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR); faster automatic recovery is usually the cheapest nine.
  • At scale, failures are daily events: 1 − (1 − p)N grows fast with N.
  • Correlated failures break the independence assumption; spread replicas across failure domains and stage changes.

Check your understanding

About how much downtime per year does 99.99% availability allow?

0.0001 × 365.25 days × 24 h × 60 min ≈ 52.6 minutes. 8.8 hours is three nines; 5 minutes is five nines.

A request must pass through three services in series, each 99.9% available and failing independently. What is the availability of the request path?

0.999³ ≈ 0.997. Every dependency on the critical path lowers the total.

A component fails on average every 1,000 hours and takes 1 hour to recover. What is its availability, and what is usually the cheaper way to add a nine?

1000 / 1001 ≈ 99.9%. A tenfold faster recovery (MTTR 6 min) gives about 99.99%, and automation is usually easier than tenfold rarer failures.

Why does a 4-node consensus cluster tolerate no more failures than a 3-node one?

Fault tolerance is n minus the majority: 3 − 2 = 1 and 4 − 3 = 1. Five nodes (majority 3) tolerate two failures.

Three database replicas are spread over three racks in one availability zone. Which event does the replica count NOT protect against?

Replicas only help against failures they don't share. A zone outage or a fleet-wide config change is a common-mode failure that hits every replica together.
