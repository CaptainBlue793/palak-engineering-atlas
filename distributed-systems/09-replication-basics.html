Replication Basics

Replication means keeping copies of the same data on several machines. If the data never changed there would be nothing to study: copy it once and stop. Everything hard about replication comes from writes. A change made on one machine has to reach the others, it takes time to get there, and meanwhile the copies disagree, a machine may die holding the only copy of the newest change, and two machines may each accept a change the other hasn't heard of. There are three basic answers to "which machines may accept a write?", and they are a single leader, several leaders, and no leader. Each is combined with a second choice: whether the writer waits for the copies to be made. This chapter covers both choices and what each combination can lose. Why replicate

There are three reasons to keep more than one copy, and most systems want all three.

  • To survive failures. A machine, a disk or a data centre can be lost and the data is still there, and still being served.
  • To be near the users. A copy in Frankfurt answers a reader in Berlin in a few milliseconds, where a copy in Virginia would take a hundred.
  • To serve more reads. Ten copies can answer ten times the queries of one.

The cost is the same in every case. A write isn't finished when one machine has it, and until the others have it too the system is in a state that a single machine can never be in: different copies give different answers. How a design handles that period, how long it lasts, what readers can see during it, and what happens if a machine fails in the middle of it, is what distinguishes one replicated system from another.

Single leader

The commonest arrangement gives one replica the job of accepting writes. It is the leader, also called the primary. Every write goes to it. It applies the write, appends it to a log, and sends the log to the other replicas, the followers, which apply the same changes in the same order. Reads can go to the leader or to any follower.

The great virtue of a single leader is that there is one order of writes, decided in one place, so two writes can never conflict. Its weaknesses follow from the same fact. Every write has to reach that one machine, however far away the writer is. When the leader fails, someone has to notice, choose a follower to promote and make sure the old leader has really stopped, which Chapter 13 and Chapter 14 show to be harder than it sounds. And followers lag, so a read from a follower can return a value older than one the same client has already written. PostgreSQL, MySQL, MongoDB and Kafka all replicate this way, and so does every Raft and Paxos system, which add a safe way of choosing the leader.

Multi-leader

Give each region a leader of its own, and a write never has to cross an ocean. Each leader accepts writes locally and replicates them to the other leaders in the background. The same shape appears wherever a copy must stay writable while disconnected: a calendar on a phone that works on a plane is a leader, and so is each browser tab in a collaborative editor.

The price is that the single order of writes is gone. Two leaders can accept different writes to the same record, each acknowledging its client, each unaware of the other. When replication delivers each write to the other side, the two copies disagree and something has to give. That is a write conflict, and it can't happen with one leader.

Leaderless

The third arrangement has no special replica at all. A write is sent to all the replicas of a key at once, by the client or by whichever node receives the request, and it counts as successful when enough of them have acknowledged it. A read likewise asks several replicas and takes the newest answer. If "enough" is chosen so that the set written to and the set read from must overlap, a read is sure to meet at least one replica holding the latest write. Those overlapping sets are quorums, the subject of Chapter 12.

With no leader there is no failover: a dead replica is one voice missing from the count, and requests go on being served by the rest. There is also no single order of writes, so concurrent writes to one key conflict as in the multi-leader case, and a replica that was down when a write happened stays out of date until a later read notices and repairs it, or a background process does (Chapter 24). Amazon's Dynamo made the design well known, and Cassandra, Riak and ScyllaDB follow it.

Sync vs async

Whichever replica accepts a write, there is a second decision: when does the writer get its answer?

The middle column is where most serious systems end up. Waiting for nobody risks acknowledged writes, and waiting for everybody makes the system only as available as its least available member. Waiting for a majority, in particular, has a property the later chapters depend on: any two majorities share at least one member, so whatever a majority has acknowledged, any later majority will find.

Things to notice. Go through the four situations with the asynchronous single leader: it gives a stale read in the first, refuses the cut-off region's write in the third, and loses an acknowledged write in the fourth. Waiting for one follower repairs the fourth, at 15 ms on every write, and leaves the stale read in place, since Virginia is still fed asynchronously. It pays for that in the second and third situations, where two acknowledged writes meet and one is silently discarded. Its reads and writes each cost a round trip to a second site, it refuses the cut-off region as a single leader does, and it too discards one of two concurrent writes. Use case: one managed database, two kinds of replica

Amazon's managed database service, RDS, sells the two choices of this chapter as two separate features, and the split shows what each kind of replication is for. A Multi-AZ deployment keeps a standby copy of the database in a different availability zone, a separate data centre in the same city. Replication to it is synchronous: a commit isn't acknowledged until the standby has the change. In the classic form the standby serves no queries at all. It exists for one purpose, to take over if the primary's machine or data centre is lost, with no acknowledged transaction missing, and AWS quotes a failover time of a minute or two. A read replica is the opposite arrangement. It is fed asynchronously using the database engine's own replication, it can be in another region, it serves read queries, and the service publishes a metric called replica lag for it, since by design it may be behind. Promoting a read replica after a disaster can lose the last moments of writes. Customers who want both protections run both: a synchronous standby for durability, and asynchronous replicas for read capacity and for placing data near distant users. Neither kind of replica can do the other's job. A synchronous copy on another continent would add its round trip to every commit, and an asynchronous one can't promise to hold everything the primary acknowledged.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Reading from a follower straight after writing to the leader, and showing users their own change undone.
  • Treating an acknowledgement as proof of durability when replication is asynchronous.
  • Choosing multi-leader for write speed without deciding how conflicts will be resolved.
  • Fully synchronous replication to every replica, which stops all writes when any one replica is slow.
  • Assuming failover is safe by default. An asynchronous follower may be missing writes, and the old leader may still be alive.
  • Using replicas as backups. A mistaken delete is replicated as faithfully as everything else.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What are the trade-offs between synchronous and asynchronous replication?

How a strong answer sounds
With synchronous replication the leader waits for a follower to confirm it has the write before acknowledging the client. That guarantees the write exists on at least two machines, so if the leader dies immediately afterwards nothing acknowledged is lost. The cost is latency, every write pays a round trip to the follower, and availability: if the follower is down or slow, writes stall. With asynchronous replication the leader acknowledges as soon as it has the write locally and ships it afterwards. Writes are fast and don't depend on followers being healthy, but there is a window in which an acknowledged write exists only on the leader, and a failover in that window loses it. Followers also lag, so reads from them can be stale. In practice systems choose a middle path: wait for one follower, or for a majority, and replicate to the rest asynchronously. That keeps durability without making every replica a single point of failure for writes.

Deeper: Your product is going multi-region. When would you pick multi-leader replication over a single leader, and what would you have to build?

How a strong answer sounds
I'd start by resisting it. A single leader with read replicas in each region gives local reads, one order of writes and no conflicts, and writes from distant regions pay one cross-region round trip, which is acceptable for most products. I'd move to multi-leader only for a specific need: writes must be fast in every region, or each region must keep accepting writes when it is cut off from the others, or clients work offline and sync later. What I'd then have to build is conflict handling, since two regions can accept different writes to the same record. The options, in my order of preference, are: avoid conflicts by giving each record a home region and routing its writes there, so that multi-leader behaves as many single leaders; use data types that merge automatically, such as counters and sets as CRDTs, where the data allows; detect conflicts with version vectors and merge in application code; and only as a last resort last-write-wins, which silently discards acknowledged writes and depends on clock sync. I'd also need to handle things that aren't row conflicts: unique constraints and auto-increment IDs can't be enforced locally, so IDs must be generated without coordination and uniqueness checked asynchronously; operations that must not be duplicated, such as payments, need a single authority. And I'd need monitoring for replication lag and conflict rates, and a plan for a region that returns after a long partition with a backlog of conflicting writes.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Replication buys fault tolerance, locality and read capacity. Its difficulty is entirely in propagating writes.
  • A single leader gives one order of writes and no conflicts, at the cost of failover and a round trip for distant writers.
  • Followers lag, so reads from them can be stale, including reads of your own writes.
  • Multi-leader gives local writes and survives partitions, and creates conflicts that must be resolved somehow.
  • Leaderless writes to several replicas and reads from several. No failover, and conflicts as in multi-leader.
  • Asynchronous replication can lose acknowledged writes, and fully synchronous halts on one slow replica. Waiting for some, usually a majority, is the usual answer.

Check your understanding

A user updates their profile, and the next page shows the old profile. Replication is single-leader and asynchronous. What happened?

Replication lag. The follower will catch up. Reading your own writes from the leader avoids it.

Which problem exists with multi-leader replication and not with a single leader?

One leader puts every write in a single order. Several leaders can each accept a write the others haven't seen.

A leader acknowledges a write, then crashes before any follower has received it. Under which mode is the write lost?

Asynchronous replication acknowledges when only the leader has the write. The other modes couldn't have acknowledged it yet.

Why is fully synchronous replication, waiting for every replica, rarely used?

Availability falls to that of the least available replica. Waiting for some of them keeps durability without that.

In a leaderless system with three replicas, one replica is down. What happens to a write that needs two acknowledgements?

There is no leader to fail over. Two acknowledgements are enough, and repair happens afterwards.
