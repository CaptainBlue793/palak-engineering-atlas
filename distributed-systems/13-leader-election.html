Leader Election

A single leader makes replication simple: one node decides the order of writes, and the rest follow (Chapter 9). That leaves the question of what happens when the leader fails. The others have to notice, agree on a replacement, and carry on, with nobody outside the system telling them what to do. Choosing a new leader turns out to be the easy half. The classic election algorithms do it in a few messages. The hard half is being sure that the old leader has stopped, since a node that is slow or cut off looks exactly like one that has crashed (Chapter 5), and a system that elects a second leader while the first is still working has two nodes each giving orders. That state is split brain, and it is the real subject of this chapter.

Why elect a leader

Many jobs in a distributed system are easy if exactly one node does them and hard otherwise: ordering the writes to a database, assigning work to machines in a cluster, running a scheduled task once, handing out ID ranges. Giving the job to one fixed node makes that node a single point of failure. A leader election lets the nodes choose one among themselves, and choose again when it fails.

Two properties are wanted, and they pull against each other.

  • Safety: there is never more than one leader at a time.
  • Liveness: if the leader fails, a new one is eventually chosen.

An algorithm that never elects anyone is perfectly safe, and one that lets every node appoint itself is perfectly live. Being quick to replace a failed leader means acting on suspicion, and suspicion is sometimes wrong.

Bully and ring

The two classic algorithms date from around 1980, and both assume a kinder world than the one Chapter 2 described: every node knows the full list of members, messages arrive within a known time, and so a node that doesn't answer in time really is dead. Each node has a unique number, and the goal is for the live node with the highest number to become leader.

In the bully algorithm, a node that notices the leader is gone sends an ELECTION message to every node with a higher number than its own. If none of them answers, it has the highest number alive: it declares itself leader and announces it to everyone with a COORDINATOR message. If any of them answers OK, the node stands down, and each node that answered holds an election of its own in the same way. The highest live node hears no answer from above, and wins.

In the ring algorithm, the nodes are arranged in a logical circle. A node that notices the failure sends an ELECTION message to its successor, carrying its own number. Each node adds its number and passes the message on, skipping any neighbour that is dead. When the message returns to the node that started it, the list holds every live node. The highest number on it is the leader, and a second message goes round to announce the result.

    Split brain

    Two leaders at once is the worst outcome in a single-leader system. Each accepts writes, each replicates them to the followers it can reach, and the data forks into two histories that can't be merged mechanically. P8 told the story of GitHub in 2018, where 43 seconds of it took a day to repair. Three situations produce it.

    CauseWhat happensWhat the old leader thinks
    A network partitionThe side without the leader elects its ownNothing is wrong: its clients can still reach it
    A false suspicionThe leader was slow, in a garbage-collection pause or under load, and missed its heartbeats. The others elected a replacementIt wakes up and carries on from where it stopped, unaware that any time passed
    A restart with old stateA former leader crashes, a new one is elected, and the old one comes back still configured as leaderIt was the leader when it last looked

    The majority rule in the panel deals with the first cause. An election counts only if a majority of all the nodes took part, and since two majorities must share a member (Chapter 12), two sides of a partition can't both hold one. It does less for the other two causes, in which the old leader doesn't know it has been replaced. For those, every election is given a number that only goes up, a term or epoch, as in Chapter 7. The new leader has a higher term than the old, every message carries its sender's term, and a node that sees a term higher than its own knows at once that it is out of date. That stops a deposed leader as soon as it talks to anyone who knows better. It doesn't stop what the old leader does before then.

    Leases

    A lease closes most of that remaining gap by putting a time limit on leadership. A leader holds the position until a stated moment, and has to renew before then to keep it. If it can't renew, it must stop acting as leader when the lease runs out, of its own accord and without being told. The other nodes, for their part, promise not to elect anyone until the lease has certainly expired. A leader that is cut off therefore deposes itself, and its successor doesn't start until it has.

    final class LeaderLease {
	private static final long MARGIN_NANOS = Duration.ofSeconds(2).toNanos();
	private volatile long validUntilNanos;                    // on our own monotonic clock

	boolean acquireOrRenew(LeaseStore store, String me, Duration ttl) {
		long askedAt = System.nanoTime();                     // before the call: the lease started no later than this
		if (!store.acquireOrRenew("leader", me, ttl)) return false;
		validUntilNanos = askedAt + ttl.toNanos() - MARGIN_NANOS;
		return true;
	}

	boolean isLeader() { return System.nanoTime() < validUntilNanos; }
}

    Three details in that code are the whole technique. The time is taken before the request is sent, since the lease began when the store granted it, which was no later than that, and any delay in the reply would otherwise extend the leader's belief past the truth. Durations are measured on the monotonic clock (P5), so that a step in the wall clock can't lengthen a lease. And the leader stops a margin early, to allow for the two machines' clocks running at slightly different rates (Chapter 6). Leases don't need synchronised clocks. They need clocks that measure a ten-second interval about equally, which is a far weaker demand.

    The length of the lease is a trade. A long lease tolerates slow renewals and network hiccups, and means a long wait after a real crash, since nobody may take over until it expires. A short lease fails over quickly and expires by accident whenever the leader is briefly slow.

    Fencing

    A lease is still a check followed by an action, and time passes between the two. A leader can call isLeader(), be told yes, and then be stopped by a long garbage-collection pause or a suspended virtual machine. When it resumes, its lease has expired and a successor is at work, and the old leader carries straight on to the write it was about to make, with no chance to check again. No amount of care inside the leader prevents that, since the leader can't notice its own pause.

    The remaining protection has to sit at the receiving end. The resource being written to, the storage system or the database, must itself refuse requests from a leader that has been superseded. Each leader is given the number of its term, it sends that number with every request, and the resource remembers the highest it has seen and rejects anything lower. That number is a fencing token. Chapter 32 develops it fully, together with the argument over whether a lock without one is safe at all. For now the point is the layering: a majority to elect, a term to identify, a lease to make the old leader stop by itself, and fencing for the cases where it doesn't.

    ☸️
    Use case: how Kubernetes' own controllers choose a leader

    A Kubernetes control plane normally runs three copies of its scheduler and its controller manager, for availability, and only one copy of each may be active: two schedulers assigning the same pod to different machines would be a small split brain of their own. They settle it with a lease, and the mechanism can be inspected on any cluster. There is an object of kind Lease for each component, stored in etcd, the cluster's consensus-backed store, which makes updates to it linearizable. The object records the holder's identity, the time of the last renewal and the lease's duration. Each copy of the component tries to write its own name into the lease. The write is conditional on the object's version, so if two try at once, only one succeeds, and that one is the leader. It then renews every couple of seconds. The defaults in the client library are a lease of 15 seconds, a renewal deadline of 10 and a retry period of 2: a leader that hasn't managed to renew within 10 seconds gives up leadership itself, five seconds before any other copy is allowed to claim the lease, and that five-second gap is the safety margin in the code above. A copy that loses leadership doesn't try to wind down gracefully. It exits, and is restarted as a follower, since a process that has stopped can't act on stale beliefs. The Kubernetes documentation is also candid about the limit discussed above: the scheme assumes clocks that run at similar rates, and it doesn't by itself fence a paused leader. The controllers are written so that a duplicated action is harmless, with every change they make being a conditional update against the same store, which rejects a write based on stale data. That check plays the part of the fencing token.

    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Electing on a timeout alone. Silence doesn't mean the leader is dead. It may be slow, or you may be the one cut off.
    • No majority requirement, so that each side of a partition elects its own leader.
    • An even number of nodes split across two sites. A clean split leaves neither side with a majority, and a tie-breaker in a third place is needed.
    • Leases timed by the wall clock, or measured from when the reply arrived.
    • Trusting isLeader() across a pause. The answer was true when it was given, and may not be when it is used.
    • A deposed leader that keeps running. It should stop, or exit, the moment it can't prove it still leads.

    Interview drill

    Explain it simply: What is split brain, and how do you prevent it?

    How a strong answer sounds
    Split brain is when two nodes both believe they are the leader at the same time and both act on it, typically both accepting writes, so the system's state diverges into two histories. It happens because a node can't distinguish a crashed leader from one that is merely unreachable or slow: after a network partition, or a long pause on the leader, the others time out and elect a replacement while the original is still alive. Prevention is layered. First, require a majority to elect a leader, so that at most one side of a partition can do so. Second, number each leadership with a term or epoch that only increases, and have every node reject messages from a lower term, so that a deposed leader is ignored as soon as it speaks to an up-to-date node. Third, use leases, so that a leader that can't renew stops by itself after a bounded time, and no successor starts before that. And fourth, since a paused leader can still act after its lease has expired, have the resources it writes to check a fencing token, the term number, and refuse anything from an older leader.

    Deeper: You implement leader election with a 10-second lease in a consensus store. Walk through how it can still go wrong, and what you'd do about each case.

    How a strong answer sounds
    Three ways. First, a process pause: the leader checks that it holds the lease, then is stopped for 15 seconds by garbage collection, a VM migration or a stuck disk, and on resuming performs the action it was about to. Its lease has expired and another node is leader, so there are two actors. Nothing inside the leader can prevent this, since it can't observe its own pause, so the fix is at the resource: every operation carries the lease's epoch as a fencing token, and the storage rejects tokens lower than the highest it has seen. If the resource can't check tokens, I'd at least make operations idempotent or conditional on a version. Second, clocks: the leader and the store measure the 10 seconds on different clocks. If the leader's clock runs slow, it believes the lease is valid for longer than the store does. I'd measure with the monotonic clock, count from before the request was sent and not from when the response arrived, and stop a safety margin early; and I'd make sure nothing steps the clock in a way that affects the measurement. Third, the network: a renewal request arrives and is applied but the response is lost, so the leader thinks it failed and steps down unnecessarily. That is safe but hurts availability, so I'd retry within a renewal deadline shorter than the lease, and tune the lease length against observed latency: long enough to ride out normal jitter, short enough that failover after a real crash is acceptable. I'd also make a node that loses the lease exit, so that no code path carries on with stale assumptions, and test the whole thing by injecting pauses and partitions.

    🧠 Key takeaways

    • An election must be safe, at most one leader, and live, eventually some leader. Speed in one costs risk in the other.
    • Bully and ring elect the highest live node, and assume that an unanswered message means a dead node.
    • Under a partition or a false suspicion that assumption fails, and each side elects a leader: split brain.
    • Requiring a majority lets at most one side win. Terms let everyone recognise a stale leader.
    • A lease makes an isolated leader stop by itself, given clocks that measure intervals alike.
    • A paused leader can act after its lease has gone, so the resource must check a fencing token (Chapter 32).

    Check your understanding

    In the bully algorithm, node 3 of nodes 1 to 6 notices that the leader, node 6, has died. What does it do first?

    It challenges every node with a higher number. It wins only if none of them answers.

    Five nodes are split into groups of two and three by a partition. The leader is in the group of three. With the plain bully algorithm, what happens?

    The two nodes can't tell a partition from a crash, and the algorithm has no rule against a minority electing.

    Why does requiring a majority to win an election prevent two leaders being elected during a partition?

    There aren't enough nodes for two disjoint majorities. At most one side can elect.

    A leader holds a 10-second lease. The network to the lease store fails. What must the leader do?

    The lease is the leader's promise to stop. The others will elect a successor once it has run out.

    A leader confirms its lease is valid, then pauses for 30 seconds in garbage collection, then performs a write. What stops that write doing damage?

    The leader can't see its own pause. Only the receiving end can tell that the request comes from a superseded leader.
