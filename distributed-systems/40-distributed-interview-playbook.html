Distributed Systems Interview Playbook

You have now met every idea a distributed systems interview draws on. Knowing them and producing them under questioning are different skills, and this chapter is about the second. Interviewers in this area ask a small number of kinds of question, over and over, and each kind has a shape that a good answer follows. "Explain Raft" is a test of whether you can give a protocol's purpose, its mechanism and its one subtle point in the time available. "What happens if the leader pauses for thirty seconds?" is a test of whether you can trace a failure through a design without skipping the uncomfortable step. "Can you have exactly-once delivery?" is a test of whether you know where the theorems are. None of them is a test of recall. What is being judged is whether you reason from the model, name the trade-off before being asked, and say "it depends" followed by what it depends on. The sections below map the question types, give a timed answer to the commonest of them, and drill the kind that candidates find hardest.

  • Recognise which kind of question is being asked, and what it is testing
  • Explain Raft clearly in three minutes
  • Answer consistency questions precisely, avoiding the standard confusions
  • Walk through a failure in a given architecture: what happens, what the client sees, what is lost
  • Spot a trick question, and answer the question behind it

Explaining Raft in three minutes

This is the question most often asked, and a rambling answer to it is the commonest way to lose time. The outline below fits in three minutes spoken at an ordinary pace. Each part has one job.

TimePartWhat to say
0:00 – 0:20The problem"Raft keeps a log of commands identical on several servers, so that each can apply them in the same order and they stay copies of one state machine, even when some crash. It needs a majority alive: three servers tolerate one failure, five tolerate two."
0:20 – 1:00Electing a leader"Time is divided into numbered terms, with at most one leader in each. Followers expect heartbeats. One that hears none for a randomised timeout becomes a candidate, increments the term, votes for itself and asks the others. A server gives one vote per term, first come first served. A majority of votes makes a leader. The random timeouts make repeated ties unlikely."
1:00 – 1:50Replicating the log"Clients send commands to the leader. It appends each to its log and sends it to the followers, along with the index and term of the entry just before, so a follower can check that its log matches up to that point, and if not, the leader backs up and overwrites the follower's conflicting entries. Once a majority has stored an entry, it is committed. The leader applies it, replies to the client, and tells followers the commit point in the next message."
1:50 – 2:30Why it is safe"The key rule is in voting: a server refuses its vote to a candidate whose log is less up to date than its own. A committed entry is on a majority, any winning candidate needs votes from a majority, the two overlap, so every new leader already holds every committed entry. Nothing committed is ever overwritten. One more rule: a leader only counts replicas to commit entries from its own term."
2:30 – 3:00Limits and context"Safety never depends on timing. Progress does: with a bad network there may be no leader for a while. A minority side of a partition can't commit anything, so it chooses consistency over availability. In practice you add snapshots to bound the log, and a careful protocol for changing membership. etcd, Consul and CockroachDB use it."

Notice what is left out: message names, the exact timeout values, the fields of each RPC. Offer them if asked. If the interviewer then says "go deeper", the usual directions are the commit rule for entries from earlier terms, linearizable reads without writing to the log, and what happens to a client whose request was appended and whose leader then died. All three are in Chapter 18.

Consistency questions

More marks are lost on vocabulary here than on substance. These are the distinctions that come up, with the short form of each answer.

QuestionShort answerThe trap
Linearizable or serializable?Linearizable is about single operations and real time: each appears to take effect at one instant between its call and its reply. Serializable is about transactions over several objects: the result equals some one-at-a-time order, and says nothing of real time. Both together is strict serializabilityUsing them as synonyms. A database can be serializable and still show stale reads (Ch 11)
What does CAP say?During a network partition, a system must choose between answering every request and keeping linearizability. With no partition it can have both, and the trade is then latency against consistency"Pick two of three". Partitions aren't optional, and the C isn't the C of ACID (Ch 10)
Does a quorum read return the latest write?With R + W > N a read overlaps the latest acknowledged write in at least one replica. That alone isn't linearizability: a write still in progress can be seen by one reader and missed by the nextForgetting concurrent and failed writes, and sloppy quorums (Ch 12)
What is eventual consistency?If writes stop, replicas converge. It promises nothing about when, or about what is read meanwhileTreating it as "slightly delayed strong consistency". It also needs a rule for conflicts (Ch 25)
How do you give read-your-writes?Route a user's reads to the leader for a while after they write, or have the client carry the position of its last write and make replicas wait until they reach itAssuming a session is pinned to one replica for ever (Ch 11)
Exactly-once?Delivery can't be exactly-once. Processing can have exactly-once effect: deliver at least once and make the handler idempotent, or commit the effect and the position togetherBelieving a broker's setting covers your database (Ch 28)

Failure-scenario questions

"Walk me through what happens when…" is where interviews are decided, since the answer can't be recited. Use five questions, in this order, for any system and any fault.

  1. Where is the state? Which nodes hold which data, and which of it has been acknowledged to a client.
  2. Who notices, and how soon? A timeout, a missed heartbeat, an expired lease. Nobody notices instantly, and the delay is part of the answer.
  3. What does the system do unaided? Elect, promote, retry, reroute, or nothing.
  4. What does the client see? An error, a timeout, a stale read, a duplicate, or nothing at all.
  5. What is lost or wrong afterwards, and what would bound it?

Trick questions

A trick question has an answer that sounds right and is wrong, or a false assumption built in. The interviewer is checking whether you examine the premise.

QuestionThe tempting answerA better answer
A Raft cluster has four nodes. How many failures does it survive?Two, half of themOne. A majority of four is three. Four nodes tolerate what three do, with one more thing to break, which is why clusters are odd-sized
Does adding replicas make writes faster?Yes, more machines share the loadNo. Every replica must receive every write, and a larger quorum waits for more acknowledgements. Replicas scale reads. Writes scale by partitioning (Ch 20)
We hold a distributed lock, so only one worker writes. Agreed?YesOnly one worker believes it holds the lock at any moment the service knows of. A paused holder still writes. The resource must check a fencing token (Ch 32)
Is two-phase commit a consensus protocol?Yes, the nodes agree to commitIt solves atomic commit, where every participant has a veto and all must answer. Consensus needs only a majority and tolerates failures, and 2PC blocks on one (Ch 21)
Our servers run NTP, so we order events by timestamp. Fine?Yes, the clocks are synchronisedThey agree to within some milliseconds on a good day. Two events closer than that can be ordered wrongly. Use logical clocks, or a bounded-error clock with a rule for the uncertainty (Ch 6, 35)
TCP guarantees delivery, so why do we need retries and idempotency?We don't, within a connectionTCP guarantees order and no duplicates within one connection while it lasts. When it breaks, the sender doesn't know what was processed, and a retry on a new connection is a new request (Ch 4)
Can a system be both consistent and available?No, CAP forbids itYes, whenever the network isn't partitioned, which is most of the time. CAP constrains only what happens during a partition (Ch 10)
Is Raft Byzantine fault tolerant?It tolerates failures, so yesNo. It assumes nodes that fail by stopping. One node that lies about its log or its votes can break it. Byzantine tolerance needs 3f + 1 nodes and a different protocol (Ch 33)
Use a timeout to detect that a node has crashed. How long should it be?A numberNo timeout detects a crash. It detects silence, which may be a slow node or network. Short means false alarms and long means slow recovery, so the design must be safe when the guess is wrong (Ch 5)

The honest reply to a question whose premise is false is to say so, politely, and then answer the question that was meant: "strictly it tolerates one, and here is why people expect two".

Use case: "the primary just died", answered for PostgreSQL and Patroni

Here is the first drill in the panel worked for a real stack, as you might give it in an interview at a company that runs one. PostgreSQL replicates by shipping its write-ahead log from a primary to standbys, asynchronously by default. PostgreSQL has no automatic failover of its own, so most installations add a manager, and a common one is Patroni. Where is the state? All of it on the primary. Each standby has the log up to wherever it has received, usually a fraction of a second behind. Who notices? Patroni runs beside each PostgreSQL instance and keeps a leader key in a consensus store, etcd or Consul, with a time to live of 30 seconds by default, renewed every 10. If the primary's machine dies, the key stops being renewed and expires. So detection takes up to about half a minute: that is the setting to mention, with the trade it embodies, that a shorter one fails over on a brief network fault. What does the system do? The Patroni agents on the standbys see the key vanish and race to create it. The consensus store lets exactly one succeed, which is the election of Chapter 31. Before trying, each agent compares its replay position with the others', and one that is far behind stands aside, so the most advanced standby normally wins and is promoted. What does the client see? Errors for thirty to forty seconds, then a new primary, reached through a proxy or a DNS name that follows the leader key. What is lost? Any transaction the old primary acknowledged and hadn't yet shipped: with asynchronous replication, the last moments of writes. To bound that at zero you enable synchronous replication, and every commit then waits for a standby, and stalls if none is available. The follow-up is nearly always "and if the old primary wasn't dead, only cut off?" A Patroni agent that can't renew its key demotes its own PostgreSQL before the key expires, and a hardware or kernel watchdog can reset the machine if the agent itself has hung. That is fencing by self-restraint, with the weakness that Chapter 32 described: it relies on the old primary being able to act. Saying that last sentence unprompted is what the question was for.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Answering before fixing the requirement. "It depends on whether stale reads are acceptable" is the start of a good answer, if you then take each branch.
  • Naming a tool in place of a mechanism. "Use Kafka" or "use ZooKeeper" explains nothing. Say what property of it you are relying on.
  • Stopping the failure walk-through at the server. Follow it to the client: what do they see, and what do they do next?
  • "That can't happen." In this field it can. Say how unlikely it is and what would bound the damage.
  • Using consistency words loosely. Say linearizable, serializable or eventual, and mean it.
  • Claiming no trade-off. Every design here gives something up. An answer that doesn't say what hasn't finished.
  • Reciting. An interviewer who hears a memorised paragraph will move one step off it, and see what is left.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is the difference between Raft and Paxos, and why do most new systems choose Raft?

How a strong answer sounds
Both solve the same problem, getting a group of servers to agree on a sequence of values despite crashes, with the same fault tolerance: a majority must be alive, safety holds regardless of timing, and progress needs the network to behave. The difference is in how they are structured and described. Basic Paxos agrees on a single value with two phases, prepare and accept, and any node may propose; to build a replicated log you run an instance per log slot and add a stable leader as an optimisation, which is Multi-Paxos, and the papers leave many of those details to the implementer. Raft was designed for understandability and specifies the whole thing: a strong leader that is the only source of log entries, terms, a log-matching check on each append, and one explicit restriction, that only a candidate with an up-to-date log can win an election, which means a new leader never needs to fetch missing committed entries from others. In Paxos a new leader may be behind and must recover entries during its prepare phase. Performance is similar. New systems mostly pick Raft because it is completely specified, including membership changes and log compaction, has many tested libraries, and is easier to implement and reason about correctly, which matters more than theoretical flexibility. Paxos variants are still used where their flexibility helps, for example leaderless or flexible-quorum variants.

Deeper: Design a service that runs each scheduled job exactly once, across three datacentres.

How a strong answer sounds
First I'd challenge "exactly once": a worker can crash after doing the job and before recording that it did, so what is achievable is at-least-once execution with idempotent jobs, or exactly-once effect where the job's side effect and its completion record commit atomically. I'd ask which the jobs need, and what "on time" means. Design: job definitions and state live in a consensus-replicated store spanning the three datacentres, so it survives the loss of one and stays consistent; cross-region consensus costs write latency, which is acceptable for scheduling metadata. A small set of schedulers elect one leader through that store to decide which jobs are due, so a job isn't enqueued twice; leadership uses a lease, and since a paused leader could act after losing it, each enqueue is a conditional write, "mark run N of job J as dispatched if it is still pending", so a stale leader's write fails. That is a fencing check enforced by the store. Workers claim a run the same way, with a lease they renew while working; if a worker dies, the lease expires and another claims it, which is where at-least-once comes from. Each run has a stable ID, job plus scheduled time, passed to the job as an idempotency key, so a job that calls a payment API or writes to a database can deduplicate. Completion is recorded with a conditional write on the run's attempt number, so a zombie worker that finishes late can't overwrite a newer attempt's result. Failure cases I'd walk through: a datacentre lost, the store keeps quorum and another scheduler takes over within the lease time; a partition, the minority side can't claim or dispatch, so nothing runs twice, at the cost of availability there; clock skew, due times are evaluated by the leader against the store's time, not each worker's clock. What I've given up: jobs may run late during failover, and non-idempotent jobs can run twice, which no design can prevent, only narrow.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • There are about six kinds of question. Identify the kind, and you know the shape of the answer.
  • Always: pin down the guarantee, state the failure model, give the mechanism briefly, break it yourself, name the cost.
  • Raft in three minutes: the problem, elections, replication and commit, the voting restriction that makes it safe, and its limits.
  • Use consistency terms exactly. Linearizable, serializable and eventual are different promises.
  • For any failure: where is the state, who notices and when, what happens unaided, what does the client see, what is lost.
  • When a question's premise is wrong, say so and answer the question behind it.

Check your understanding

A consensus cluster is grown from three nodes to four. How many node failures can it now tolerate?

The fourth node raises the quorum without raising the tolerance. Five nodes are needed to survive two failures.

An interviewer asks "is your design consistent?" What should you do first?

The word covers linearizable, serializable, eventual and more. An answer is only possible once one is chosen.

In a single-leader system with asynchronous followers, the leader crashes and a follower is promoted. What is the honest statement about data?

Asynchronous means the leader replied before the followers had the write. The loss is the replication lag at the moment of the crash.

Which statement about Raft should be in a three-minute explanation, as the reason it is safe?

That rule, with majority overlap, is the safety argument. The others are details to give only if asked.

"We use a distributed lock, so two workers can never write at once." What is missing?

The lock makes concurrent writers rare. Only a check at the resource makes them impossible.
