Raft I: Terms & Leader Election

Paxos, as the papers describe it, chooses one value, and the path from there to a working replicated log was left to each implementer (Chapter 16). In 2014 Diego Ongaro and John Ousterhout published Raft, a consensus algorithm with the same guarantees and a different aim: to be understood. They tested that aim, teaching both algorithms to students and examining them on each, and most did better on Raft. It builds everything around a strong leader. Time is divided into numbered terms, each term has at most one leader, and the leader alone decides what goes into the log. That splits the problem into parts that can be studied separately. This chapter takes the first: how a cluster elects a leader, and why two can never be elected for the same term. Chapter 18 takes the rest: how the leader replicates the log, and why nothing committed is ever lost.

Roles and terms

A Raft cluster is a fixed set of servers, usually three or five. At any moment each server is in one of three roles.

RoleWhat it doesHow it leaves the role
FollowerPassive. It answers requests from leaders and candidates and starts nothing itselfIts election timer runs out with no word from a leader: it becomes a candidate
CandidateAsks every other server for its voteWins a majority and becomes leader, or hears from a leader and becomes a follower, or times out and tries again
LeaderHandles all client requests and sends the log to the followers, with a heartbeat whenever there is nothing else to sendSees a higher term and becomes a follower

Time is divided into terms, numbered 1, 2, 3 and upwards. Each term begins with an election. If a candidate wins, it leads for the rest of that term. If nobody wins, the term ends without a leader and the next begins. The term is a logical clock (Chapter 7): every server stores its current term, every message carries the sender's, and one rule applies to every message, whatever its kind.

  • A server that sees a term higher than its own adopts it at once and becomes a follower, whatever it was before.
  • A server that receives a message with a term lower than its own rejects it, and tells the sender its current term.

That rule is how a deposed leader finds out. It may be cut off for a minute and still believe it leads term 4. The first message it exchanges with a server in term 5 demotes it.

Election timeouts

A leader shows that it is alive by sending heartbeats to every follower at a short, regular interval. Each follower runs an election timer, and restarts it whenever a heartbeat arrives. If the timer ever runs out, the follower concludes that there is no working leader and starts an election.

The length of that timer is chosen at random, afresh each time it is restarted, from a range such as 150 to 300 ms. The randomness matters, as the panel below shows. With different timers, one follower usually runs out well before the others, asks for votes and wins before anyone else has stirred. With identical timers they would all run out together, every one of them would vote for itself, and nobody would have a majority.

RequestVote

A follower whose timer runs out does four things: it adds one to its term, becomes a candidate, votes for itself, and sends a RequestVote message to every other server. Each server answers by two rules.

  • It grants at most one vote in each term, to the first candidate that asks. Having voted, it refuses every other candidate of that term.
  • It refuses a candidate whose term is lower than its own.

Chapter 18 adds a third rule, about the candidate's log, which is what protects committed entries. For electing a leader these two are enough.

synchronized void onElectionTimeout() {
	currentTerm++;
	role = Role.CANDIDATE;
	votedFor = self;
	persist();                                     // term and vote are on disk before any message leaves
	votes = 1;
	resetElectionTimer();                          // a new random value: if this election fails, try again later
	for (Peer p : peers) p.requestVote(currentTerm, self).thenAccept(this::onVoteReply);
}

synchronized VoteReply onRequestVote(long term, int candidate) {
	if (term > currentTerm) stepDown(term);        // adopt the higher term, become a follower, clear votedFor
	boolean grant = term == currentTerm && (votedFor == null || votedFor == candidate);
	if (grant) {
		votedFor = candidate;
		persist();
		resetElectionTimer();                      // we have just helped someone: give them time to win
	}
	return new VoteReply(currentTerm, grant);
}

synchronized void onVoteReply(VoteReply reply) {
	if (reply.term() > currentTerm) { stepDown(reply.term()); return; }
	if (role == Role.CANDIDATE && reply.term() == currentTerm && reply.granted() && ++votes > clusterSize / 2)
		becomeLeader();                            // and send heartbeats at once, to stop other elections
}

The safety of the election rests on the first rule and on arithmetic. A candidate needs votes from a majority. Each server votes once in a term. Two majorities overlap in at least one server (Chapter 12), and that server can't have voted for both candidates. So at most one leader is elected in any term. The property needs one thing from the hardware: a server's current term and its vote must be on disk before it replies. A server that voted, crashed, restarted with no memory of the vote and voted again in the same term would break it.

Last, with a leader in place, split the network. The side with three servers keeps or elects a leader. The two servers on the other side stand for election over and over, raising their term each time, and never win, since two votes aren't a majority.

    Split votes

    Even with random timers, two followers will occasionally time out close enough together that both stand before either has heard from the other. The votes divide, and it can happen that neither reaches a majority. Raft does nothing clever about this. Each candidate's own election timer is still running, with a new random length, and when it runs out the candidate starts a fresh election in the next term. Since the new timers are independent, one candidate will very probably be well ahead this time. The original paper measured it: with timeouts spread over 150 to 300 ms a leader was elected in a few hundred milliseconds at worst, and with no randomness elections took many seconds or failed to finish.

    The timing has to satisfy a chain of inequalities for any of this to work.

    QuantityTypical valueMust be
    Time to send a message and get the reply, the disk write included0.5 to 20 msMuch smaller than the election timeout, or healthy leaders are deposed by slow heartbeats
    Heartbeat intervalA tenth to a third of the election timeoutShort enough that a few can be lost without a timer running out
    Election timeout150 to 300 ms on a local network, a second or more between data centresMuch smaller than the time between failures, or the cluster spends its life electing
    Time between server failuresWeeks or months

    The election timeout is the same dial as every failure detector's (Chapter 5). A short one replaces a dead leader quickly and deposes a live one whenever it is briefly slow. A long one is stable, and leaves the cluster leaderless for longer after a real failure.

    Leader heartbeats

    A new leader sends heartbeats immediately, without waiting for the interval, so that the other servers learn of it before their timers run out and start competing elections. A heartbeat is the ordinary message that carries log entries, with none in it, as the next chapter shows. Every server that receives one with a term at least as high as its own accepts the sender as leader and restarts its timer. A candidate that receives one abandons its candidacy.

    The last scene in the panel has a consequence worth knowing about. The two servers on the minority side of a partition can't win, and each failed attempt still raises their term. When the network heals, they come back with a term far above the leader's, and by the first rule of terms the working leader must step down when it hears of it. The cluster then holds an election it didn't need, and a server that contributed nothing has disrupted one that was working. Production implementations add a pre-vote: before raising its term, a would-be candidate asks the others whether they would vote for it, and only stands if a majority say yes. A cut-off server never gets that majority, so its term stays where it was.

    ⏱️
    Use case: two numbers in etcd's configuration

    etcd is the Raft-based store that holds the state of every Kubernetes cluster (Chapter 44), and the timing chain above appears in it as two settings that operators are expected to understand. The heartbeat interval defaults to 100 ms and the election timeout to 1,000 ms. They are longer than the Raft paper's figures on purpose. A heartbeat in a real deployment has to be written to disk as well as sent, and etcd's documentation advises setting the heartbeat interval to about the largest round-trip time between members, and the election timeout to at least ten times the heartbeat. The defaults suit a cluster inside one data centre on fast disks. When members are in different regions, with round trips of 100 ms or more, the documentation tells operators to raise both, to an election timeout of several seconds, since otherwise ordinary network jitter makes followers time out on a healthy leader. The price is stated as plainly: after a real leader failure the cluster will be unable to accept writes for about one election timeout. The other common cause of unwanted elections has nothing to do with the network. A leader on a slow or contended disk takes too long to persist each heartbeat round, falls behind its own schedule, and is voted out by followers that think it has died, which is why the project's hardware guidance is mostly about disk latency. etcd also enables the pre-vote described above by default in current releases, after operators reported clusters losing their leader each time a partitioned member rejoined.

    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Not persisting the term and the vote before replying. A restart then allows a second vote in the same term, and two leaders.
    • Fixed or barely randomised election timeouts. Candidates keep colliding.
    • An election timeout close to the heartbeat interval or the network's round trip. One delayed heartbeat deposes a healthy leader.
    • Counting a majority of the servers that are up. It is a majority of the configured cluster, always.
    • Forgetting to step down on a higher term in some message handler. The rule applies to every message.
    • An even number of servers. Four tolerate one failure, as three do, and need three votes where three servers need two.

    Interview drill

    Explain it simply: How does Raft elect a leader?

    How a strong answer sounds
    Every server starts as a follower with a randomised election timer, typically 150 to 300 milliseconds. A leader sends periodic heartbeats, and each one resets the followers' timers. If a follower's timer expires without hearing from a leader, it increments its term number, becomes a candidate, votes for itself and sends RequestVote to all the others. Each server grants at most one vote for each term, first come first served, and rejects candidates with a lower term than its own. A candidate that collects votes from a majority of the cluster becomes leader for that term and immediately starts sending heartbeats, which stops the others standing. Since each server votes once in a term and any two majorities overlap, there can be at most one leader in a term. If two candidates split the vote and neither gets a majority, their timers, re-randomised, expire again and one of them will usually get ahead in the next term. The term and the vote are written to disk before replying, so a restarted server can't vote twice. The full protocol also requires the candidate's log to be at least as up to date as the voter's, which is what keeps committed entries safe.

    Deeper: A five-node Raft cluster loses its leader roughly once an hour although no machine fails. What would you look at?

    How a strong answer sounds
    A leader change with no failure means followers' election timers are expiring while the leader is alive, or something is forcing the leader to step down. I'd separate those. For timer expiry, the cause is heartbeats arriving late, and there are three usual suspects. Disk latency: the leader persists before sending, and followers persist before acknowledging, so a slow fsync, from a shared or throttled disk or a compaction, delays heartbeats; I'd look at the write-ahead-log fsync latency percentiles on every node. Process pauses: garbage collection, or CPU starvation on an overcommitted host or a container hitting its CPU limit, can stall the leader for longer than the timeout. And the network: the election timeout may simply be too tight for the real round-trip time and jitter, especially across zones or regions; the rule of thumb is a heartbeat around the maximum round trip and an election timeout ten times that. For forced step-downs, the signature is a higher term arriving from somewhere: a node that was briefly partitioned, kept standing for election, inflated its term, and on rejoining forced a new election. I'd check whether the leader changes coincide with one particular node reconnecting, and whether pre-vote is enabled, since that is exactly what it prevents. In both cases I'd correlate the timestamps of leader changes with metrics on the old leader and on the node that started the election, and then either fix the resource problem or lengthen the timeouts, accepting slower failover.

    🧠 Key takeaways

    • Raft servers are followers, candidates or leaders. Time is divided into numbered terms, with at most one leader in each.
    • Any server that sees a higher term adopts it and becomes a follower. That is how stale leaders are removed.
    • A follower whose election timer runs out raises its term and asks for votes. A majority makes it leader.
    • One vote for each server in each term, plus overlapping majorities, means one leader for each term. The vote must be on disk.
    • Random timers make split votes rare and brief. Identical timers make them permanent.
    • Heartbeats hold the timers back. The election timeout trades failover speed against false elections, as every failure detector does.

    Check your understanding

    A leader in term 4 receives a message carrying term 6. What does it do?

    A higher term means the cluster has moved on. Every server yields to it, whatever its role.

    Why can't two candidates both win the election for the same term?

    A majority for each would overlap in at least one server, which had only one vote to give.

    What goes wrong if every server uses the same fixed election timeout?

    Safety holds: nobody wins wrongly. Nobody wins at all, since the votes always split.

    Which two things must a Raft server write to disk before answering a RequestVote?

    Otherwise a crash and restart would let it vote again in the same term.

    Five servers are split two and three by a partition. What happens on the side with two?

    A majority is counted over the whole cluster. The minority side can't elect, and so can't diverge.
