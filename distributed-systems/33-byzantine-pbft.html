Byzantine Fault Tolerance & PBFT

Every protocol in this course so far has trusted its participants in one respect. A node may crash, pause, restart or fall silent, and whatever it does say is honest: a Raft follower that votes means its vote, and a replica that acknowledges a write has stored it. That is the crash-fault model, and it fits machines run by one organisation inside one datacentre. It doesn't fit a node that has been broken into, a consortium of banks that don't trust each other's operators, a flight computer whose sensor wire has frayed, or a server whose memory has flipped a bit in a message that still parses. Such a node can do anything. It can tell one peer "yes" and another "no", claim to have received what it never did, or follow the protocol perfectly until the moment that does most harm. These are Byzantine faults, and reaching agreement despite them is possible, at a price: more replicas, more rounds and many more messages. This chapter covers where the price comes from, the algorithm that first made it practical, and the reasons most systems still decline to pay.

Byzantine faults

The name is from a 1982 paper by Leslie Lamport, Robert Shostak and Marshall Pease, which posed the problem as generals camped round a city who must agree to attack or retreat, communicating by messenger, with some of the generals traitors. The work behind it was practical: it came from a NASA-funded project on computers for aircraft control, where a faulty unit sending different readings to different neighbours was a known hazard.

A Byzantine node is one that deviates from the protocol in any way at all. The defining behaviour is equivocation: saying different things to different peers. A crashed node harms nobody's reasoning, since silence is the same for everyone. An equivocating node gives each peer a view that looks consistent to that peer, and the views don't match.

One tool limits what a traitor can do: message authentication. If every message is signed by its sender, a traitor can still lie in its own name and can't forge a statement from anyone else, or alter one in transit. All practical Byzantine protocols assume this. It reduces the traitor to equivocating and withholding, which is quite enough.

Why 3f + 1

The number comes from two requirements pulling against each other.

Progress. Up to f nodes may never answer. A node that waited for everyone would wait for ever, so it must be willing to act after hearing from n − f.

Safety. Among those n − f replies, up to f may be from traitors, for the f that stayed silent could have been honest and merely slow. So the honest replies it can count on number n − 2f. For the honest ones to outvote the liars it needs n − 2f > f, which is n > 3f. The smallest such system has n = 3f + 1: four nodes to survive one traitor, seven for two, ten for three.

The quorum size follows. A node acts on 2f + 1 matching messages. Any two such sets, out of 3f + 1 nodes, share at least f + 1 members, and at most f of those are traitors, so the two sets share at least one honest node. An honest node doesn't say two different things. So two quorums can never have endorsed conflicting values. In the crash model two majorities share at least one node and that was sufficient, since any node could be believed. Here the overlap must be large enough to be sure of containing a truthful one.

Three nodes show the difficulty directly. Suppose a leader and two followers, with a majority of two deciding. A traitorous leader tells one follower "the value is X" and the other "the value is Y". Each follower has a majority, itself and the leader, for a different value. Each could ask the other what it was told, and that only moves the problem: when the two reports differ, a follower can't tell whether the leader lied to it or its fellow follower is lying about what the leader said. With one fault among three, no protocol can sort it out. The panel lets you try.

PBFT phases

Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance, published by Miguel Castro and Barbara Liskov in 1999, was the first Byzantine protocol fast enough to consider using, within a small factor of an unreplicated service. It is state machine replication (Chapter 15): replicas agree on an order for client requests and each executes them in that order. Like Raft it works in numbered views, each with one primary that proposes the order. A request then passes through three phases.

PhaseWho sendsA replica moves on when it hasWhat that establishes
Pre-prepareThe primary, to allThe proposal, correctly signed, for a sequence number it hasn't accepted another request forA proposed order. Nothing more: the primary may have told others something else
PrepareEvery replica, to allThe pre-prepare and 2f matching PREPAREs from other replicas. It is now preparedNo other request can be prepared at this sequence number in this view. A lying primary has been caught or made harmless
CommitEvery prepared replica, to all2f + 1 matching COMMITs. It executes the request and repliesEnough replicas know it was prepared that the decision survives a change of primary

The second phase is the one with no counterpart in Raft. A Raft follower accepts what its leader sends. A PBFT replica treats the primary's proposal as a claim, and checks it against what everyone else was told, since 2f + 1 replicas vouching for the same proposal can't coexist with 2f + 1 vouching for a different one. The third phase exists for the same reason Raft has a commit rule: a replica that is prepared knows the order is fixed in this view, and doesn't yet know that enough others know it for the next primary to be obliged to keep it.

record Cluster(int n) {
	int f() { return (n - 1) / 3; }                 // traitors tolerated: 1 of 4, 2 of 7, 3 of 10
	int quorum() { return 2 * f() + 1; }
}

void onMessage(Signed<Message> m) {
	if (!keys.verify(m)) return;                    // not from who it claims: discard
	log.add(m.body());                              // stored per (view, sequence, digest, sender)
	var k = m.body().slot();
	if (prepared(k) && sentCommit.add(k)) broadcast(new Commit(k, me));
	if (committed(k) && executed.add(k)) executeInSequenceOrder(k);
}

boolean prepared(Slot k) {                          // the proposal, and 2f others who were told the same
	return log.hasPrePrepare(k) && distinctSenders(log.prepares(k)) >= 2 * cluster.f();
}
boolean committed(Slot k) {
	return prepared(k) && distinctSenders(log.commits(k)) >= cluster.quorum();
}

A faulty primary. If the primary proposes nothing, or proposes conflicting things so that nobody can gather a quorum, the replicas' timers run out and they start a view change. Each sends the next primary a signed account of everything it has prepared. The new primary collects 2f + 1 of these and must re-propose any request that appears in them as prepared, with its original sequence number. Since anything committed was prepared at 2f + 1 replicas, and any 2f + 1 accounts overlap that set in an honest one, nothing committed can be left out. The primary rotates through the replicas in turn, so after at most f bad primaries an honest one is reached.

The client takes part as well. It can't trust a single reply, so it waits for f + 1 replicas to return the same result, which guarantees at least one of them is honest.

Things to notice. As it opens there are four replicas and the primary is a traitor. It tells nodes 1 and 2 that the value is X, and node 3 that it is Y. In the prepare phase the three compare what they were told. Nodes 1 and 2 each collect three matching messages, the quorum, and node 3 collects two. In the commit phase the traitor backs X to the first pair and Y to node 3, and it makes no difference: nodes 1 and 2 decide X, and node 3 decides nothing, having no quorum for Y. No two honest replicas disagree, and that is the whole of the guarantee. Now set the replicas to 3 with the same single traitor. Node 1 decides X and node 2 decides Y, each with a full quorum of two. It is the three-node argument from earlier, carried out.

Go back to 4 replicas and raise the traitors to 2, one more than the design allows. Agreement breaks: nodes 1 and 2 decide differently, each holding three matching messages, two of them from traitors. With 7 replicas, 2 traitors are survived and 3 are not. Across every combination the panel offers, honest replicas disagree only when the traitors outnumber what the cluster was built for.

Silence is the lesser threat. Make the traitors silent with one of them the primary: view 0 produces nothing, the replicas time out, node 1 takes over, and everyone decides. Make two of four silent and nothing is ever decided, and nothing wrong is decided either. Last, set the traitors to 0 and read the message count: 24 for four replicas and 84 for seven, for one request. Raft would have used 6 and 12.

Cost

  • Replicas. 3f + 1 where a crash-tolerant system needs 2f + 1. To survive one fault: four machines, not three. And for the tolerance to mean anything the replicas must fail independently: four copies of the same software with the same bug, or four machines with the same administrator password, are one fault.
  • Messages. Two all-to-all rounds make the normal case quadratic: with n replicas, about 2n² messages for each request, or each batch of requests. Raft sends about 2n. At n = 4 that is 24 against 6. At n = 100 it is close to twenty thousand against two hundred. A view change in the original protocol is worse, of the order of n³.
  • Cryptography. Every message is authenticated and every receipt verified. PBFT used cheap message authentication codes between each pair of nodes to keep this affordable. Later protocols use signatures and combine many into one.
  • Scale. The quadratic term keeps classical PBFT to a few tens of replicas. Protocols since, HotStuff in particular, bring the common case down to linear by routing votes through the leader and aggregating signatures, which is what allows the hundreds of validators in Chapter 34.

Where BFT is used

Three settings justify the cost.

  • No single owner. When the replicas are run by different organisations that don't fully trust one another, "the operator of node 3 is dishonest" is a fault to plan for. This is the world of consortium ledgers and public blockchains, and the next chapter.
  • Safety-critical control. Aircraft and spacecraft use several flight computers that vote, designed so that one computer delivering inconsistent values to the others can't mislead them. The faults here are physical: a failing component that reads as 1 to one receiver and 0 to another.
  • Key infrastructure under attack. A few systems that guard high-value secrets or roots of trust replicate across separate administrative domains so that one compromised site isn't enough.

And one setting, the largest, where it is almost never used: services inside a company's own datacentres. The replicas there run the same code, are deployed by the same pipeline and are managed by the same people. An attacker who can take over one can usually take over all, and a bug that makes one lie makes all of them lie, so the assumption of at most f faults in 3f + 1 would not hold when it was needed. The Byzantine-looking faults that do occur in such places, flipped bits, corrupt disk blocks, truncated messages, are handled far more cheaply by checksums on everything stored and sent, which turn a lying node into one that is detected and ignored: a crash fault. The S3 incident in Chapter 23 was a Byzantine fault in the strict sense, and the remedy was a checksum.

Use case: Hyperledger Fabric took PBFT out, and years later put BFT back

Hyperledger Fabric is a ledger platform for groups of companies, a supply chain's members or a set of banks, who want a shared record without one of them owning it. That is the textbook case for Byzantine tolerance, and its history shows how the cost is weighed in practice. Fabric's first releases, the 0.6 line in 2016, used PBFT directly: every validating peer ran the three phases for every batch of transactions. For version 1.0 in 2017 the project redesigned the system, separating the job of putting transactions in order from the job of executing and validating them, and the ordering service that shipped was built on Apache Kafka. That made ordering crash-fault tolerant only. Version 1.4 added an ordering service built on Raft, also crash-tolerant, which became the recommended choice. For about seven years the best-known permissioned blockchain ran with no Byzantine tolerance in the component that decides the order of everything. The reasons were the ones in this chapter. PBFT's all-to-all rounds limited throughput and the number of nodes. In many real deployments the ordering nodes were all operated by one member or one cloud provider, so replicas tolerating each other's dishonesty protected against little. And the redesign contained the damage a dishonest orderer could do: it can't forge a transaction, since each carries signatures from the organisations that endorsed it, and every peer validates each block for itself. A bad orderer could censor or reorder, and couldn't invent. A Byzantine ordering service finally arrived in version 3.0, in 2024, for consortia in which several organisations do each run ordering nodes and want none of them to be trusted. The sequence is a fair summary of the field: Byzantine agreement is chosen when the participants are truly independent and the harm of one lying is real, and until both hold, a crash-tolerant log with signatures on the data is what gets deployed.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Assuming three replicas tolerate one traitor. Three tolerate one crash. One traitor needs four.
  • Using a majority as the quorum. Two majorities may overlap only in a traitor. The quorum is 2f + 1 of 3f + 1.
  • Counting identical replicas as independent. The same software, operator or cloud account is one point of failure.
  • Reaching for BFT inside one organisation. Checksums, signatures on data and access control address the real threats at a fraction of the cost.
  • A client trusting one reply. It needs f + 1 matching replies, or a proof it can verify.
  • Forgetting liveness. With more than f replicas down, a Byzantine system stops. It needs more machines than Raft to stay up, not fewer.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is a Byzantine fault, and why does tolerating f of them need 3f + 1 nodes?

How a strong answer sounds
A Byzantine fault is when a node doesn't just stop but behaves arbitrarily: it can send wrong or conflicting information to different nodes, whether from a bug, corrupted hardware or a deliberate attack. Crash-tolerant consensus like Raft assumes nodes are honest when they speak, so it needs 2f + 1 nodes and majority quorums. With Byzantine nodes you need 3f + 1. The reasoning: you can't wait for all nodes because f might never respond, so you must decide after hearing from n minus f. But among those responses, f could be from faulty nodes, since the ones you didn't hear from might simply be slow honest ones. So you can only rely on n minus 2f honest responses, and they need to outnumber the f possible liars: n minus 2f greater than f gives n greater than 3f. The quorum is 2f + 1, and any two such quorums overlap in at least f + 1 nodes, so at least one honest node is in both, which prevents two conflicting decisions. So four nodes tolerate one Byzantine fault, seven tolerate two.

Deeper: Walk through PBFT's normal case. Why are there three phases, and what does it cost?

How a strong answer sounds
PBFT is state machine replication with a primary per view. The client sends a request to the primary. Phase one, pre-prepare: the primary assigns a sequence number and broadcasts the request with it. Phase two, prepare: each replica that accepts the pre-prepare broadcasts a prepare message. A replica is prepared when it has the pre-prepare and 2f matching prepares from others, a quorum of 2f + 1 including the primary. Because quorums intersect in an honest node, no two honest replicas can be prepared for different requests at the same view and sequence number, so this phase defeats a primary that equivocates. Phase three, commit: prepared replicas broadcast commit; when a replica has 2f + 1 commits it executes and replies to the client, which waits for f + 1 matching replies. The commit phase is needed for safety across view changes: being prepared tells one replica the order is fixed within this view, but a new primary only learns about it from 2f + 1 replicas' reports, so the decision must be known to be prepared at a quorum before anyone executes. If the primary is faulty, replicas time out and run a view change, in which the new primary collects 2f + 1 prepared certificates and re-proposes anything that might have committed. Cost: 3f + 1 replicas, and O(n squared) messages per request in the normal case because prepare and commit are all-to-all, plus authentication on every message; view change is more expensive still. So classical PBFT is practical for perhaps tens of nodes. Later protocols such as HotStuff make the leader collect votes and use aggregated signatures to get linear communication and a simpler view change, which is what large proof-of-stake systems use.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • A Byzantine node may do anything, above all tell different peers different things. Causes include bugs and hardware faults as well as attackers.
  • Tolerating f of them needs 3f + 1 replicas and quorums of 2f + 1, so that any two quorums share an honest member.
  • Signatures stop a traitor forging others' messages. It can still lie in its own name, and withhold.
  • PBFT: the primary proposes, replicas cross-check the proposal in the prepare phase, and the commit phase makes the decision survive a change of primary.
  • The cost is more replicas, quadratic messages, and cryptography on every message.
  • It is used between parties that don't trust each other and in safety-critical control. Inside one organisation, checksums turn most such faults into crashes.

Check your understanding

What can a Byzantine node do that a crashed node can't?

Equivocation is the defining behaviour. Silence looks the same to everyone, and a lie can be tailored to each listener.

How many replicas are needed to tolerate two Byzantine faults?

3f + 1 with f = 2. Five would tolerate two crashes.

In a system of 3f + 1 replicas, why is the quorum 2f + 1 and not a simple majority?

Two bare majorities could overlap only in traitors, who would happily endorse both of two conflicting values.

What does PBFT's prepare phase protect against?

Replicas compare what they were told. Two conflicting proposals can't both gather 2f + 1 matching messages.

Why do most services inside one company's datacentres not use Byzantine fault tolerance?

The f-of-3f+1 assumption fails when one bug or one intrusion affects every replica. Detecting corruption turns it into a crash fault.
