Raft II: Log Replication & Safety

Chapter 17 ended with a leader and nothing for it to do. This chapter gives it the job consensus exists for: taking commands from clients and getting the same log, in the same order, onto every server (Chapter 15). One message type does nearly all of it. The interesting part is what happens around failures: a leader dies with entries only it holds, a follower returns with a log that disagrees with everyone else's, a server with half the history stands for election. Raft meets each case with a small rule, and the rules together guarantee that an entry, once committed, is in the log of every leader that will ever be elected. The chapter ends with the hardest thing a consensus group has to do, which is to change its own membership while it is running.

AppendEntries

Each entry in a Raft log holds a command and the term in which the leader created it. An entry is identified by its position, its index, and that term. The leader sends entries to followers with AppendEntries, which is also the heartbeat of Chapter 17 when it carries none.

FieldMeaning
termThe leader's term
prevLogIndex, prevLogTermThe index and term of the entry immediately before the new ones
entriesThe new entries, or none for a heartbeat
leaderCommitThe highest index the leader knows to be committed

The second row is the heart of it. A follower doesn't simply append what it is sent. It first checks that its own log has an entry at prevLogIndex whose term is prevLogTerm. If it doesn't, it refuses, and the leader tries again starting one entry earlier.

synchronized AppendReply onAppendEntries(long term, int prevIndex, long prevTerm, List<Entry> entries, int leaderCommit) {
	if (term < currentTerm) return AppendReply.rejected(currentTerm);          // a stale leader
	if (term > currentTerm) stepDown(term);
	resetElectionTimer();
	if (prevIndex > 0 && (log.size() < prevIndex || log.termAt(prevIndex) != prevTerm))
		return AppendReply.rejected(currentTerm);                              // our log doesn't join on to theirs here
	int index = prevIndex;
	for (Entry e : entries) {
		index++;
		if (log.size() >= index && log.termAt(index) != e.term()) log.truncateFrom(index);   // a conflicting entry, and all after it, go
		if (log.size() < index) log.append(e);
	}
	persist();
	if (leaderCommit > commitIndex) commitIndex = Math.min(leaderCommit, index);
	return AppendReply.accepted(currentTerm, index);
}

Log matching

The consistency check maintains a property that makes logs easy to reason about. If two logs contain an entry with the same index and the same term, the logs are identical in every entry up to that index. Two facts give it. A leader creates at most one entry for each index in its term and never moves it, so index and term together name one command. And a follower adds an entry only after confirming that the one before it matches, which by the same reasoning confirms the one before that, back to the start.

So to compare a follower's log with its own, a leader needs only to find the last position where index and term agree. Everything before it is identical. Everything after it on the follower is wrong, left over from some earlier leader that failed before getting it committed, and the leader overwrites it. The leader keeps, for each follower, a nextIndex: the next entry to send. It starts just past the end of the leader's own log. Each refusal moves it back by one, until the check passes. From that point the follower's log is made to match the leader's.

A leader never changes or deletes entries in its own log. Only followers' logs are ever cut back, and only where they conflict with the leader's.

Commit index

An entry is committed once the leader that created it has stored it on a majority of the servers. From then on it is permanent, the leader applies it to its state machine and answers the client. The leader tracks the highest committed index and includes it in every AppendEntries, and each follower applies entries up to that point as it learns of them.

There is one subtlety, and it is the rule most often left out of implementations. A new leader may find entries from an earlier term in its log that aren't yet known to be committed. It replicates them, as it does everything in its log. It must not treat such an entry as committed merely because it has reached a majority. An old-term entry on a majority can still be overwritten, if the leader then fails and a server holding a later-term entry in that position wins the next election. So the rule is: a leader commits by counting replicas only for entries of its own term. Once one of those is committed, every earlier entry is committed with it, by the log matching property. For that reason a new leader's first act is to append an empty entry in its own term and replicate it, which settles everything before it.

Election restriction

Replication alone doesn't make a committed entry safe. A server that was down while the entry was committed doesn't have it. If that server were elected, it would overwrite the followers' logs with its own shorter one, and the entry would be gone. Chapter 17's voting rules allow that. The third rule prevents it.

A server refuses its vote to a candidate whose log is less up to date than its own. "Up to date" compares the last entries of the two logs: the one whose last entry has the higher term is more up to date, and if the terms are equal, the longer log is.

Candidate's last entryVoter's last entryVote?Why
index 7, term 3index 5, term 3YesSame last term, and the candidate's log is longer
index 5, term 3index 7, term 3NoSame last term, and the candidate's log is shorter
index 4, term 4index 9, term 3YesA later last term wins, whatever the lengths
index 9, term 2index 4, term 3NoThe candidate's long log ends in an older term

The argument that this is enough is the one from Chapter 16. A committed entry is on a majority. A winning candidate has votes from a majority. The two majorities share a server, which has the entry and voted for the candidate, and it would only have voted if the candidate's log was at least as up to date as its own. With a little more care about terms, that gives the property the whole design aims at: every leader's log contains every committed entry. A leader never needs to be sent entries it lacks. Log entries flow in one direction only, from leader to followers.

Membership changes

Sooner or later a server has to be replaced, or the cluster grown from three to five. The difficulty is that "a majority" is defined by the list of members, and the servers can't all switch lists at the same instant. For a moment some servers believe in the old configuration and some in the new, and it is possible for a majority of the old list and a majority of the new list to have no server in common. Each could elect a leader in the same term.

MethodHow it worksCost
Switch directlyEvery server adopts the new list when it hears of itUnsafe: two disjoint majorities can exist during the change
Joint consensus, the original Raft methodAn intermediate configuration in which decisions need a majority of the old list and a majority of the new. Once that is committed, move to the new list aloneSafe for any change. Two steps, and more to implement
One server at a timeAdd or remove a single server in each change. Majorities of two lists that differ by one server always overlapSimple. Larger changes take several rounds

Either way, the new configuration is itself an entry in the log, replicated and committed by the ordinary mechanism, and a server uses the latest configuration in its log from the moment it has it. Two practical points go with every method. A new server starts with an empty log, and counting it as a voter at once would make the cluster depend on a member that can't yet acknowledge anything. So it joins first as a non-voting learner, receives the log, and is promoted when it has caught up. And when the leader is the server being removed, it finishes committing the change and then steps down.

🐞
Use case: the bug in Raft's simpler membership change

The one-server-at-a-time method comes from Diego Ongaro's doctoral dissertation, published in 2014, where it was offered as the simpler alternative to joint consensus and was widely adopted for that reason. In 2015 Ongaro announced on the Raft mailing list that it had a bug, which had been reported to him by people implementing it. The argument that single-server changes are safe says that the majorities of consecutive configurations overlap. That is true of the configuration before a change and the one after it. The bug arises when two changes are started from the same base configuration by leaders in different terms, neither having been committed. A leader of a four-server cluster begins adding a fifth and fails after reaching one other server. A new leader, elected by servers that never saw that change, begins removing a member instead. There are now two uncommitted configurations, each one step from the original and two steps from each other, and a majority of one need not overlap a majority of the other. With the right sequence of failures, two leaders could each commit an entry in the same position. The fix is one line of this chapter put to a new use: a leader must not begin a configuration change until it has committed an entry of its own term. That guarantees it has learnt of, and settled, any change its predecessors left unfinished. The episode is instructive beyond its content. Raft had been designed for understandability, specified formally and checked with a model checker, and the core algorithm had a machine-checked proof by 2015. The membership change, an extension outside what had been proved, is where the error was. The parts of a protocol that are exercised least and examined least are where its bugs survive longest.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Committing an old-term entry by counting replicas. It can still be overwritten. Commit an entry of the current term, and the earlier ones follow.
  • Granting votes without comparing logs. A server missing committed entries can then become leader and erase them.
  • Comparing logs by length alone. The term of the last entry comes first.
  • Truncating a follower's log when nothing conflicts. A delayed, shorter AppendEntries would then delete entries a later one had added.
  • Applying entries before they are committed, or answering a client from a leader that hasn't confirmed it still leads.
  • Adding several voting members at once, or counting an empty new server as a voter.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: In Raft, when is a log entry committed, and why is a committed entry never lost?

How a strong answer sounds
An entry is committed when the leader that created it, in its current term, has replicated it to a majority of the servers. At that point the leader applies it and answers the client, and tells followers through the commit index in later AppendEntries messages. It can't be lost because of how leaders are elected. A candidate needs votes from a majority, and a server only votes for a candidate whose log is at least as up to date as its own, comparing the term of the last entry and then the length. Any majority of voters overlaps the majority that stores the committed entry, so at least one voter has it, and that voter would refuse a candidate without it. So every future leader already has every committed entry in its log. And leaders never delete or overwrite their own entries; they only append, and they make followers' logs match theirs. So the entry stays at the same index in every leader's log from then on, and eventually on every server.

Deeper: Why can't a Raft leader commit an entry from a previous term as soon as it is stored on a majority?

How a strong answer sounds
Because being on a majority doesn't yet protect an old-term entry from being overwritten. The scenario from the paper goes like this, with five servers. S1 is leader in term 2 and puts an entry at index 2 on itself and S2, then crashes. S5 becomes leader in term 3 with votes from S3 and S4, appends a different entry at index 2 in its own log only, and crashes. S1 comes back, is elected in term 4, and continues replicating its term-2 entry at index 2, which now reaches S3: it is on three of five servers. If S1 declared it committed now, and then crashed, S5 could still win an election: its last entry is from term 3, which is more up to date than a log ending in term 2, so S2, S3 and S4 would all vote for it. S5 would then overwrite index 2 everywhere with its term-3 entry, and a "committed" entry would be gone. So counting replicas isn't sufficient for old-term entries. The rule is that a leader only commits by counting for entries from its own term. If S1 had first got a term-4 entry on to a majority, S5 could no longer win, since the voters' logs would end in term 4, and by the log matching property committing the term-4 entry commits everything before it. That is also why a new leader appends a no-op entry as soon as it is elected: it gives it a current-term entry to commit, which settles all the earlier ones and lets it serve reads safely.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • AppendEntries carries new entries together with the index and term of the one before them. A follower whose log doesn't match there refuses.
  • Log matching: the same index and term means identical logs up to that point. The leader backs up to the last match and overwrites what follows.
  • An entry is committed when its own leader has it on a majority. A leader counts replicas only for entries of its current term.
  • Voters refuse a candidate with a less up-to-date log, so every leader already holds every committed entry.
  • Leaders only append. Entries flow from leader to follower, and only uncommitted follower entries are ever discarded.
  • Membership changes go through the log, one server at a time or by joint consensus, with new servers catching up before they vote.

Check your understanding

A follower receives AppendEntries with prevLogIndex 6 and prevLogTerm 3. Its own entry 6 is from term 2. What does it do?

The logs don't join at 6. The leader backs up until it finds a matching entry, and replaces everything after it.

Two servers each have an entry at index 8 from term 4. What does the log matching property say?

Each entry was only appended after its predecessor was checked, so agreement at 8 implies agreement all the way back.

A candidate's log ends at index 9, term 2. A voter's log ends at index 4, term 3. Does the voter grant its vote?

The last term is compared first, and length only breaks ties.

A new leader in term 5 finds an entry from term 3 in its log, and gets it on to a majority. When may it treat that entry as committed?

An old-term entry on a majority can still lose to a later-term entry elsewhere. A committed entry of the current term rules that out.

Why can't a cluster switch from three members to five in a single step?

Two of the old three, and three of the new five, can be disjoint. Joint consensus or one-at-a-time changes keep every pair of majorities overlapping.
