Case Study: Apache Kafka

In 2010 the engineers at LinkedIn had a problem that every growing company reaches. Data was produced in dozens of places, page views, searches, profile edits, database changes, server metrics, and wanted in dozens of others: the search index, the recommendation models, the data warehouse, the monitoring system, a new team's experiment. Each pair had been joined by its own pipeline, built when it was needed and owned by whoever built it, and the number of pipelines grew as the product of the two lists. The existing message queues couldn't carry the volume, and fell over when a consumer got behind. A small team took an idea that databases had used internally for forty years, the append-only log, and made it a service that every team could write to and read from. They named it after a writer. Fifteen years on, Kafka is the default way that organisations move event data, a single deployment may carry trillions of records a day, and its central idea has been turned on Kafka itself twice: once to store where its consumers are, and once to replace the coordination service it depended on. Chapters 28 and 29 covered the mechanisms. This chapter is the design story: why a log, what the choice made easy, and how the system changed.

The log abstraction

With ten sources of data and ten places that want it, connecting each to each is a hundred pipelines, each with its own format, its own failure behaviour and its own owner. Put one system in the middle, to which every source writes and from which every destination reads, and it is twenty connections. The idea is old. What kept it from working was the thing in the middle.

The message queues of the time were built round a different job: hand each message to a worker, and forget it once the worker confirms. The broker tracked, for every message and every consumer, whether it had been delivered and acknowledged. That bookkeeping was held in memory, and it grew with the backlog. A consumer that stopped for an hour left the broker holding an hour of messages it was built to hold for milliseconds, and performance collapsed exactly when a consumer was in trouble. A nightly batch job, which by design is a consumer twenty-four hours behind, could not be a consumer at all.

Kafka's answer was to do less. A topic's partition is a file that is appended to. A record's position in it, its offset, is its identity. The broker doesn't know or care who has read what: each consumer remembers its own offset and asks for "the records after this one". Records are deleted when they reach a certain age, whether or not anyone has read them.

The last row is the honest cost. A queue is the better tool for handing out independent tasks to a pool of workers. A log is the better tool for a stream of facts that several parties want, each in order and at its own pace.

The simplicity is also where the speed comes from, as Chapter 29 described: sequential writes, the operating system's page cache in place of a cache of its own, records sent from file to socket without passing through the broker's memory, and batching from the producer through the disk to the consumer.

A log is also a table, slowly. Jay Kreps, one of Kafka's authors, set out the larger idea in a 2013 essay. A database table is the result of applying a sequence of changes, and the sequence, the changelog, contains strictly more information than the table: replay it and you have the table again, as of any moment. Kafka supports this directly with log compaction, a retention policy that keeps the latest record for each key and discards older ones, so that a compacted topic is a table written as a log. Capturing a database's changes into such a topic (Chapter 22 met this as change data capture) lets any number of other systems build and maintain their own copy of the data in whatever form suits them, and rebuild it from scratch whenever they like.

Properties props = new Properties();
props.put(StreamsConfig.APPLICATION_ID_CONFIG, "views-by-country");
props.put(StreamsConfig.PROCESSING_GUARANTEE_CONFIG, StreamsConfig.EXACTLY_ONCE_V2);

StreamsBuilder builder = new StreamsBuilder();
KTable<String, Profile> profiles = builder.table("user-profiles");          // a compacted topic, read as a table: latest value for each key
builder.<String, PageView>stream("page-views")                          // an endless stream of events, keyed by user
		.join(profiles, (view, profile) -> view.withCountry(profile.country()))   // look each event up in the table
		.groupBy((userId, view) -> view.country())
		.count(Materialized.as("views-by-country"))                      // state on local disk, backed by its own changelog topic
		.toStream()
		.to("views-by-country", Produced.with(Serdes.String(), Serdes.Long()));   // a table again, as a stream of updates
new KafkaStreams(builder.build(), props).start();

Things to notice. At minute 70 the fraud service has been down for half an hour. It is 30 minutes behind, and nothing else has changed: the indexer is current, the loader is waiting for its half-hourly run, and the broker is holding the fraud service's place without knowing it. Drag to minute 100. The service is back, resumes from minute 40 exactly, and by minute 160 has caught up, having lost nothing. At minute 100 a fourth consumer has also appeared, a new team's service started at minute 90, replaying from the first record in the log.

Now lengthen the outage to two and a half hours, past the two hours the log keeps. From minute 160 the fraud service's place has expired, and when it returns at minute 190 it has lost 30 minutes of records, 1.8 million of them, with no error raised anywhere. Set retention to 7 days and the same outage costs nothing. Set it to 30 minutes and even the one-hour outage loses 1.8 million records. Retention is the length of outage a consumer can survive, and its lag is how much of that has been used up.

Replication

The first versions of Kafka had no replication at all. A broker's disk was the only copy of its partitions, and a failed machine meant lost data. For the original workload, activity events on their way to a batch warehouse, that was a tolerable trade. As the log came to carry things that mattered more, it wasn't. Replication arrived in version 0.8 in 2013, with the leader-and-followers, in-sync-replica design that Chapter 29 covered: fewer copies for the same tolerance than a majority quorum needs, at the price of relying on something else to keep the membership consistent.

The rest of the replication story is one of a sound design having its edges found. The Jepsen analysis of 2013 showed acknowledged writes being lost when the in-sync set shrank to one, and led to min.insync.replicas. Electing a stale replica was made opt-in. In 2017 the rule by which a restarted follower trimmed its log was replaced: it had used the high watermark, which a follower learns a moment late, and in certain sequences of crashes that let two replicas keep different records at the same offset. The fix tags each stretch of the log with the epoch of the leader that wrote it, the same idea as a Raft term, and has a follower ask the leader where that epoch ended. Each of these was found by testing or by specification, of the kinds in Chapters 37 and 38, and none by inspection.

From ZooKeeper to KRaft

For most of its life a Kafka cluster was two distributed systems. The brokers held the data. A ZooKeeper ensemble (Chapter 31) held everything the brokers needed to agree on: which brokers were alive, which one was the controller, which topics existed, who led each partition and who was in sync. At first it even held each consumer group's offsets, written on every commit.

That last use was the first to go, and the reason is Chapter 31's warning. Offsets are written constantly, and a coordination service runs every write through consensus and keeps everything in memory. So Kafka moved offsets into an ordinary, compacted Kafka topic, __consumer_offsets: a consumer's position became a record in a log. It was the first time the system's central idea was applied to the system.

The second time took longer. The remaining metadata stayed in ZooKeeper, with one broker, the controller, as its only writer, and three difficulties grew with cluster size.

  • Slow failover. A new controller had to read the entire cluster's metadata from ZooKeeper before it could do anything. With hundreds of thousands of partitions that took minutes, during which no partition anywhere could change leader.
  • Three copies of the truth. The metadata was in ZooKeeper, in the controller's memory, and in each broker's memory, kept in step by notifications that could be missed. Brokers sometimes acted on a view the controller no longer held.
  • A second system to run, with its own configuration, security model, monitoring and failure modes.

The replacement, proposed in 2019 and known as KRaft, stores the metadata the way Kafka stores everything else. Each change to the cluster is a record appended to a metadata log. A small group of controller nodes replicate that log among themselves with Raft, and one of them is the active controller. Every broker follows the same log, exactly as a consumer follows a topic, with an offset.

The gain that matters most is the fourth row. A log gives every reader a position, and a position makes "is this node up to date?" a comparison of two numbers. The same property that lets a data consumer resume after an hour away lets a broker that was partitioned for a minute know precisely what it missed. The migration itself took about six years from proposal to the removal of the old code, most of it spent on the unglamorous part: letting existing clusters move without stopping.

Exactly-once pipelines

Until 2017 the standard advice was that Kafka gave at-least-once delivery and consumers must cope. Version 0.11 added the idempotent producer and transactions, covered in Chapter 28, and with them the claim of exactly-once processing. What that bought is best seen across a whole pipeline, where each hop has its own guarantee and the weakest decides the result.

So "Kafka supports exactly-once" is true of a bounded region: reading from Kafka, computing, and writing to Kafka. A pipeline that starts and ends elsewhere needs the techniques of Chapter 28 at each boundary.

Use case: The New York Times keeps everything it has published in one log

In 2017 engineers at The New York Times described how they had rebuilt the system that feeds the paper's content to its website, apps and search. The older arrangement was the one this chapter opened with. Several content management systems, each with its own API and its own data model, and many consumers, each calling those APIs in its own way, each with its own idea of an article and its own gaps when a call failed. The replacement is unusually pure as a use of the log. Every piece of content the paper has published, each article, image, tag and byline, back to its first edition in 1851, is written as a record to a single Kafka topic. The topic has one partition, so that all of it is in one total order and a reference from one item to another never arrives before the thing referred to. It is configured never to expire. The log is the system of record: the authoritative store of the paper's published output is a Kafka topic, and nothing else is. The full history was reported as under a hundred gigabytes, which fits on any broker. Every consuming system builds its own view from it. The service behind the website reads the log from the first record and keeps a store shaped for serving pages. The search system reads the same log from the first record and builds an index. A new service, or a new version of an old one with a different data model, starts at offset zero and catches up, and nobody has to export anything for it. The properties in this chapter's first table are what make that workable: consumers keep their own positions, reading is cheap however far behind the reader is, and the broker neither knows nor cares how many readers there are. Few organisations can use one partition, since few have so modest a write rate. The principle travels: keep the facts in an ordered log, and treat every database, index and cache as something derived from it that can be thrown away and rebuilt.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Using Kafka as a task queue. One slow record blocks its partition, and workers can't outnumber partitions. A queue fits that job better.
  • Retention shorter than the longest outage you must survive. A consumer that returns after its records have expired has lost them, silently.
  • No monitoring of consumer lag. Lag is the one number that says whether a consumer is keeping up, and how close it is to the edge of retention.
  • Treating a normal topic as a database. Without compaction or unlimited retention the history disappears. Decide which the topic is.
  • Assuming exactly-once reaches the sink. It covers Kafka to Kafka. The last hop needs an idempotent write.
  • Events that only make sense with the producer's database. Put the fact in the record. A consumer replaying last year's log can't ask what a row held then.
  • Changing a record's format without a plan. Old records stay in the log, and old consumers stay in production (P3).

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Why is Kafka built round a log, and how is that different from a message queue?

How a strong answer sounds
Kafka was built at LinkedIn to move large volumes of event data from many producers to many consumers, including slow ones like batch jobs. Traditional message queues track delivery per message per consumer and delete messages once acknowledged, which makes the broker do more work as backlog grows and means a message can't be re-read or given to a new consumer later. Kafka instead stores each topic partition as an append-only log on disk. Records get sequential offsets and are retained for a configured time or size, regardless of whether anyone has consumed them. The broker doesn't track who has read what; each consumer group keeps its own offset. That has several consequences: the broker's work per record is constant and is mostly sequential I/O, so throughput is very high; a consumer that is hours behind costs nothing extra; many independent consumer groups can read the same data; and a consumer can rewind to reprocess, or a new one can start from the beginning. The trade-off is that within a group one partition goes to one consumer, so it isn't a natural work queue, and ordering is only per partition. Because a log of changes can be replayed to rebuild state, Kafka also works as the backbone for change data capture and stream processing.

Deeper: Why did Kafka remove ZooKeeper, and what did it replace it with?

How a strong answer sounds
Kafka used ZooKeeper to store cluster metadata: broker membership, topic and partition configuration, partition leaders and in-sync replica sets, and to elect a controller broker that managed that state. It worked but had problems that grew with scale. Operating two distributed systems doubled the operational and security surface. Controller failover was slow, because a newly elected controller had to load all metadata from ZooKeeper, which is proportional to the number of partitions and could take minutes on large clusters, during which no leader elections could happen. Metadata lived in three places, ZooKeeper, the controller's memory and each broker's cache, updated by RPCs that could be missed, so views could diverge. And ZooKeeper's write throughput and memory model limited clusters to roughly a couple of hundred thousand partitions. KRaft, from KIP-500, replaces it with a built-in Raft quorum of controller nodes. Metadata is modelled as an event log, the cluster metadata topic, replicated by Raft among the controllers; the leader is the active controller. Brokers are observers that fetch the metadata log and apply it, so each knows its exact offset and can catch up from where it left off, with snapshots for bootstrap. Failover is fast because standby controllers already have the log in memory. It scales to millions of partitions and makes Kafka a single system to deploy. It was production-ready for new clusters in 3.3 and ZooKeeper mode was removed in 4.0. I'd add that the data plane still uses ISR replication, which depends on the controller for consistent leader and ISR state, so there are deliberately two replication protocols.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Kafka was built to replace a tangle of point-to-point pipelines with one log that every producer writes to and every consumer reads.
  • The broker appends and serves. Each consumer keeps its own offset, and records expire by age, not by being read.
  • That makes slow, repeated and brand-new consumers cheap, and makes Kafka a poor task queue.
  • A log of changes can rebuild a table. Compaction and change data capture turn Kafka into a source from which other stores are derived.
  • Replication came later, and was hardened by testing: minimum in-sync replicas, clean elections, leader epochs.
  • KRaft moved Kafka's metadata from ZooKeeper into a Raft-replicated log that brokers follow by offset.
  • Exactly-once covers reading from Kafka and writing to Kafka. The ends of a pipeline need their own guarantees.

Check your understanding

In Kafka, who keeps track of how far a consumer has read?

The broker only appends and serves ranges. That is why a slow consumer costs it nothing and many groups can read one topic.

A consumer is offline for three hours. The topic keeps records for two. What happens when it returns?

Retention is by age, whoever has or hasn't read. Lag must stay well inside the retention period.

What does log compaction keep?

A compacted topic is the current state of a table, in log form. Replaying it gives the latest value for every key.

What was the main problem with a controller failover when Kafka's metadata lived in ZooKeeper?

With KRaft the standby controllers have been following the metadata log all along, and take over almost at once.

A pipeline reads from Kafka, processes with transactions, writes to Kafka, and a connector then appends the results to a file store that can't deduplicate. What is the end-to-end guarantee?

A pipeline is as strong as its weakest hop. The sink needs an idempotent write for the guarantee to reach the end.
