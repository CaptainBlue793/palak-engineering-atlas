Case Study: GFS & HDFS

In the early 2000s Google had a problem nobody else had at that size. It was storing a copy of the web, and the logs of everything its users did, on thousands of the cheapest machines it could buy, and at that number a disk or a machine was failing every day. No file system on the market was built for it. In 2003 three of its engineers described the one they had written, the Google File System. Its design was a set of blunt simplifications. There is one master, which knows where everything is and keeps that knowledge in its own memory. Files are cut into very large pieces. Each piece is kept three times. And the guarantees given to applications are weaker than a normal file system's, on the reasoning that an application built to expect a repeated record is cheaper than a storage system built never to produce one. Within three years an open-source copy, HDFS, had become the base of the Hadoop ecosystem, and a generation of data processing was built on the two. This chapter covers how the design works, what its unusual append operation really promises, and how the single master, its most criticised feature, became in time exactly the limit its critics said it would.

Master and chunkservers

GFS was designed from four observations about Google's own workload, and it is only as good as they are true. Machines fail constantly, so recovery must be automatic and routine. Files are huge, many gigabytes each. They are written almost entirely by appending, and seldom overwritten. And what matters is throughput for bulk processing, far more than the response time of any one request.

A cluster has one master and hundreds or thousands of chunkservers. A file is divided into chunks of 64 megabytes. Each chunk is stored as an ordinary file on the local disks of several chunkservers, three by default.

The master holds only metadata, and all of it in memory: the tree of file names, the list of chunks making up each file, and the chunkservers on which each chunk lives. Changes to the first two are written to an operation log, which is copied to other machines before a change is acknowledged, and from which the master can rebuild its state after a crash. The third, the locations, is deliberately not stored. The master asks each chunkserver what it holds when it starts, and keeps up to date through regular heartbeats. A chunkserver has the final word on which chunks are on its disks, so there is nothing for the two to disagree about.

That separation is what makes a single master tolerable. It takes part in every operation and does very little work in each: a lookup in memory, a short reply. Data moves directly between clients and chunkservers. The master's other duties run in the background: noticing dead chunkservers by missed heartbeats, re-replicating chunks that have fallen below three copies, spreading load, and collecting the chunks of deleted files, which are removed lazily some days after deletion and not at once.

Chunks and replicas

Sixty-four megabytes was an unusually large unit, sixteen thousand times the block of an ordinary file system, and chosen on purpose. Fewer, larger chunks mean less metadata for the master to hold, fewer questions for clients to ask it, and long sequential transfers between a client and one chunkserver. The cost falls on small files, of which more below.

  • Placement. The three replicas go on different machines, and across more than one rack, so that the loss of a rack's switch or power doesn't take every copy. HDFS's default is one copy on the writer's own machine, one on a machine in another rack, and the third on a different machine in that second rack.
  • Integrity. Each chunkserver keeps a checksum for every small block of every chunk, verifies it on each read, and scans idle chunks in the background. A corrupt replica is reported, discarded and copied afresh from a good one. Disks returning wrong data silently is, at this scale, a daily event.
  • Stale replicas. Every chunk has a version number, raised each time it is opened for writing. A chunkserver that was down during a write comes back with an old version, which the master recognises and never serves.
  • Re-replication. When a chunkserver dies, the master copies each chunk it held from a surviving replica to a new machine, taking first the chunks that have lost the most copies.

Writes use the primary-backup pattern of Chapter 14, a chunk at a time. For each chunk being written the master grants a lease, of sixty seconds, to one replica, the primary. The primary chooses the order of all changes to that chunk and the others apply them in the same order. The data itself is pushed along a chain, from the client to the nearest replica and from each replica to the next nearest, so that every machine's outbound network is used in full, and only then does the primary give the short instruction to apply it.

Record append

Google's programs mostly didn't write to chosen positions in a file. They appended: hundreds of crawlers or log collectors adding records to one shared file, with other programs reading along behind. GFS made that an operation of its own, record append. The client supplies a record. The primary chooses the offset at which it goes, the current end of the chunk, writes it there, and tells the other replicas to write it at exactly that offset. Many clients can append to one file together with no locking between them.

The interesting part is what happens when a replica fails to write. The primary reports an error and the client tries again. The retry is a new append, at a new offset further on, on every replica. The space where the failed attempt went is left as it is: holding the record on the replicas that had succeeded, and holding nothing useful on the one that hadn't.

This is the at-least-once delivery of Chapter 28, built into a file system, with the same remedy on the reading side. Google's libraries for writing and reading records did the checksumming and the deduplication, so most programmers never saw it. The bargain is characteristic of the whole design: the storage layer is simpler and faster, and one library carries the cost of the weaker guarantee for everyone.

// Reading a file written by record append. The same loop yields the same records from any replica.
Set<RecordId> seen = new HashSet<>();
for (Region region : scan(chunk)) {
	if (!region.checksumMatches()) continue;        // padding, or what is left of a failed attempt
	Record r = region.decode();
	if (!seen.add(r.id())) continue;               // the same record again, from a retried append
	handle(r);
}

Things to notice. Append two records normally and the three replicas are identical. Now make the next one fail on replica 2. Record C lands in the fourth position on every replica, and the third position holds C on replicas 1 and 3 and a dead region on replica 2. The replicas are no longer the same bytes, and a reader of any of them gets A, B, C. Append one more, then choose the fourth option: E fails on replica 2, another client's F is appended, and E is retried. Readers of replicas 1 and 3 now get E before F, and a reader of replica 2 gets F before E. Each has every record once, and they disagree about the order.

The last option is the one to be clear about. G fails on replica 2 and its client gives up. G is on two replicas, visible to their readers, and absent from the third. No promise has been broken, since nobody was told that G was written. A program that needs to know must hear "succeeded", and anything short of that means "perhaps". In a test of 300 random runs, 2,573 appends in all, the replicas differed from one another at the end of every run, and no acknowledged record was missing from any of them.

The single-master bottleneck

One master holding everything in memory is the design's great simplification, and it sets two limits: how much one machine can remember, and what happens when that machine is unavailable.

Memory. The master needs a fixed amount of memory for every file and every chunk, whatever their size. In HDFS the usual planning figure is about 150 bytes for each. So the limit on a cluster is a count of objects, not a number of bytes, and the same petabyte can be easy or impossible depending on the files it is in.

This is the small-files problem. A file smaller than a block still costs a file entry and a block entry, so a million tiny files cost the master as much as a million huge ones, while holding almost nothing. Systems built on HDFS work round it by packing small items into large container files, and it is one reason why table formats and databases, and not raw files, became the usual way to use such storage.

Availability. If the master is down, nothing can be opened. GFS kept the outage short: the operation log was replicated, a replacement could start in tens of seconds, and read-only shadow masters served slightly stale metadata meanwhile. HDFS, for its first six years, had no such thing. Its NameNode was a single point of failure, and a restart on a big cluster could take an hour while it reloaded its image of the namespace and waited for every DataNode to report its blocks.

The fix that arrived in Hadoop 2 is assembled from this course's parts. Two NameNodes run, one active and one standby. The active one writes each change to its log on a group of journal nodes, and a change counts once a majority has it: a small replicated log, as in Chapter 18. The standby reads that log continuously and so is seconds behind. DataNodes send their block reports to both. A coordination service, ZooKeeper, decides which is active (Chapter 31), and before a standby takes over, the old active is fenced (Chapter 32): the journal nodes accept writes only from the holder of the newest epoch number, so a deposed NameNode that wakes from a pause can no longer change anything.

Google reached the end of the design first. In an interview in 2009 the engineer then responsible for GFS described how its assumptions had worn out. The workload had shifted from batch jobs to services people waited on, such as mail, for which a pause while a master recovered was unacceptable. Files had become smaller and far more numerous, and the master's memory, and the load of every client asking it questions, had become the limit on a cluster's size. The 64 MB chunk suited fewer and fewer uses. The successor, Colossus, keeps the split between metadata and data and removes the single master: metadata is held in a distributed database, and many metadata servers share the work. It also stores most data with erasure coding, which survives the same failures as three copies in about half the space.

Use case: Facebook, a hundred petabytes behind one NameNode

Around 2010 Facebook ran what was then described as the largest Hadoop cluster in the world: its data warehouse, some 2,000 machines holding 21 petabytes, which had passed 100 petabytes two years later. Every query the company's analysts ran, and every pipeline that fed its products, went through that cluster's single NameNode. The engineers wrote openly about what that meant. The NameNode's heap ran to many tens of gigabytes, and tuning the garbage collector so that it didn't pause for minutes was a specialised craft. Restarting it took about an hour, most of it spent reading its saved image and then waiting for two thousand DataNodes to report tens of millions of blocks. So every upgrade of the NameNode's software, and every change to its configuration, was an hour in which the warehouse was unavailable to the whole company, and since the cluster was so large and so busy, such changes were frequent. Facebook's analysis of its own incidents made a point that is easy to miss: most of the downtime came from this planned maintenance and not from crashes. Their answer, in 2011, was a system they called AvatarNode. A second NameNode ran as a hot standby, reading the active one's log of changes from shared storage as it was written, and receiving block reports from every DataNode, which had been altered to report to both. Which of the two was active was recorded in ZooKeeper, and clients looked it up there. A switch from one to the other took under a minute, and upgrades could be done by upgrading the standby and switching. It was released as open source, and the design that the Hadoop project itself shipped soon afterwards, with a quorum of journal nodes in place of Facebook's shared file server, follows the same plan. The history is this chapter's in miniature. The single master was what made the system simple enough to build, and at a certain size the work became making it less single.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Millions of small files. The master's memory is spent on names and block lists. Combine small items into large files.
  • Treating it as a general file system. It is built for large sequential reads and appends. Random small writes and low-latency reads fit it badly.
  • Assuming an append happened exactly once. With GFS-style record append, give every record an ID and a checksum.
  • Counting three replicas on one rack as three. A switch or a power feed takes them together. Placement must know the racks.
  • Reading "written" as "on disk". A write is in a pipeline of buffers until it is synced. Decide which is needed, and call for it.
  • Master failover with no fencing. Two active masters each handing out the same blocks corrupt the namespace.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How does GFS, or HDFS, store a large file reliably on unreliable machines?

How a strong answer sounds
The file is split into large fixed-size chunks, 64 megabytes in GFS and 128 in HDFS, and each chunk is stored on three different machines, spread across racks. There are two kinds of server. A single master, the NameNode in HDFS, keeps all the metadata in memory: the directory tree, which chunks make up each file, and which servers hold each chunk. Many chunkservers, or DataNodes, store the chunks as local files. To read, a client asks the master where a chunk is, then reads the data directly from one of the chunkservers, so file data never flows through the master. To write, the data is pushed through a pipeline of the three replicas, with one acting as primary to order the writes. Reliability comes from several mechanisms: chunkservers send heartbeats, and when one dies the master re-replicates its chunks from the remaining copies; every block has a checksum, verified on read and by background scans, so corrupted replicas are detected and replaced; and chunk version numbers identify stale replicas. The master's own state is protected by a replicated operation log and checkpoints, and in modern HDFS by a hot standby.

Deeper: What are the problems with a single master, and how were they addressed?

How a strong answer sounds
Three problems. First, capacity: all metadata is in the master's memory, roughly 150 bytes per file and per block in HDFS, so the cluster is limited by object count rather than bytes. Large files are fine, but hundreds of millions of small files exhaust the heap; that is the small-files problem. Second, availability: the master is a single point of failure, and a restart on a large cluster takes a long time because it must reload its namespace image and wait for block reports from every DataNode. Third, throughput: every metadata operation goes through one process, with a global lock in HDFS, and long garbage-collection pauses on a huge heap stall the whole cluster. The responses. For availability, HDFS added an active and a standby NameNode: the active writes its edit log to a quorum of journal nodes, the standby tails it and also receives block reports, ZooKeeper-based failover controllers choose the active, and the journal nodes enforce an epoch number so a stale active is fenced. For capacity and throughput, HDFS federation lets several independent NameNodes each manage a part of the namespace over the same DataNodes, and a router layer can present them as one; operationally people also consolidate small files into containers or use table formats, and erasure coding reduces storage though not metadata. Google went further and replaced GFS with Colossus, which stores metadata in a scalable distributed database with many metadata servers, removing the single master altogether, and object stores such as S3 take the same approach. The general lesson is that centralised metadata is a great simplification up to a scale, after which the metadata itself needs to be a distributed system.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • GFS was built for constant failure, huge files, appends and bulk throughput, and its simplifications fit those assumptions.
  • One master holds all metadata in memory. Chunkservers hold 64 MB chunks, three copies of each across racks. Data never passes through the master.
  • Checksums, version numbers, heartbeats and re-replication make loss of disks and machines a routine event.
  • Record append is at-least-once: replicas may hold duplicates and padding, and readers use checksums and record IDs.
  • The master limits a cluster by number of objects, hence the small-files problem, and was a single point of failure.
  • HDFS added a standby master over a quorum journal with fencing, and federation. Google replaced GFS with a system whose metadata is distributed.

Check your understanding

A client reads a 1 GB file from GFS. What passes through the master?

The master serves metadata only. Data flows between the client and chunkservers, which is why one master can serve a large cluster.

Why does the master not keep a permanent record of which chunkservers hold each chunk?

Disks fail and servers come and go. Asking the servers at start-up and through heartbeats is simpler than keeping a stored list correct.

A record append fails on one replica and the client retries successfully. What is on the replicas afterwards?

Nothing is undone. The retry goes further on, so some replicas hold the record twice and one has an unusable region.

An HDFS cluster stores one petabyte as ten billion files of 100 KB. What breaks first?

Twenty billion objects at about 150 bytes each is terabytes of heap. The limit is the count of objects, not the bytes.

In HDFS high availability, what stops a NameNode that has been replaced from continuing to change the namespace?

That is a fencing token, enforced by the store being written to. A paused former active can't append to the log when it wakes.
