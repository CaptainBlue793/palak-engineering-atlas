CAP & PACELC

The CAP theorem is the best-known result in distributed systems and probably the most misquoted. The popular version, "consistency, availability, partition tolerance: pick two", suggests a menu with three equal options, and that picture is wrong in a way that leads to bad designs. The theorem says something narrower and more useful. When the network splits a system in two, each part has to choose between answering requests that may be wrong and refusing to answer. It can't do neither. That is the whole of it, and it is only about what happens during the split. PACELC is a later extension that adds the part CAP leaves out: when nothing is broken there is still a choice to make, between consistency and speed, and that one is paid on every request. Chapter 9 showed these trade-offs as outcomes in a table. This chapter gives them their names and their precise statements.

The CAP theorem precisely

Eric Brewer put the idea forward in a conference talk in 2000, and Seth Gilbert and Nancy Lynch stated and proved it in 2002. Their statement uses three terms with exact meanings, each narrower than the everyday word.

TermIn the theorem it meansIt doesn't mean
ConsistencyThe system behaves as if there were one copy of the data: once a write completes, every later read returns it. This is linearizabilityThe C of ACID, which is about a database's own invariants
AvailabilityEvery request received by a node that hasn't crashed gets a response that isn't an error"Up 99.99% of the time". The theorem's version is absolute: every such node, every request
Partition toleranceThe system keeps its promises even if the network drops any messages between nodes, for any length of timeA feature a product may or may not have

The theorem: a system can't provide both consistency and availability if the network may partition. The proof needs two nodes and four messages.

Notice where the third property went. It isn't one of the options. Partitions happen: cables are cut, switches fail, a garbage-collection pause makes a node unreachable for seconds, and nobody designing a system gets to decide that they won't. So the menu of three is really a choice of one out of two, and only at certain times.

"Pick two" suggestsWhat the theorem says
You may give up partition tolerance and keep the other twoOnly by having no network between replicas, which means one machine. A system that claims both C and A is choosing one of them without saying which, when a partition comes
The choice is made once, for the whole systemIt is made for each operation, and only while a partition lasts. One system can refuse payments and go on serving the catalogue
Consistency and availability are all or nothingBoth come in degrees. Weaker consistency models and "available to most clients" are the space most real systems live in
An AP system is inconsistent, and a CP system is unavailableWith no partition, both can be consistent and available. They differ only in what they give up when one happens

CP vs AP systems

The useful part of CAP is the question it makes a designer answer in advance: when a node can't reach the others, what does it do with a request?

A CP design says: refuse. A node that can't confirm it has the latest data returns an error or waits. In practice this is done with majorities. The side of a partition that holds a majority of the replicas carries on, and the minority side stops serving. Nothing wrong is ever returned, and some clients get nothing. Coordination services, consensus-based databases and anything guarding money or a unique resource are built this way.

An AP design says: answer. Every node keeps serving reads and accepting writes with what it has. Clients on both sides stay in service, readers may see stale data, and writes accepted on the two sides may conflict and have to be reconciled when the partition heals, by the means of Chapter 8. Shopping carts, social feeds, DNS and caches are built this way: for them a stale answer is far better than none.

Value read(String key) {
	if (!canReachMajority()) {
		if (policy.onPartition == Choice.CONSISTENCY)
			throw new UnavailableException("no quorum");      // CP: never wrong, sometimes silent
		return local.get(key);                                  // AP: always answers, sometimes stale
	}
	return policy.otherwise == Choice.CONSISTENCY
			? readConfirmedByMajority(key)                        // a round trip to other replicas on every read
			: local.get(key);                                    // immediate, and possibly a little behind
}

The second return statement is a different decision from the first, and CAP has nothing to say about it. It is the subject of the next section.

PACELC

Partitions are rare. A well-run system spends nearly all its time with the network intact, and CAP says nothing about that time. Yet there is a trade-off in normal operation too, visible throughout Chapter 9: a write that waits for other replicas is slower than one that doesn't, and a read that checks with other replicas is slower than one answered from the local copy. In 2010 Daniel Abadi proposed an extended formulation, with an awkward name that spells out its structure:

if there is a Partition, choose Availability or Consistency;
Else, choose Latency or Consistency.

A system is then described by two choices, written as a pair. PA/EL gives up consistency in both situations, in favour of staying up and staying fast. PC/EC keeps consistency in both, and pays with refusals during a partition and round trips otherwise. The mixed cases exist as well. The "else" half matters more in practice, since it is paid for on every request of every day, where the partition half is paid on the bad days only. It is also the half that grows with distance: within one data centre a round trip to another replica is a millisecond and consistency is nearly free, and between continents it is a hundred milliseconds on each operation.

Things to notice. Start with a healthy network and C across an ocean. Preferring consistency, the read is right and takes 81 ms. Preferring latency, it takes 1 ms and returns the old value, since the write is still crossing the ocean. Nothing has failed, and there is already a choice between right and fast: that is the "else" half of PACELC. Move C into the same data centre and the choice almost disappears. The consistent read costs 2 ms, and the fast one happens to be right as well, since replication arrives within the five milliseconds. Now cut C off. The "otherwise" setting stops mattering for the read, and the partition setting decides: with consistency the read is refused, and with availability it returns the stale value at once. No combination of settings gives a correct answer from C during the partition, which is the theorem. Notice also that A and B keep working throughout. A CP system is still up during a partition for every client that can reach its majority.

Classifying real databases

Vendors and blog posts attach these labels to products, and the labels are less solid than they look. Nearly every modern database lets the two choices be set for each cluster, each table or each request. The label describes a configuration.

Three habits make the classification useful. Ask about the operation, since one system often treats reads and writes differently. Ask about the configuration actually deployed, since defaults are frequently the weakest setting. And test the claim where it matters: Chapter 37 describes the Jepsen project, which has found a long list of databases that lost data in configurations their documentation called consistent.

Use case: the cash machine that chooses availability

Twelve years after his talk, Eric Brewer wrote a retrospective article on CAP, and to show how the choice is made in practice he used an example most people would have guessed the other way: a bank's cash machine. Money looks like the textbook case for consistency. Yet when an ATM loses its connection to the bank it doesn't simply go dark. Many are designed to carry on in a stand-in mode, dispensing cash up to a modest limit and recording each withdrawal locally. When the connection returns, the stored transactions are sent to the bank and applied. If a customer has taken out more than the account held, the bank detects the overdraft at that point and deals with it in the ordinary way, with a fee or a letter. The reasoning behind that design is the lesson Brewer wanted to draw. The bank has considered what each choice costs. Refusing all withdrawals during an outage costs customers and reputation every time it happens. Allowing them costs an occasional overdraft, whose size the withdrawal limit bounds, and which the bank already has a process to recover. So it chooses availability, limits the damage an inconsistency can do, keeps a log sufficient to detect inconsistencies afterwards, and compensates for them. That pattern, operate through the partition with bounded risk, then detect and repair, is how a great many business systems treat partitions, and it shows that the choice between C and A is a business decision about the cost of being wrong against the cost of being closed. It is seldom all or nothing: the same bank would refuse a large transfer under the same conditions.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • "We chose CA." A replicated system will meet a partition, and will then be C or A whether or not anyone decided which.
  • Reading CAP's "availability" as uptime. It is a strict property of every non-failed node, not a number of nines.
  • Thinking AP means "no consistency". It means consistency is given up while a partition lasts, and restored by reconciliation afterwards.
  • Ignoring the "else". The latency cost of consistency is paid on every request, and across regions it dominates.
  • Classifying a product instead of a configuration, and trusting the default to be the safe one.
  • One policy for a whole application. Carts and payments can, and usually should, choose differently.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What does the CAP theorem say?

How a strong answer sounds
It says that when a network partition splits a replicated system, each side has to choose between consistency and availability for a given request: it can't have both. Consistency here means linearizability, every read sees the latest completed write as if there were one copy. Availability means every node that hasn't crashed returns a non-error response. The reasoning is simple: if a node can't communicate with the others, it can't know whether there have been writes it hasn't seen, so it either answers with possibly stale data, giving up consistency, or refuses, giving up availability. I'd add two clarifications. "Pick two of three" is misleading, since partitions aren't optional in a real network, so the actual choice is between C and A, and only during a partition. And it isn't all-or-nothing for a whole system: the choice can differ for each operation, and both properties come in degrees. When there is no partition the relevant trade-off is a different one, between consistency and latency, which PACELC captures.

Deeper: You're designing a multi-region system with a shopping cart, an inventory count and a payment step. How do CAP and PACELC shape each?

How a strong answer sounds
I'd make the choice for each, since the cost of being wrong differs. The cart is the classic AP, latency-first case: a customer must always be able to add an item, a stale or conflicting cart is cheap to fix, and writes should be local to the user's region. I'd accept writes in any region, replicate asynchronously, and merge conflicting versions, by union with tombstones for removals or with a CRDT. Payment is the opposite: charging twice or losing a charge is unacceptable, so it is CP. I'd route it to a single authority, a consensus-replicated store in one region or across three, require a majority, use idempotency keys so that retries are safe, and accept that during a partition some customers can't pay and that every payment costs a cross-region round trip, perhaps a hundred milliseconds, which nobody notices at checkout. Inventory sits between them and is the interesting one. Strict consistency prevents overselling but makes every "add to cart" depend on a remote majority. I'd take a middle path: show an approximate count read locally, latency over consistency, and do the authoritative decrement at checkout against the consistent store, which is when it matters. If availability during a partition matters more than a rare oversell, I'd let each region sell from an allocated share of the stock, which bounds the damage, and reconcile afterwards, compensating with a cancellation or a back-order. So: one application, three different answers, each justified by what a wrong answer costs against what a refusal or a slow answer costs.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • CAP: during a network partition, a node can answer and risk being wrong, or refuse. It can't promise both.
  • Consistency there means linearizability, and availability means every non-failed node answers every request.
  • Partition tolerance isn't optional, so the choice is C or A, during partitions only, and it can differ for each operation.
  • CP systems stop the minority side. AP systems keep both sides serving and reconcile afterwards.
  • PACELC adds the everyday trade-off: with no partition, consistency costs latency, in proportion to the distance between replicas.
  • CAP labels describe configurations, not products. Check the settings in use, and what they do for each kind of request.

Check your understanding

Why is "pick any two of C, A and P" a misleading summary of CAP?

A replicated system will see partitions. "CA" just means the choice hasn't been thought about.

During a partition, a five-node CP system is split three and two. What happens?

Only the majority can be sure of holding the latest data. The minority gives up availability to stay correct.

What does the "ELC" part of PACELC describe?

Else: Latency or Consistency. Waiting for other replicas costs time on every request, partition or not.

A store answers every read from the nearest replica without checking the others, and keeps accepting writes on both sides of a partition. How is it classified?

Availability during a partition, and latency otherwise: the Dynamo-style default.

In CAP, what does "availability" require?

The theorem's definition is strict. A CP system can have excellent uptime and still not be "available" in this sense.
