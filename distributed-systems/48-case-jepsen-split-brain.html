Case Study: A Jepsen Split-Brain Report

The course ends where a working engineer usually meets its subject: with a report saying that a system which passed every test has lost data. In May 2013 Kyle Kingsbury published the result of a simple experiment on Redis, run with its high-availability layer, Sentinel. Five machines. A few clients adding numbers to a set and noting which additions were acknowledged. A network cut across the middle for a while, then repaired. At the end he read the set back. Of roughly two thousand acknowledged writes, more than half were gone. No process had crashed, no disk had failed, no error had been returned to any client, and every component had done what its documentation said. The loss was a property of the design, and it had been waiting for the first network partition to show itself. This chapter goes through that report in the order an engineer should read any such report: what was tested, what was done to the network, what was observed, why it happened, and what was changed. Every piece of the explanation is something from an earlier chapter, and that is the point of ending here.

  • Read a fault-injection report: the workload, the fault, the evidence and the claim
  • Trace how a partition produces two primaries in a system with asynchronous replication and automatic failover
  • Explain exactly which acknowledged writes are lost, and why nothing reports it
  • Name the three design ingredients that combine into the loss, and what changes if each is removed
  • Judge a proposed fix: does it prevent the loss, bound it, or move it?

This chapter applies the course and teaches little that is new. It uses asynchronous replication (Chapter 9), leader election and split brain (Chapter 13), primary-backup and the view service (Chapter 14), CAP (Chapter 10), fencing (Chapter 32), and the Jepsen method (Chapter 37). Chapter 29's use case was another report of the same kind.

The test

The system under test was a common production arrangement of the time, and still is. One Redis server is the primary and takes all writes. The others are replicas, which receive each write from the primary a moment later: replication is asynchronous, and the primary replies to its client without waiting for them (Chapter 9). Beside each Redis runs a Sentinel process. The Sentinels watch the primary, and if a majority of them agree that it is unreachable, they promote one of the replicas and tell clients where the new primary is. This is the "view service" of Chapter 14, made fault-tolerant by running several.

The test, in the form Chapter 37 described:

  • Five nodes, n1 to n5, each with Redis and a Sentinel. n1 is the primary.
  • The workload: clients add distinct integers to one set, one after another, each client finding the current primary by asking a Sentinel. For each attempt a client records whether the write was acknowledged, failed, or timed out.
  • The fault: part-way through, cut the network into two groups, and some time later restore it.
  • The check: wait for the cluster to settle, read the whole set from the primary, and compare. Every acknowledged integer should be there.

The choice of workload is worth noticing. A set of unique integers needs no cleverness to check: no search for a valid ordering, only a subtraction of one list from another. An element that was acknowledged and is absent admits no innocent explanation.

The partition

The network was cut so that the primary and one replica were on one side, and three nodes on the other: [n1 n2] and [n3 n4 n5]. Clients existed on both sides. What followed is each component behaving reasonably on what it could see.

The Sentinels on the majority side did exactly what they are for. A quorum of them judged the primary unreachable, which it was, and promoted a replica so that service could continue. On the minority side, n1 was never told. From where it stood it had lost contact with three replicas, an ordinary event, and it went on serving its clients. Nothing in the design required a primary to stop when it could no longer see most of the cluster.

The anomaly

When the network was repaired, the Sentinels informed n1 of the new arrangement. A Redis server that becomes a replica synchronises by taking a full copy of its new primary's data, replacing its own. n1's dataset, with every write it had accepted during the partition, was overwritten.

The published figures for one run were, in round numbers, two thousand writes attempted, all but a couple acknowledged, and fewer than nine hundred present at the end: about 56% of acknowledged writes lost. The proportion in any run depends on how long the partition lasts and how many clients are on the losing side. The shape doesn't: every write acknowledged by the old primary after the cut is lost, and none of the clients concerned receives an error at any time.

Things to notice. As it opens, the design is the one in the report, the partition lasts 30 seconds, and two clients are on each side. The report reads 220 total, 210 acknowledged, 150 survivors: 60 acknowledged writes lost, 29%. In the picture they are the solid red stretch on clients 1 and 2, from the cut to the repair. Those two clients saw nothing unusual at any moment. Lengthen the partition to 60 seconds and 120 writes are lost, 36%. The loss grows with the partition, with no limit.

Now put all four clients on the majority side. Nothing is lost, in any design. The fault is as present as before, and this test can't see it, since no client can reach the old primary to give it a write. A test that only kills the primary, or has no client beside it, passes a system that fails the real thing.

Put the clients back and try the second design. The old primary stops ten seconds after losing its replicas, so 20 writes are lost whatever the partition's length, and clients 1 and 2 then get errors. The third design loses nothing: across a 30-second partition clients 1 and 2 get 60 timeouts and not one false "OK". From one design to the next the red shrinks and then gives way to grey and amber. It is the same partition each time, paid for in data, then in a little data and some availability, then in availability alone.

Root cause

It is tempting to call this a bug in failover and look for the faulty line. There isn't one. The loss comes from three design decisions, each defensible, that are safe singly and unsafe together.

Read as a statement about Chapter 10, the system stayed available on both sides of a partition, which means it could not also be consistent, and the inconsistency was then resolved by discarding one side. That is a legitimate place to stand for a cache. The difficulty was that many people were using it as a store of record without knowing where it stood, and the system's own behaviour gave them no hint: writes were acknowledged, promptly, throughout.

The fix

There are three kinds of response to a finding like this, and a reader of the follow-up should know which one is on offer.

  • Prevent it. Acknowledge a write only when a majority holds it, and let a new primary be chosen only from nodes that have everything acknowledged. That is consensus, and it changes what the system is: every write now waits for a round trip, and a minority side stops serving. Redis kept its original design for its main product, and a separate consensus-based module was developed years later for those who want this.
  • Bound it. This is what Redis added. A primary can be configured to refuse writes unless it has heard from at least a given number of replicas within a given time. An isolated primary then keeps accepting writes only until that time runs out, about ten seconds by default, and the loss is limited to that window. The anomaly is smaller and still there, and the minority side becomes unavailable where before it was wrong.
  • Say so. The cheapest fix and often the most valuable. The documentation now states that Redis with Sentinel doesn't guarantee that acknowledged writes survive a failover, and describes the design as eventually consistent with "last failover wins" as its merge. An engineer who reads that can make the right choice.
# redis.conf: bound the window. With fewer than one replica heard from in the last 10 seconds, refuse writes.
min-replicas-to-write 1
min-replicas-max-lag 10
// A client can also ask how far a write has spread, with the WAIT command. Here through the Lettuce client.
RedisCommands<String, String> redis = connection.sync();
redis.sadd("orders", orderId);
long copies = redis.waitForReplication(2, 200);       // block until 2 replicas have it, or 200 ms pass
if (copies < 2) flagForReview(orderId);                // not a rollback: the write is on the primary whatever this returns

// And the check at the heart of the test is two lines in any language.
Set<Integer> lost = new TreeSet<>(acknowledged);
lost.removeAll(finalRead);                           // should be empty

The WAIT command is a good example of a fix that looks stronger than it is. It tells a client that a write reached some replicas. It doesn't stop a failover from promoting a replica that lacks the write, and it doesn't undo the write if too few replicas answered. It reduces the chance of loss and changes no guarantee, a distinction the analysis that followed spent some time on.

The practical lesson is a question to ask of any replicated store before trusting it with data that matters: what happens to a write that was acknowledged by a node that is then replaced? If the honest answer is "it is discarded", the system is a cache or an eventually consistent store, whatever else it is called, and should be used as one.

🔁
Use case: the same test, seven years on, against the fix

The consensus-based design that the first report implied was eventually built: a module called Redis-Raft, which puts a Raft log underneath Redis so that a write is acknowledged only once a majority has it. In 2020, before its release, its developers asked for it to be given the same treatment as the system it was meant to improve on, and the resulting analysis is a fitting last example for this course. The tests were of the kind in Chapter 37, grown more elaborate in seven years: partitions, process crashes and pauses, clock changes, and membership changes while under load, with histories checked for linearizability and for transactional anomalies. Against development builds they found twenty-one problems. Some were plain crashes. Others were exactly the failures the design existed to prevent, among them stale reads, lost updates, and in early builds a split brain, with two nodes each believing itself leader, that produced divergent data. Most were in the places this course has pointed at repeatedly: the handling of snapshots, the changing of cluster membership, and what a node does in the moments after it restarts. Raft was correct. The implementation of Raft, with its interactions with an existing system, was not yet. Nearly all of the issues were fixed during the collaboration, before any user depended on it. Three things are worth taking from the comparison. A consensus algorithm is evidence of a sound design and no evidence about the code. Testing with injected faults, done before release and repeated, finds what review doesn't. And the most encouraging change between 2013 and 2020 is in who asked for the test: the first report was an outsider's surprise, and the second was commissioned by the authors, as an ordinary step in building something that claims to keep data safe.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Taking "acknowledged" to mean "durable". Find out which nodes had the write when the reply was sent.
  • Automatic failover with no restraint on the old primary. Promotion alone creates a second primary. Something must stop the first.
  • Reading "we tested failover" as covering partitions. Killing a process is the easy case. The old primary staying alive and reachable to some clients is the hard one.
  • Treating a bounded loss as no loss. A ten-second window of discarded writes is better than an unbounded one, and is still discarded writes.
  • Believing the test passed because no errors were returned. In this failure every client was told its write succeeded.
  • Using a cache's replication as a database's. The same product may be an excellent cache and an unsafe system of record.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is split brain, and how does it cause data loss in a primary-replica system?

How a strong answer sounds
Split brain is when two nodes both believe they are the primary at the same time and both accept writes. It typically happens with automatic failover during a network partition. Say the primary and some clients are on one side, and a majority of the monitoring processes and replicas are on the other. The majority side can't reach the primary, concludes it is dead, and promotes a replica. But the old primary isn't dead; it is only cut off, and it has no way of knowing it has been replaced, so it keeps accepting writes from the clients that can still reach it. Now there are two diverging copies of the data. When the partition heals, the system has to pick one. Usually the old primary is demoted to a replica of the new one and resynchronises by discarding its own state, so every write it acknowledged during the partition is lost, even though the clients were told those writes succeeded. With asynchronous replication, where the primary acknowledges before replicas have the data, nothing prevents this. Preventing it needs either acknowledging writes only when a majority has them, so an isolated primary can't acknowledge anything, or fencing, so the old primary is forced to stop.

Deeper: You run a primary with asynchronous replicas and automatic failover. A Jepsen-style test shows acknowledged writes lost under partition. What are your options?

How a strong answer sounds
First I'd decide what the data is. If it is a cache or otherwise reconstructible, the behaviour may be acceptable and the fix is to document it and make sure nobody treats it as a system of record. If acknowledged writes must survive, there are three levels of response. One, prevent it: require acknowledgement from a majority before replying to the client, and only allow a node that has all acknowledged writes to be promoted. That is consensus-based replication; many databases offer it as a setting, such as majority write concern, or it means moving to a system built on Raft. The cost is a network round trip on every write and that the minority side of a partition can't accept writes. Two, bound it: keep asynchronous replication but have the primary stop accepting writes when it hasn't heard from enough replicas recently, and make the failover wait at least that long before promoting, so the two primaries' windows don't overlap much. The loss window shrinks from the length of the partition to the configured lag, and you trade away availability on the isolated side. Three, fence the old primary: have the failover mechanism actively cut it off, by revoking its access to storage or the network, or by clients and proxies refusing a primary with an older epoch. That stops clients writing to it but doesn't protect writes it acknowledged just before being fenced and hadn't yet replicated, so it combines with semi-synchronous replication. I'd also consider whether the data can be modelled to merge instead, like a CRDT set, in which case both sides' writes survive. Whatever I choose, I'd rerun the same test afterwards with partitions that leave the old primary reachable by some clients, because process-kill tests don't exercise this, and I'd keep the test in CI.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • The test: clients add unique values and record acknowledgements, the network is partitioned and healed, and the final state is compared with what was acknowledged.
  • Under a partition, automatic failover on the majority side and an old primary still serving on the minority side give two primaries.
  • On healing, the old primary takes the new one's data. Everything it acknowledged during the partition is lost, with no error to any client.
  • The cause is three reasonable choices together: asynchronous acknowledgement, failover that doesn't bind the old primary, and reconciliation by overwrite.
  • A fix may prevent the loss (majority acknowledgement), bound it (stop writing when replicas go quiet), or state it honestly. Know which you were given.
  • Ask of any store: what happens to a write acknowledged by a node that is then replaced?

Check your understanding

In the test, why do clients add unique integers to a set?

A workload chosen so that the evidence is unambiguous. No ordering search or model is needed.

The network is cut with the primary on the minority side. Why does it keep accepting writes?

From its side it has lost some replicas, an ordinary event. The promotion happened where it couldn't hear.

Which writes are lost when the partition heals?

The old primary rejoins as a replica and takes a full copy of the new primary's data, replacing its own.

A primary is configured to refuse writes when it hasn't heard from a replica for ten seconds. What does that do to the anomaly?

The window is limited and not closed. It trades wrong answers on the minority side for unavailability there.

Which change would prevent the loss of acknowledged writes altogether?

An isolated primary can then acknowledge nothing, so there is nothing to lose. The price is a round trip on every write.
