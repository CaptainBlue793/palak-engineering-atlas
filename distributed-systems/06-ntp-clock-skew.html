Physical Clocks, NTP & Skew

P5 looked at the clocks inside one machine. This chapter puts two machines side by side and asks how far apart their clocks are. The answer is never zero, and you can't measure it exactly: every machine's oscillator runs at a slightly different rate, and the only way to compare two clocks is to send a message over a network whose delay you don't know. NTP manages this well enough to keep servers within a few milliseconds of each other, which is good enough for logs and certificates and far from good enough to order two writes that arrived a millisecond apart. Systems that order events by timestamp anyway lose data, quietly. This chapter covers where the error comes from, how NTP estimates it, what bounds you can rely on, and what goes wrong when code assumes more.

Clock drift

A computer keeps time by counting the oscillations of a quartz crystal. No two crystals oscillate at quite the same rate, and one crystal's rate changes with temperature and age. The difference between a clock's rate and the true rate is its drift, measured in parts per million. A clock that drifts by 1 ppm gains or loses one microsecond every second.

DriftError after a minuteAfter an hourAfter a dayTypical of
1 ppm0.06 ms3.6 ms86 msA good crystal at a steady temperature

Two machines drifting in opposite directions move apart at the sum of their rates. So even if two clocks were set perfectly at noon, by the next noon ordinary servers could disagree by seconds. Skew is that disagreement at a given moment, and without regular correction it grows without limit. Virtual machines add trouble of their own: a VM that is paused, migrated to another host or starved of CPU doesn't see the time pass, and resumes with a clock that is suddenly behind.

How NTP works

The Network Time Protocol corrects a clock by asking a better clock what time it is. Time sources form a hierarchy of strata. Stratum 0 is a reference: an atomic clock or a GPS receiver. A stratum 1 server is connected directly to one. A stratum 2 server synchronises to stratum 1 servers over the network, and so on down, each level a little less accurate than the one it asked.

Asking is the hard part. The reply takes time to arrive, and by then the answer is stale by an amount nobody measured. NTP's method is to record four timestamps for each exchange and solve for two unknowns.

The delay is the time the two messages spent on the network: the whole round trip as the client saw it, less the time the server held the request. The offset is the estimate of how far the server's clock is ahead of the client's. It comes from averaging the two apparent differences, t1 − t0 on the way out and t2 − t3 on the way back. Each of those contains the true offset plus or minus a network delay, and if the two delays are equal they cancel.

record NtpSample(long t0, long t1, long t2, long t3) {      // milliseconds; t0 and t3 from our clock, t1 and t2 from the server's
	double offsetMs() { return ((t1 - t0) + (t2 - t3)) / 2.0; }    // server's clock minus ours, if the path is symmetric
	double delayMs()  { return (t3 - t0) - (t2 - t1); }            // time on the wire, both directions together
}

// Take several samples and trust the one that spent least time on the network:
// a short round trip leaves the least room for the two directions to differ.
static NtpSample best(List<NtpSample> samples) {
	return Collections.min(samples, Comparator.comparingDouble(NtpSample::delayMs));
}

That "if" is the weakness. The calculation assumes the request and the reply took equally long, and nothing in the four timestamps can confirm it. If the request took 40 ms and the reply 10 ms, the estimate is wrong by 15 ms, half the difference, and the client has no way to tell. What it does know is a limit: the error can't exceed half the round-trip delay, since at worst one direction took all of it. A real NTP client polls several servers, discards the ones that disagree with the majority, prefers low-delay samples, and then slews its clock gently towards the result (P5), polling again every one to seventeen minutes.

Things to notice. With equal delays in both directions, NTP's estimate is exact whatever the size of the delay or of the clock's error: a 20 ms path and a 150 ms path give the same answer. Make the request slower than the reply and an error appears, equal to half the difference, while the delay figure looks perfectly healthy. Nothing the client can compute distinguishes that case from a symmetric one. The guaranteed bound, half the round trip, is the only promise available, and it gets worse on a long path even when the estimate happens to be good. Then the drift takes over: at 30 ppm the clock is 8 ms out again after 256 seconds, and 31 ms out after 1,024. A synchronised clock is right for an instant after each correction and wanders until the next.

Skew bounds

How far apart, then, are two servers' clocks in practice? It depends almost entirely on the network between each server and its time source.

SetupTypical error against true timeLimited by
NTP over the public internetSeveral ms to tens of msLong, asymmetric, variable paths
NTP to servers in the same data centreUnder a millisecond, with careOperating-system scheduling and switch queues
A cloud provider's time serviceUnder a millisecond, and on recent hardware tens of microsecondsThe provider's reference clocks and the hypervisor
PTP with hardware timestampsUnder a microsecondThe network cards and switches supporting it
A machine whose time daemon has stoppedUnbounded: seconds a dayNothing

Two cautions go with every figure in that table. They are typical values, and correctness arguments need worst cases. A server whose NTP daemon has crashed, or which is polling an upstream that is itself wrong, or which has just been resumed from a paused virtual machine, can be seconds out while reporting nothing unusual. And a machine can't observe its own error. The best it has is the daemon's estimate: chronyc tracking reports the current offset and a figure for the maximum error, built from the round-trip delay to the source and the time since the last update. A system that depends on a skew bound has to monitor that estimate and act when it is exceeded. Chapter 35 shows two databases that do exactly that.

Last-write-wins and skew

The commonest way for skew to cause damage is the simplest conflict rule there is. When two replicas hold different values for a key, keep the one with the later timestamp. It is called last-write-wins, and it is the default in Cassandra and an option in many other stores.

record Versioned(String value, long timestampMs) {}

final class LwwRegister {
	private Versioned current = new Versioned(null, Long.MIN_VALUE);

	synchronized void write(Versioned incoming) {
		if (incoming.timestampMs() > current.timestampMs()) current = incoming;   // otherwise dropped, with no error
	}
	synchronized Versioned read() { return current; }
}

"Last" here means "largest timestamp", and the timestamps come from different machines' clocks. Suppose node A's clock is 80 ms fast.

True timeWhat happensTimestamp attachedValue after merging
12:00:00.000A user sets their email to old@x.com, through node A12:00:00.080, from A's fast clockold@x.com
12:00:00.050The user corrects it to new@x.com, through node B12:00:00.050, from B's correct clockold@x.com: the newer write has the smaller timestamp and is dropped
12:00:00.060Both writes have been acknowledged as successfulold@x.com, on every replica, permanently

Nothing failed and nothing was logged. The second write was accepted, replicated and then discarded on every replica, since each of them correctly applied the rule. The same mechanism makes a delete lose to an earlier write, so that removed data comes back, and lets one node with a clock running minutes fast win every conflict until someone notices. The size of the window is the skew: with 80 ms of skew, any two writes to the same key within 80 ms of each other may be ordered wrongly.

The ways out all avoid comparing readings from different clocks. Let one node, a leader, assign every timestamp. Or stop using physical time for ordering and use the logical clocks of Chapter 7, or the version vectors of Chapter 8, which detect that two writes conflict where last-write-wins would silently choose. Or keep physical time and make the uncertainty explicit, as Chapter 35 does.

PTP

NTP's error has two sources: the two directions of the path differ, and the timestamps are taken by software, some unknown time after a packet really arrived. The Precision Time Protocol, IEEE 1588, attacks both. Timestamps are taken by the network card's hardware at the moment a packet crosses the wire, which removes the operating system's scheduling delays. And the switches along the path take part: each measures how long it held a timing packet and reports it, or acts as a clock itself for the next hop, so that queueing delay stops being an unknown.

Most services have no need of it: a millisecond is ample for logs and for expiring tokens. PTP earns its cost where the clock error is a term in the system's performance, as in a database that waits out its clock uncertainty on every commit, or where a regulator demands it, as for trading firms that must timestamp orders to within 100 microseconds of UTC.

🕰️
Use case: Facebook replaces its time daemon

In 2020 Facebook's engineers described rebuilding the service that keeps the company's servers on time. It had run the classic ntpd on every machine, and when they measured carefully the clocks were good to about ten milliseconds, with occasional servers far worse and no reliable way to say which. They replaced it with chrony, a newer NTP implementation that polls more cleverly, uses hardware timestamps where the network card offers them, and reports an estimate of its own error. They also built their own stratum 1 sources, appliances holding GPS receivers and atomic clocks, in each data centre, so that every server's time source was a few fast, symmetric hops away. By their account the typical error fell from around ten milliseconds to around a hundred microseconds. The protocol was the same NTP, and the gain came from removing the two things the panel above varies: long asymmetric paths and stale corrections. Two years later the company, by then Meta, wrote that it was moving its data centres from NTP to PTP to reach well under a microsecond, and gave the reason in terms this course will return to: a database that knows its clocks agree to within a microsecond can decide much sooner that a read is safe to serve.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Ordering events from different machines by wall-clock timestamp and treating the result as the order they happened in.
  • Last-write-wins on data that matters. Under skew it discards acknowledged writes and reports success.
  • Assuming NTP means "synchronised". It means within some milliseconds, usually, while the daemon is running.
  • Not monitoring clock offset. A stopped time daemon produces no error until the skew breaks something.
  • Timestamps issued by clients. A phone's clock can be wrong by hours, or set on purpose.
  • Reading a small NTP delay as a small error. Asymmetry is invisible to the client. Only half the delay is a bound.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Why can't we just use timestamps to order events across servers?

How a strong answer sounds
Because each server's timestamp comes from its own clock, and the clocks disagree. Every crystal drifts at its own rate, tens of microseconds each second, so the clocks need constant correction, and NTP corrects them by exchanging messages over a network whose delay it can only estimate. The result is that well-run servers agree to within a few milliseconds, and badly run ones may be off by seconds. If two events on different servers are closer together in time than that skew, their timestamps can be in the wrong order: the later event can have the smaller timestamp. So timestamps are fine for humans reading logs and for coarse things such as expiry, and they aren't a safe basis for deciding which of two writes happened last. For ordering you use logical clocks, which capture causality without real time, or you route decisions through one node, or you use physical clocks with an explicit error bound and wait out the uncertainty.

Deeper: How does NTP estimate the offset between two clocks, and where does its error come from?

How a strong answer sounds
The client records when it sends a request, t0; the server records when it receives it, t1, and when it replies, t2; the client records when the reply arrives, t3. The round-trip delay is (t3 − t0) − (t2 − t1), the total time less the server's processing. The offset estimate is ((t1 − t0) + (t2 − t3)) / 2. Each of the two terms is the true offset plus or minus a one-way delay, so averaging cancels the delays if the outbound and return paths take the same time. That symmetry assumption is the main source of error: if the paths differ, the estimate is off by half the difference, and the client can't detect it from the timestamps. The error is bounded by half the round-trip delay. Other sources are software timestamping, where the packet waits in the kernel or the NIC before being stamped, and drift between polls, since the correction is right at one instant and the crystal then wanders at tens of ppm. Clients reduce these by polling several servers, discarding outliers, preferring the samples with the smallest delay, and using hardware timestamps; PTP goes further by having the network equipment itself account for its delay.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Every clock drifts, by tens of parts per million. Uncorrected, two servers move apart by seconds a day.
  • NTP estimates the offset from four timestamps, assuming the two directions of the path take equally long.
  • Asymmetry puts in an error of half the difference, which the client can't see. Half the round-trip delay is the only guaranteed bound.
  • Typical skew is milliseconds on a good network. The worst case, with a stopped daemon or a paused VM, has no limit.
  • Last-write-wins across machines silently discards the newer of two writes that fall within the skew.
  • PTP reaches under a microsecond with hardware timestamps and help from the switches, where the cost is justified.
