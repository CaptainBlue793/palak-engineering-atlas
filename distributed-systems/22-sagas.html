Sagas & Compensation

Placing an order in a system built from services touches four of them: the order service records it, inventory reserves the item, payments charges the card, and shipping books a courier. All four should happen or none should. Chapter 21's answer, two-phase commit, would need each service to hold locks while waiting on the others, and the payment provider is another company that offers no such thing. The answer used in practice gives up on doing the four things at one instant. Each service commits its own step in its own database, straight away. If a later step can't be done, the earlier ones are compensated: the stock is released and the card refunded. This is a saga. It is simple to describe and easy to get subtly wrong, since a refund is not the same as never having charged, and for a while the rest of the world can see a half-finished order. This chapter covers how to build one that ends in a sensible state whatever fails.

Why not 2PC across services

Two-phase commit works well inside one system, where the participants are shards of one database run by one team. Between services it fails on four counts.

  • Locks would be held across team boundaries. A prepared participant keeps its locks until the coordinator speaks. Inventory's rows would be frozen whenever payments was slow, and an outage in any one service would become a stall in all of them.
  • Availability multiplies. Every participant must be up for any transaction to commit. Four services at 99.9% give about 99.6% (P4).
  • The participants can't take part. Two-phase commit needs each one to expose prepare and commit. HTTP and gRPC APIs don't. A card network, a courier's API, an email provider and most message brokers have no "prepare".
  • The work can be long. A payment may wait for the customer's bank to approve it. A booking may wait for a person. No one holds database locks for minutes.

Each service also owns its own database, which is the point of having separate services. So the only atomic thing available is a local transaction in one of them.

The saga

A saga is a sequence of local transactions, T1, T2 … Tn, each in one service, each committed as soon as it is done. Each has a compensating transaction, C1 … Cn, that reverses its effect. The saga promises one of two outcomes: every step runs, or some run and then their compensations run, in reverse order. The idea and the name come from a 1987 paper by Hector Garcia-Molina and Kenneth Salem, written about long-running transactions inside a single database. It became common practice when systems were split into services.

Two-phase commitSaga
All or nothingAt one instantIn the end: all, or done and then compensated
IsolationOthers never see a partial resultNone. Each step is visible as soon as it commits
Locks across servicesHeld through both phasesNever
If one service is downNothing commits. Prepared participants may blockThe saga waits or compensates. Other work carries on
What each service providesPrepare and commitAn action and a compensation, both safe to repeat
How long it may runMillisecondsSeconds to days

Compensating actions

A compensation is not a rollback. A rollback makes a change vanish as though it never happened. A compensation is a second, visible change that offsets the first. After a refund the customer's statement shows two lines, the confirmation email was still sent, and a shopper who saw "sold out" in the meantime has gone elsewhere. So compensations are designed step by step, as business decisions: a cancelled booking may carry a fee, and a refund may be a credit note.

Some steps can't be compensated at all. A parcel handed to a courier, a message sent to a customer, a payout to a bank account: none can be taken back. That gives each step one of three roles, and the roles decide the order.

Kind of stepMeaningExamples
CompensatableCan be reversed afterwards. These go firstCreate a PENDING order, reserve stock, authorise a card
PivotThe point of no return. If it succeeds the saga will finish; if it fails the saga compensatesCapture the payment, hand over to the courier
RetriableCan't fail for a business reason, only for a technical one, so it is retried until it works. These go lastMark the order CONFIRMED, send the email, award loyalty points

So: put everything that might be refused before anything that can't be reversed. Check stock and authorise the card before telling the warehouse to pack.

Three rules make compensations dependable.

  • A compensation must succeed in the end. There is no compensation for a compensation. If the refund call fails it is retried, for hours if need be, and if it can never succeed a person is alerted. A saga can pause at this point. It can't give up.
  • Every action and compensation is idempotent. Each will be sent more than once, after a timeout or a crash (Chapter 4). The saga's ID and step number make a natural idempotency key.
  • A compensation may arrive before its action. The orchestrator sends "reserve", times out, and sends "release". The delayed "reserve" then arrives. The service must record that this key was cancelled, and refuse the late request, or the stock stays reserved for ever.

What a saga gives up

A saga keeps atomicity in its weakened form, and each step is consistent and durable. It has no isolation. Between the first step and the last, other requests see and act on a state that may be about to be reversed. The anomalies are those of the Databases course, appearing now between services where no isolation level can be set: a second saga overwrites the first one's change (a lost update), or reads a reservation that is later released (a dirty read). The usual countermeasures:

  • A semantic lock. The first step marks the record: the order is PENDING, the seat is HELD. Other code checks the marker and waits, refuses, or warns. It is a lock in the application's data, with a meaning the business chose.
  • Commutative updates. "Reduce the balance by 40" and "increase it by 40" give the right result in any order with other updates. "Set the balance to 60" doesn't.
  • Re-read before acting. A step checks that what it read earlier is unchanged, and aborts the saga if not, as an optimistic transaction would.
  • Order by risk. Put the step most likely to be refused first, so that most failed sagas have nothing to compensate.

The last two faults are about not knowing. When the reply from Payments is lost, the charge has happened and the orchestrator can't tell. When the orchestrator crashes, its log says the step was started and not that it finished. In both cases the only safe move is to send the step again, and whether that is harmless depends on the key. With keys, Payments recognises the request and returns the first result: £40. Without them it charges twice and the saga completes "successfully" with £80 taken. The chain of calls, having no log, does nothing at all after the crash: the card is charged and no code remembers the order.

Orchestration vs choreography

There are two ways to decide who runs the next step. With orchestration, one component holds the saga's definition, calls each service in turn, and records progress in a durable log, as in the figure and the panel. With choreography, there is no such component. Each service publishes an event when it finishes its step, and the next service reacts: inventory hears OrderPlaced and reserves, payments hears StockReserved and charges, and on failure a service publishes PaymentDeclined, which inventory hears and releases.

OrchestrationChoreography
Where the flow is written downIn one placeNowhere. It is implied by who subscribes to what
CouplingThe orchestrator knows every service. The services know nothing of each otherEach service knows the events of its neighbours
"Where is order 8812 stuck?"Read the orchestrator's logTrace events across every service (Chapter 36)
Adding a stepChange the orchestratorAdd a subscriber, and check every compensation path still works
RiskThe orchestrator grows into a place where all business logic collectsCycles of events, and flows that nobody fully understands
SuitsFlows of more than three or four steps, or with branchingShort flows, and reactions that are optional (send an email, update search)

An orchestrator is small. Its one firm requirement is that it logs each step before sending it, so that after a crash it can resume and not start again.

record Step<C>(String name, BiConsumer<C, String> action, BiConsumer<C, String> compensation) {}

void run(String sagaId, C ctx) {
	int i = log.firstUnfinishedStep(sagaId);                  // 0 for a new saga; where it stopped, after a crash
	try {
		for (; i < steps.size(); i++) {
			log.append(sagaId, i, STARTED);                     // before the call, so a crash can't hide it
			retryOnTimeout(steps.get(i).action(), ctx, sagaId + ":" + i);   // the key makes the retry safe
			log.append(sagaId, i, DONE);
		}
		log.append(sagaId, COMPLETED);
	} catch (BusinessRefusal e) {                             // "card declined", not "connection reset"
		log.append(sagaId, i, REFUSED);
		for (int j = i - 1; j >= 0; j--) {
			retryUntilItWorks(steps.get(j).compensation(), ctx, sagaId + ":undo:" + j);
			log.append(sagaId, j, COMPENSATED);
		}
		log.append(sagaId, CANCELLED);
	}
}

The distinction in the catch matters. A refusal is an answer: the step didn't happen and won't. A timeout is not an answer, and treating it as a refusal is how a card gets charged for a cancelled order. A real orchestrator also resumes a saga that crashed while compensating, which this sketch leaves out.

The outbox pattern

Every saga step has the same small problem inside it. The service must do two things: change its own database, and tell someone, by publishing an event or replying to the orchestrator. Those are two systems, with no transaction between them. This is the dual-write problem.

  • Commit, then publish: a crash between the two leaves the order saved and no event sent. The saga stops for ever, silently.
  • Publish, then commit: a crash, or a failed commit, leaves an event announcing an order that doesn't exist.

The transactional outbox removes the second system from the critical moment. The service writes the event as a row in an outbox table, in the same local transaction as the business change. One commit makes both durable or neither. A separate relay then reads the table and publishes.

// 1. One local transaction: the business row and the message stand or fall together.
@Transactional
void placeOrder(Order order) {
	orders.insert(order);
	outbox.insert(new OutboxRow(UUID.randomUUID(), "orders", order.id(), "OrderPlaced", toJson(order)));
}

// 2. The relay, on a timer. A crash after send and before markSent means the row is sent again.
void relay() {
	for (OutboxRow row : outbox.oldestUnsent(100)) {        // ... WHERE sent_at IS NULL ORDER BY id LIMIT 100
		broker.send(row.topic(), row.key(), row.payload(), row.id()).get();   // wait for the broker's acknowledgement
		outbox.markSent(row.id());
	}
}

The relay can fail between sending and marking, so a message may be published twice. It is never lost. That is at-least-once delivery, and it is why each row carries an ID for consumers to deduplicate on. Chapter 28 takes delivery guarantees further. A common alternative to polling is change data capture: a tool such as Debezium reads the database's write-ahead log (Databases, WAL), and publishes each new outbox row as it commits, in commit order and with no polling delay.

The receiving side has the mirror image, an inbox: record the message's ID in a table in the same transaction as the work it triggers, and skip any ID already there.

Use case: Cadence and Temporal, the orchestrator as a product

Writing an orchestrator by hand means writing the same things each time: a durable log, timers, retries with backoff, recovery after a crash, and a way to see which step each of a million sagas has reached. Engineers at Uber built this once as a general service, Cadence, and released it as open source in 2017. Its lead authors had built workflow services before, Amazon's Simple Workflow Service among them, and in 2019 they started Temporal, a fork that has since become the more widely used of the two. The model is called durable execution. The developer writes the saga as ordinary code, a Java method that calls inventory.reserve(), then payments.charge(), with a try and catch for compensation. The calls are to "activities", which run in the developer's own workers, and the engine records the result of each one in an event history kept in its database. If the worker running the method dies, another worker starts the same method from the top and replays it: each activity call returns its recorded result at once without running again, until the code reaches the point where the history ends, and from there it continues for real. The method may sleep for thirty days in one line, since the sleep is a durable timer in the engine and not a thread. The price is the rule of Chapter 15: replay only works if the workflow code is deterministic. It mustn't read the clock, generate a random number, or branch on anything not in the history, and changing the code of a workflow while instances are part-way through needs explicit versioning, for the same reason. Activities are run at least once, so the engine doesn't remove the need for idempotency keys in the services it calls. It removes the log, the timers and the recovery.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Treating a timeout as a refusal. The step may have happened. Retry with the same key, and compensate only on a definite answer.
  • Compensations that can fail and aren't retried. The saga ends half undone, with nobody told.
  • Putting an irreversible step early. The email is sent, then the card is declined.
  • No marker on in-progress records. Other requests treat a PENDING order or a held seat as final.
  • Committing to the database and then publishing, with no outbox. It works until the first crash between the two.
  • Not handling a compensation that arrives before its action. The late action then takes effect after the saga is over.
  • Using a saga where one database would do. If the steps could share a database, a local transaction is simpler and stronger.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is a saga, and how does it differ from a distributed transaction?

How a strong answer sounds
A saga is a way to keep data consistent across several services without a distributed transaction. The operation is split into a sequence of local transactions, one per service, and each commits on its own straight away. Each step has a compensating action that reverses its business effect. If a step fails, the saga runs the compensations for the steps already done, in reverse order, so the end result is either everything done or everything undone. The differences from a distributed transaction such as two-phase commit: there are no locks held across services and no blocking, so services stay independent and available. But there is no isolation, so other requests can see intermediate states, like stock reserved for an order that later fails. And compensation isn't a true rollback; it is a new action, a refund and not an un-charge, and some actions such as sending an email can't be undone at all. So you design for it: order the steps so the irreversible ones come last, make every action and compensation idempotent because they will be retried, and mark in-progress records as pending.

Deeper: A service must update its database and publish an event. How do you make that reliable?

How a strong answer sounds
That is the dual-write problem: two systems, no shared transaction, and a crash between the two writes leaves them disagreeing. If I commit and then publish, a crash loses the event. If I publish and then commit, a failed commit leaves a false event. A distributed transaction between the database and the broker is usually unavailable or undesirable. The standard fix is the transactional outbox. In the same local transaction as the business change, I insert a row into an outbox table holding the event. One commit covers both. A separate relay process reads unsent rows in order, publishes them to the broker, waits for the acknowledgement, and marks them sent. If the relay crashes after publishing and before marking, it publishes that row again, so delivery is at-least-once, never zero. Each event therefore carries a unique ID, and consumers deduplicate, typically with an inbox table of processed IDs written in the same transaction as their own work. Instead of polling, the relay can be change data capture: a tool like Debezium tails the database's log and emits outbox inserts in commit order, with lower latency and no polling load. Things to watch: ordering, which is per aggregate if I key messages by the entity ID and the relay preserves order; cleaning up sent rows so the table doesn't grow; and monitoring the oldest unsent row, since a stuck relay means every downstream saga silently stalls. The alternative design is event sourcing, where the event log is the source of truth and there is only one write, but that is a much larger commitment.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Between independent services there is no shared transaction. The building block is a local transaction in one service.
  • A saga is a sequence of local transactions, each with a compensation, ending in all done or all compensated.
  • Compensation is a new, visible action. Order the steps: compensatable first, then the pivot, then retriable ones.
  • Every action and compensation must be idempotent, and compensations must be retried until they succeed.
  • A saga has no isolation. Use a PENDING marker, commutative updates and re-reads to contain it.
  • Orchestration keeps the flow in one place with a durable log. Choreography spreads it across events.
  • The outbox pattern makes "update the database and publish" atomic, at the price of duplicates.

Check your understanding

Why is two-phase commit a poor fit across microservices?

One slow or failed service stalls the rest, and most service APIs offer no prepare step.

A saga has reserved stock and charged the card, and then booking the courier is refused. What happens?

Both steps have committed, so they can only be offset by new transactions, last one first.

The orchestrator's call to "charge card" times out. What should it do?

A timeout doesn't say whether the charge happened. The key lets the service answer for the first attempt.

Which property of a database transaction does a saga not provide?

Each step commits and is visible at once, which is why in-progress records are marked PENDING.

What does the transactional outbox guarantee?

The row and the change share one commit. The relay may send a row twice after a crash, so consumers deduplicate by ID.
