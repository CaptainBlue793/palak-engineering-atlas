Impossibility Results: FLP & Friends

Most of this course has been about what can be built. This chapter is about what can't, and it belongs near the end, since only now is it clear what the limits are limits on. Distributed computing has a small number of theorems of an unusual kind. They don't say that a problem is hard, or that nobody has found a solution yet. They prove that no solution exists, that every protocol anyone will ever write for the problem, however clever, must fail in some execution. Two generals can't agree on a time to attack if their messengers may be captured. No algorithm can guarantee consensus if one machine may crash and messages may be arbitrarily slow. These results sound like the end of the subject, and the subject plainly continued: Raft elects leaders, databases commit transactions and connections open and close billions of times a day. The reconciliation is the useful part. Each impossibility is exact about its assumptions, and each working system gets round it by giving up one of them, on purpose and knowingly. An engineer who knows the theorems can say which one a design has given up. One who doesn't will sooner or later be asked to build the impossible, and try.

Two generals

Two armies are camped on opposite sides of a city. If both attack at the same time they win. If one attacks alone it is destroyed. The generals communicate by messengers who must cross the city, and any messenger may be captured. General A decides to attack at dawn and sends a messenger to say so. Can the two arrange to attack together, with certainty?

A's order may not arrive, so A can't attack on the strength of having sent it. So B sends an acknowledgement. That may not arrive either, so B can't attack on the strength of having sent it: A, hearing nothing, might hold back. So A acknowledges the acknowledgement, and now A is the one who can't know. The pattern never ends, and the proof says why.

Suppose some protocol does solve it, and take the one that uses the fewest messages. Consider its last message. The protocol must still work if that message is lost, since any message may be. But then the last message was unnecessary, and a protocol without it would also work, with one message fewer, contradicting the choice of the shortest. So no such protocol exists. Over a channel that can lose messages, two parties can't reach certainty that they will both act.

The result dates from the 1970s and was given its name by Jim Gray in 1978. It is the reason for a fact used since Chapter 4: a sender whose request gets no reply can't know whether the request arrived. It is why exactly-once delivery was called impossible in Chapter 28, and why the last message of any exchange is always a matter of hope.

Try it: find a rule that works

The generals plan an exchange of some number of messages, alternating, with A sending first. The exchange stops at the first message that is lost, since nobody then replies. Each general needs a rule of the only kind available to it: "I attack once I have received at least this many messages." B may not attack having heard nothing, as it wouldn't know there was a plan. The last statistic gives the whole result for each size: of the 42 pairs of rules for ten messages, one never disagrees, and it is the pair under which nobody ever attacks.

FLP

The two generals have an unreliable channel. Give them a perfect one, on which every message arrives, and take away only the knowledge of when. That is the asynchronous model of Chapter 2: no bound on how long a message takes or how slowly a process runs. In 1985 Michael Fischer, Nancy Lynch and Michael Paterson proved the result known by their initials.

In an asynchronous system, no deterministic algorithm can guarantee that a group of processes reaches consensus, if even one of them may crash.

Consensus here has its usual three requirements: all who decide choose the same value (agreement), the value was proposed by someone (validity), and every process that doesn't crash eventually decides (termination). The theorem says the three can't all be guaranteed. Every algorithm that keeps agreement and validity has some execution in which it never terminates.

The idea of the proof fits in a paragraph. Call a state of the whole system bivalent if the outcome is still open, so that either value might yet be decided, and univalent once it is settled. Some initial state must be bivalent. At some point a single message delivery tips the system from open to settled, and at that moment consider the process about to receive it. If that process is merely slow, the others must wait for it. If it has crashed, they must go on without it. They can't tell which, since in an asynchronous system a crashed process and a slow one look identical for any finite time. A scheduler that knows this can always find an order of deliveries that postpones the deciding message a little longer while keeping the outcome open, and can do so again at the next step, and for ever.

It matters as much what the theorem doesn't say. It doesn't say consensus is impossible in practice, or usually fails, or that an algorithm can be forced into a wrong answer. It says that for any deterministic algorithm there is an infinite, perfectly timed sequence of delays that keeps it undecided. Real networks are not adversaries with unlimited patience. The theorem marks precisely what must be given up, and leaves the choice of which part.

Why Raft still works

Every working consensus system escapes FLP by dropping one of its assumptions. There are three doors.

Assumption droppedWhat is assumed insteadWhat you getUsed by
Full asynchronyPartial synchrony: the network may misbehave for a while, and eventually messages arrive within some boundSafety always. Termination once the network settlesPaxos, Raft, PBFT, and nearly everything in production
DeterminismProcesses may toss coinsTermination with probability 1: an execution that never finishes is possible and has probability zeroRandomised consensus. Raft's random election timeouts borrow the idea
No knowledge of failuresA failure detector that may be wrong for a while and is eventually right about some correct processConsensus, with safety intact while the detector errsThe theory behind every "leader lease" and timeout

Raft and Paxos go through the first door, and the design shows it everywhere. Their safety, one leader for each term and no committed entry lost, rests on quorums and terms and on nothing about time. A message may take an hour and a node may pause for a day, and no two nodes will ever commit different entries at the same index. Their liveness, a leader being elected and requests being answered, depends on timeouts, and is promised only while the network is behaving: while messages take much less time than the election timeout, which in turn is much shorter than the time between failures. FLP said that termination can't be guaranteed under asynchrony. These protocols agree, and don't.

So when a Raft cluster under a bad network spends a minute holding election after election with no winner, it isn't malfunctioning. It is in the execution the theorem says must exist. Two pieces of engineering keep such executions short. Election timeouts are randomised, so that two candidates who collided once are unlikely to collide again, and the chance of a run of collisions falls off geometrically.

Duration nextElectionTimeout() {                             // uniform in [T, 2T): the second door, used to hold open the first
	return BASE.plusMillis(ThreadLocalRandom.current().nextLong(BASE.toMillis()));
}

And leaders use heartbeats as a failure detector that is allowed to be wrong: a false suspicion costs an unnecessary election and never a wrong result. The division of labour, safety from logic and liveness from timing, is the most transferable idea in this course. When reviewing any protocol, ask of each guarantee which kind it is, and for the liveness ones, what it assumes about time.

Other lower bounds

FLP and the two generals are the best known of a family. Each of the following has appeared in this course as a design constraint, usually without its name.

There is a positive result to set beside them. The CALM theorem identifies the programs that need no coordination at all: those that are monotonic, whose conclusions, once reached, are never withdrawn as more information arrives. A set that only grows is monotonic. "Is the count below ten?" is not, since a later arrival can change the answer. Monotonic programs can run on every replica independently and converge, which is the formal reason the CRDTs of Chapter 25 work, and the test for whether a new feature will need consensus.

The practical use of this table is in design review. A requirement that reads "always available, strongly consistent, across regions" or "exactly once, with no coordination" or "never blocks, never disagrees" is asking for something on the left-hand side. The productive response is to say which result it meets, and ask which of the alternatives on the right the business would accept.

Use case: TCP, which loses the generals' argument on purpose

Every TCP connection is two parties trying to agree, over a network that drops packets, that a connection exists and later that it has ended. That is the two generals problem, and TCP's designers knew the theorem. The protocol doesn't solve it. It arranges matters so that being wrong is brief and harmless. Opening a connection takes three messages: SYN, SYN-ACK, ACK. After the third, the client considers the connection open, and it can't know that its ACK arrived. If the ACK was lost, the two sides disagree: the client believes the connection is open and the server is still waiting. Nothing is added to resolve this, since a fourth message would have the same problem. The disagreement is made safe by two things. The server, hearing nothing, sends its SYN-ACK again, and the client answers again, as often as needed. And the first data the client sends carries the same acknowledgement, so the server learns the connection is open from whichever arrives first. Every message is safe to repeat and each side retries until it hears back. Closing has the same shape and a visible scar. The side that sends the final ACK of a close can't know it arrived. So it doesn't simply forget the connection. It waits in a state called TIME-WAIT, for twice the longest time a packet can survive in the network, in case the other side, not having heard, sends its FIN again and needs answering. That wait is a familiar nuisance to anyone who has restarted a server and been told its port is still in use. It is there because of a theorem: the last message can't be confirmed, so the protocol keeps listening for long enough that it probably didn't need to be. Certain agreement was impossible, and the designers settled for agreement that is very likely, with a cheap and automatic recovery in the remaining cases. Most engineering under an impossibility result looks like this.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • "FLP says consensus is impossible." It says termination can't be guaranteed under full asynchrony. Safety can, and progress is normal.
  • Adding another acknowledgement to be sure. The new last message has the old problem. Make the action safe to repeat.
  • Letting safety depend on a timeout. Timeouts are guesses. Use them to decide when to try again, never to decide what is true.
  • Treating an election storm as a bug in Raft. It is the lost liveness the theory predicts. Look at the network and the timeout settings.
  • Quoting a theorem without its model. Each result holds under stated assumptions. Check that they match your system before using it to say no, or yes.
  • Promising the impossible in a requirements document. Name the trade-off early, while it is still cheap.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is the two generals problem, and why does it matter for real systems?

How a strong answer sounds
Two generals must attack at the same time to win, and can only communicate by messengers who might be captured. General A sends "attack at dawn", but can't be sure it arrived, so needs an acknowledgement. B sends one, but can't be sure that arrived, so B doesn't know whether A will attack. A can acknowledge the acknowledgement, and now A is unsure again. No finite number of messages gives both generals certainty, and you can prove it: in any protocol, consider the last message; the protocol must work even if it is lost, so it was unnecessary, and you can keep removing messages until none are left. The conclusion is that two parties cannot reach guaranteed agreement over an unreliable channel. For real systems it means a client that sends a request and gets no reply cannot know whether the request was processed. That is why we design with retries plus idempotency, why exactly-once delivery is impossible and we settle for at-least-once with deduplication, and why TCP's handshake and close accept a small window of disagreement and recover with retransmission rather than trying to eliminate it.

Deeper: FLP says consensus is impossible in an asynchronous system. How do Paxos and Raft exist?

How a strong answer sounds
FLP is more specific than that. It says that in a fully asynchronous system, with no bound on message delay or processing speed, no deterministic algorithm can guarantee all three of agreement, validity and termination if even one process can crash. The core reason is that a crashed process is indistinguishable from a slow one, so an adversarial scheduler can always delay the critical message and keep the system undecided forever. The result attacks termination, not safety. Paxos and Raft accept this: they guarantee safety unconditionally, meaning no two nodes ever decide differently regardless of delays, crashes or partitions, and they guarantee liveness only under partial synchrony, meaning that if the network eventually behaves, with message delays well below the election timeout, a leader is elected and progress is made. During bad periods they may fail to make progress, with repeated elections for example, but they never return a wrong answer. In practice they also borrow from the other ways round FLP: randomised election timeouts make repeated split votes exponentially unlikely, which is the randomisation escape, and heartbeat timeouts act as an unreliable failure detector whose mistakes cost only availability. So the design principle is to make safety independent of timing and let timing assumptions affect only liveness. When I evaluate a protocol I ask of every property whether it is safety or liveness and what timing assumption, if any, it depends on.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • An impossibility result proves that no protocol can do something under stated assumptions. Working systems drop an assumption.
  • Two generals: over a lossy channel, no exchange of messages gives both sides certainty. The last message is always in doubt.
  • The practical answer is to retry until acknowledged and make the action safe to repeat.
  • FLP: under full asynchrony with one possible crash, no deterministic consensus algorithm can guarantee termination.
  • Raft and Paxos keep safety always and make liveness depend on the network settling. Randomised timeouts shorten the bad periods.
  • Safety should come from logic, quorums and terms. Timing should affect only when things happen, never what is true.
  • CAP, the 2f + 1 and 3f + 1 bounds, blocking commit and clock limits are the same kind of result, each forcing a choice.

Check your understanding

In the two generals problem, why doesn't one more acknowledgement solve it?

Whatever the last message is, the protocol must cope with losing it, and so can't rely on it.

What exactly does the FLP result say can't be guaranteed?

It is a result about termination. Agreement and validity can be kept in every execution.

How does Raft relate to FLP?

Raft assumes partial synchrony for liveness. In a long enough bad spell it elects nobody, and it never elects two.

Why are Raft's election timeouts randomised?

Randomness makes an endless run of collisions vanishingly unlikely, which is one of the standard ways round FLP.

A design document requires a store that is linearizable, and that every node keeps accepting writes during any network partition. What is the right response?

The requirement asks for what a theorem excludes. The useful work is finding which side the business would give up.
