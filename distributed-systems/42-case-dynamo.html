Case Study: Amazon Dynamo & DynamoDB

Two systems share most of a name, and they are different enough that confusing them is a standard interview error. Dynamo is an internal key–value store that Amazon built after its databases failed it during the holiday season of 2004, and described in 2007 in one of the most cited papers in the field. It is the leaderless, always-writable design that Chapter 26 took apart. DynamoDB is the public database service Amazon launched in 2012, which today answers tens of millions of requests a second. It kept the name, the key–value model and the commitment to predictable latency, and it changed nearly everything else: its partitions have leaders, it uses consensus, it offers strongly consistent reads and transactions, and nobody who uses it ever sees a vector clock. The distance between the two is this chapter's subject. It is a record of what a celebrated design was like to live with, and of which of its ideas a company kept when it had to sell the result to people who wouldn't read the paper.

The shopping cart

In 2004 Amazon's site ran on a large commercial relational database, and during the peak shopping weeks it suffered outages that the company traced to that database's limits. The engineers who studied the failures found that most of what the site stored was fetched by primary key alone, with no joins and no multi-row transactions: a session, a list of a customer's preferences, a shopping cart. They were paying for relational machinery they didn't use, and it was the machinery that failed to scale.

The shopping cart set the requirement. A customer who clicks "add to cart" and gets an error may not try again, so that operation must succeed, in the paper's words, even if disks are failing, network routes are flapping, or datacentres are being destroyed by tornadoes. Against that, an occasional oddity in the cart's contents costs little. This is a business decision about which error is cheaper, and the whole design follows from it: always accept the write, and sort out any disagreement afterwards.

Two details from the paper show how concretely the team thought. Service targets were set at the 99.9th percentile and not the average, for example a response within 300 ms for 99.9% of requests, on the reasoning that the customers with the longest histories, and so the slowest requests, are the most valuable ones. And the cart was reconciled by merging: if two versions of a cart existed, the application took the union of their items. An "add" was therefore never lost. A "remove" could be, since an item deleted in one version was still present in the other and came back in the union. The paper states it plainly: deleted items can resurface. That is the anomaly the design accepted.

The original design

Chapter 26 covered the parts: a consistent-hash ring with virtual nodes, each key on N nodes, any node coordinating, quorums of R and W, sloppy quorums and hints, version vectors with concurrent versions returned to the reader, hash-tree repair and gossip. A few particulars of the real system are worth adding.

  • The usual setting was N = 3, R = 2, W = 2. Each service ran its own Dynamo cluster and chose its own numbers.
  • Conflicts were rare. Over one measured day of the shopping-cart service, 99.94% of requests saw exactly one version, and fewer than one in a hundred thousand saw more than two. The paper attributes most divergence to concurrent writers, often automated ones, and not to failures.
  • Durability could be traded for speed. A node could acknowledge a write held in a memory buffer and flush it later, cutting the slowest latencies several-fold at the risk of losing writes in a crash. One of the N replicas was made to write durably to limit that risk.
  • It was not a service. A team that wanted Dynamo ran Dynamo: capacity, node replacement, repair, and the choice of N, R and W were theirs.

The last point decided what happened next. Inside Amazon, engineers reportedly admired Dynamo and chose something else. A simpler managed store, SimpleDB, had less capacity and worse latency and was more widely adopted, since nobody had to operate it. Dynamo's users also had to write and test merge logic for every data type they stored, and most business data has no merge as natural as a cart's. The model that was easiest for the system was hard on its customers.

DynamoDB's changes

DynamoDB, launched in 2012, was designed from those two observations: it must be a service that the customer never operates, and its behaviour must be simple to reason about. The second led its designers away from the paper that gave it its name.

So within a region DynamoDB is the consensus-replicated design of Chapter 18, applied to each partition separately. It chose consistency where Dynamo chose availability, and recovers the availability by spreading three replicas over three zones and electing a new leader quickly. The eventual-consistency machinery, and the burden of merging, were removed from the customer's view entirely.

Conditional writes take the place of vector clocks. Where Dynamo returned two versions and asked the application to merge, DynamoDB lets the application say "write this only if the item is as I last saw it", and tells the loser to read again.

// Optimistic concurrency with the AWS SDK for Java 2.x: succeed only if nobody has written since we read.
UpdateItemRequest request = UpdateItemRequest.builder()
		.tableName("Carts")
		.key(Map.of("UserId", AttributeValue.fromS(userId)))
		.updateExpression("SET Items = :items, Version = :next")
		.conditionExpression("Version = :seen")
		.expressionAttributeValues(Map.of(
				":items", AttributeValue.fromSs(items),
				":seen", AttributeValue.fromN(Long.toString(seenVersion)),
				":next", AttributeValue.fromN(Long.toString(seenVersion + 1))))
		.build();
try {
	dynamo.updateItem(request);
} catch (ConditionalCheckFailedException e) {
	readAgainAndRetry();                                      // another writer got there first, and we know it
}

Throughput, partitions and admission control

The other half of "simple to reason about" was performance. A customer states how many reads and writes a second a table needs, its provisioned throughput, and DynamoDB promises single-digit-millisecond latency up to that rate and refuses requests beyond it. Refusing is called throttling, and it protects every other customer on the same machines. The promise is easy to state and proved hard to keep fairly, for a reason that comes straight from Chapter 20.

A table is divided into partitions by the hash of each item's partition key, and a partition lives on one set of three replicas. In the first design the table's throughput was divided equally among its partitions, and each partition enforced its share alone. That is correct only if traffic is spread evenly over the key space. It rarely is. A table with a few popular keys would have some partitions refusing requests while the table as a whole used half of what its owner was paying for. And as a table grew and its partitions split, each partition's share shrank, so a table could begin throttling because it had been given more capacity. The engineers called this throughput dilution.

The fixes arrived over several years, and each is a small lesson in admission control. First bursting: a partition may spend capacity it left unused over the previous five minutes, which absorbs short spikes. Then adaptive capacity: the service watches for a partition being throttled while the table is under its limit, and raises that partition's share. And finally global admission control: the limit is enforced for the table as a whole, by a central service that hands out tokens to the request routers, and partitions keep only a ceiling to protect the machines they run on.

The 2,000 keys are placed in partitions by a real hash of their names, so an "even" spread is close to even and not exactly so. Skewed traffic follows a Zipf pattern, the most popular key taking the largest share and each next one less. The ceiling on one partition is taken as 1,000 writes a second, DynamoDB's published figure.

Things to notice. As it opens the table has 8 partitions and skewed traffic, is used at 80% of what is provisioned, and gives each partition an equal share of 500. It refuses 206 writes a second, 6.4% of the traffic, on two partitions, the busier of which wants 698. The owner is paying for 4,000 and using 3,200. Now grow the table. With 4 partitions it refuses 54 a second. With 16 it refuses 482, and with 32, 714, more than a fifth of all writes, for the same traffic and the same bill. That is throughput dilution. At 16 partitions the table throttles even when used at 50%.

Change the admission control to one limit for the table. With 8 partitions or more, every one of those cases falls to nothing refused. Then choose "one key takes 40%" at 80% use. 526 writes a second are still refused, since that one key wants 1,280 a second and a partition may serve 1,000. No scheme can spread a single key over two partitions. The first problem was the service's to fix, and it was fixed. The second belongs to whoever chose the key.

Lessons

In 2022 DynamoDB's engineers published a second paper, fifteen years after the first, describing the service as it had become. Read with the original, it is a rare thing: a design's authors reporting what they kept and what they gave up.

  • Predictable beats fast. The original's focus on the 99.9th percentile survived intact, and became a rule that the system should behave the same under stress as at rest. Their example is the routing table. Request routers cached it, and when a cache was cold every request went to the metadata service at once, which is how a large outage in 2015 began (Chapter 4 used it as its example). The redesign has routers refresh from the metadata store on every request, cached or not, so that its load is the same on a good day and a bad one. A system with two modes will one day find itself in the wrong one.
  • Operations are part of the product. Dynamo lost to a weaker system that needed no operators. DynamoDB's users never choose a replica count or run a repair.
  • Simple semantics win customers. A conditional write that either succeeds or reports failure is easier to build on than two versions and a request to merge them. The original's flexibility was a cost that each user paid separately.
  • Enforce limits where the limit is meant. A table's allowance belongs to the table. Enforcing it partition by partition was simpler to build, and punished customers for the shape of their data.
  • Check everything, always. The paper describes checksums on every log record and every file, continuous comparison of replicas against archived logs, and model checking of the replication protocol with TLA+ (Chapter 38). At that scale every rare fault happens daily.
  • The AP design lives where it belongs. Global tables, replicating between regions with last-writer-wins, are the original Dynamo trade-off, offered now as a choice for those who need writes in every region and can live with its conflicts.

Use case: Amazon turns off its last Oracle database

The story that began with a relational database failing during the 2004 holiday season has a recorded ending. In October 2019 Amazon announced that its consumer business, the shop, Prime, Alexa, Kindle and the rest, had shut down its final Oracle database, concluding a migration of several years. The figures it gave were 75 petabytes of data in about 7,500 Oracle databases, belonging to more than a hundred teams, moved to Amazon's own services: relational workloads to Aurora, analytics to Redshift, and the key-addressed, high-volume systems, the carts, sessions, catalogues and workflow state that had been Dynamo's reason for existing, to DynamoDB. The announcement reported lower cost, lower latency and far less administrative work. It is also the proof of the claim the 2007 paper could only argue: that most of what a large online business stores is looked up by key, and is better served by a store built for that than by a general database stretched to fit. The scale at which the claim now holds is on record too. During Amazon's Prime Day sale in 2021, Amazon's own services made trillions of calls to DynamoDB over the event, peaking at 89.2 million requests a second, with single-digit-millisecond responses throughout. What carried that load was not the leaderless ring of the paper. It was the service of this chapter: a leader for each partition, consensus across three zones, conditional writes, and admission control that had been rebuilt three times.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Describing DynamoDB with the Dynamo paper. DynamoDB has leaders and consensus, and no vector clocks or sloppy quorums within a region.
  • A low-variety partition key. A status, a date or a tenant with one huge customer sends most traffic to one partition, whose ceiling no setting raises.
  • Read, change, write back, with no condition. Two writers silently overwrite each other. Use a condition or an atomic update expression.
  • Assuming reads are consistent. The default read may be stale. Ask for a consistent read where it matters, and know that secondary indexes are updated asynchronously.
  • Treating global tables as a strongly consistent multi-region database. Between regions it is last writer wins.
  • Retrying throttled requests immediately. That adds load to a partition that is already refusing it. Back off, with jitter.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is the difference between Dynamo and DynamoDB?

How a strong answer sounds
Dynamo is the internal key–value store described in Amazon's 2007 paper, built for things like the shopping cart, where writes must always succeed. It is leaderless: data is placed on a consistent-hash ring, each key is stored on N nodes, any node can coordinate a write, and reads and writes use quorums of R and W. It favours availability, so concurrent writes can create conflicting versions, tracked with vector clocks and merged by the application. DynamoDB is the managed cloud database launched in 2012. It shares the key–value model and the focus on predictable low latency, but its internals are different. Each partition has three replicas across availability zones with a single leader elected by Multi-Paxos; writes go to the leader and are acknowledged when two of three have them. That gives a single order of writes per partition, so it can offer strongly consistent reads, conditional writes and, later, transactions, and it does not expose vector clocks or siblings. It is fully managed, with provisioned or on-demand capacity. The Dynamo-style eventually consistent, last-writer-wins model appears only in global tables, between regions.

Deeper: A DynamoDB table is being throttled although its consumed capacity is well under what is provisioned. Why, and what do you do?

How a strong answer sounds
Throttling below the table's limit means the load isn't evenly spread across partitions. DynamoDB hashes the partition key to place items in partitions, and each partition is served by one replica group with a hard ceiling, around 1,000 write units and 3,000 read units per second. Historically the table's provisioned throughput was also divided equally among partitions, so a skewed workload would throttle hot partitions while cold ones sat idle, and it got worse as the table grew and split into more partitions. Adaptive capacity and then table-level admission control largely removed that second problem, but the per-partition ceiling remains, and so does the case of a single hot key, which can't be split across partitions at all. So first I'd look at the key distribution: CloudWatch Contributor Insights shows the most accessed and most throttled keys. If one partition-key value dominates, such as a tenant ID with one large tenant, a status field or today's date, the fix is in the data model: choose a higher-cardinality key, or add a suffix to spread a hot key over several values, write sharding, and query across them. For hot reads of a few items I'd put a cache in front. If the pattern is bursts rather than skew, on-demand mode or auto scaling with a higher floor helps. I'd also check secondary indexes, since a global secondary index with its own skewed key is throttled separately and can back-pressure writes to the base table. And clients should retry throttled requests with exponential backoff and jitter, which the SDK does by default, rather than hammering the hot partition.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Dynamo (2007) was built so that the shopping cart could always be written. It accepted conflicts, and the occasional return of a deleted item.
  • It was leaderless, with N = 3, R = 2, W = 2 as the usual setting, and each team ran its own cluster and wrote its own merge logic.
  • DynamoDB (2012) is a managed service with a leader for each partition, Multi-Paxos across three zones, and writes acknowledged by two of three.
  • Conditional writes and consistent reads replaced vector clocks and siblings. Global tables keep last-writer-wins between regions.
  • Throughput is a promise enforced by admission control. Enforcing it per partition throttled skewed tables, and it is now enforced per table.
  • The lasting lessons are predictability, operability and simple semantics, ahead of flexibility.

In Dynamo's shopping cart, why could a deleted item reappear?

A union keeps every add and can't express a remove. The paper names this anomaly directly.

What replaced vector clocks and application merges as DynamoDB's answer to concurrent writers?

A leader gives each partition one order of writes, so a condition can be checked at the moment of writing.

A per-partition share is fair only for evenly spread keys. The limit was later moved to the table as a whole.
