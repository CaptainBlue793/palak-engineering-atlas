Dynamo-Style Eventual Consistency

The last seven chapters each took one mechanism: quorums, consistent hashing, partitioning, gossip, hash trees, self-merging data types. In 2007 Amazon published a paper describing a key–value store called Dynamo that used all of them together, in a design with no leader, where any node can accept any write and the system keeps answering through failed disks, failed nodes and failed datacentres. Cassandra, Riak and Voldemort were built on its plan, and "Dynamo-style" became the name of a whole family of databases. This chapter assembles the parts. It follows one write and one read through the full design, sets out the choices for resolving the conflicts that such a design must produce, and looks at the dial the family is known for: each request chooses how many replicas must answer, and with that, where it stands between fast and available at one end and consistent at the other. The last section is what operating one teaches. The story of Amazon's own system, and what it later became, is the case study in Chapter 42.

The Dynamo design

The requirement that shaped everything was stated in business terms: a customer must always be able to add an item to their cart. A write that is refused costs a sale, and a write that is accepted and later needs reconciling costs very little. So the store must be always writable, which in the terms of Chapter 10 means choosing availability under a partition, and low latency over consistency the rest of the time. A leader is ruled out by that requirement, since a leader that can't be reached, or is being replaced, is a period when writes stop.

With no leader, every node is the same, and each of the usual jobs needs a decentralised answer. The paper's central table is, in effect, this course's contents page for the last several chapters.

Conflict resolution

Two clients can write the same key through two coordinators at the same moment, and nothing in this design stops them. Both writes succeed. The replicas then hold different values, and something has to settle it. There are four approaches, and choosing among them is the main data-modelling decision in such a store.

Two details soften last-write-wins in practice. Cassandra applies it per column, not per row, so two clients updating different fields of the same row both succeed. And a data model that only ever inserts, each event under its own unique key, has nothing to overwrite: there are no conflicts in an append-only table. The dangerous case is the one that looks most natural, reading a value, changing it, and writing it back.

Tunable consistency

Chapter 12 gave the rule for one group of N replicas: a read is certain to see the latest acknowledged write when R + W > N. Real deployments put replicas in more than one datacentre, and the choice gains a second dimension: how many replicas, and where. With three replicas in each of two datacentres, six in all, Cassandra's levels mean the following.

The level is set on each request, as in the code above, so one application can read a product page at ONE and place an order at QUORUM against the same cluster.

"Guaranteed" is worked out by checking every set of replicas that could satisfy the write level against every set that could satisfy the read level, for one that shares no replica.

Things to notice. As it opens, both levels are LOCAL_QUORUM and both clients are in London. The write returns after 2 ms, having heard from L1 and L2, while L3 is still two milliseconds from receiving it. The read asks L3 and L2. L3 answers v1, L2 answers v2, and the newer wins. That is the overlap at work: any two of London's three replicas include one of the two that acknowledged. Now move the reader to Virginia, and it is given v1. Nothing has failed. The write was acknowledged 2 ms after it was sent, and Virginia is 75 ms away. Read "one second later" and the answer is v2, marked as not guaranteed. Then cut the link, and it is v1 however long you wait.

Drop both levels to ONE with both clients in London. The write takes 1 ms, the read takes 1 ms, and the read is stale, since each coordinator asked the replica nearest to itself. Raise both to QUORUM and the read is right from either city at any moment, and each request takes 76 ms, since four replicas can't be found in one datacentre. Cut the link under QUORUM and both requests are refused. EACH_QUORUM for the write with LOCAL_QUORUM for the read gives a 77 ms write and a 2 ms read that is guaranteed in both cities, and writes stop when the link is cut. Last, take two London nodes down. LOCAL_QUORUM is refused in London, and QUORUM succeeds in 79 ms using L3 and the three replicas in Virginia: the level that was more available under one fault is the less available under another.

How eventual is eventual?

The panel's "one second later" hides a real question. The model promises only that replicas converge if writes stop, with no bound on when. In a healthy cluster the time is short: the write was sent to every replica at once, and the ones that weren't waited for are a few milliseconds behind. Peter Bailis and colleagues measured and modelled this in 2012 under the name probabilistically bounded staleness, and found that with latency distributions taken from production systems, a read at ONE after a write at ONE almost always returned the latest value once somewhere between a few milliseconds and a few hundred had passed, depending mostly on how variable the replicas' write latency was. That is why weak levels are tolerable in practice.

The bound is a matter of probability, and it holds only while things are healthy. A replica that is down, or a datacentre that is cut off, is stale for as long as the fault lasts, and then until its hints arrive or a repair runs. Design for the common case with a weak level if the data allows it, and be clear about which reads would do damage if they were hours behind.

Operational lessons

These are the ways this design surprises the people who run it. None is a bug. Each follows from a choice made above.

  • A quorum is not a transaction. Reading at QUORUM returns a recent value. It doesn't stop another client changing the value before you write back. Two clients that each read a balance of 100, subtract 10 and write 90 have lost an update, at any consistency level. Use an operation that needs no read (add to a set, increment a counter) or a conditional write.
  • A failed write may have happened. When a write times out with one acknowledgement where two were needed, the client gets an error, and the one replica keeps the value. Nothing rolls it back. A later read repair can spread it to the others. Treat a timeout as "unknown", and make writes idempotent so they can be retried (Chapter 4).
  • Last-write-wins trusts the clocks. A node whose clock runs ahead wins every conflict, and a delete stamped in the future silently swallows the writes that follow it. Monitor clock offset as a correctness metric (Chapter 6).
  • Deletes are writes. A delete is recorded as a tombstone, since a replica that missed it would otherwise bring the row back. Tombstones must be kept until every replica has them, which means until a repair has run. Many deletes mean slow reads, as the store wades through markers, and skipped repairs mean deleted data returning.
  • Sloppy quorums weaken the arithmetic. If a write counts acknowledgements from stand-in nodes, R + W > N no longer guarantees overlap, since the reader asks the proper nodes. Cassandra doesn't count hints towards the level, for that reason, unless the level is ANY.
  • Repair is not optional. Hints expire and read repair touches only what is read. Without scheduled anti-entropy, replicas drift apart indefinitely (Chapter 24).
  • LOCAL_QUORUM means the other datacentre is behind. It is the usual choice, fast and unaffected by a remote outage. Failing users over to the other datacentre then shows them slightly old data, and after a partition, possibly old by the length of the partition.

Use case: Discord's messages, and a channel with one message in it

Discord moved its chat messages to Cassandra and described the result in 2017. The model suits the store: messages are appended, read by channel in time order, and never need a transaction. Each partition holds one channel's messages for a window of about ten days, so no partition grows without limit. Two things the team reported follow directly from this chapter. The first was a race between two ordinary actions. One user edited a message at the moment another deleted it. A write in Cassandra is an upsert, with no check that the row exists, and columns are reconciled one by one by timestamp. So the delete removed the row, and the edit, arriving after it, wrote its one column into a row that had otherwise ceased to exist: a message with an ID and some text, and no author. No consistency level prevents this, since each operation was valid alone. They changed the code to recognise and remove such rows on read. The second was tombstones. One very large public server deleted nearly every message in a channel, millions of them, leaving one. Loading that channel made Cassandra read through the tombstones of all the deleted messages to find it, building enough garbage in memory to stall the node for ten seconds at a time. Tombstones were being kept for ten days, the default grace period that allows for a repair to carry each delete to every replica. Since Discord repaired its cluster every night, it cut that period to two days, and changed the query code to remember which time windows of a channel were empty and skip them. In 2023 the company wrote again: with trillions of messages and nearly two hundred nodes, the maintenance described in the last section, compaction falling behind, repairs, pauses from the JVM's garbage collector, had come to dominate the team's time, and it had migrated to ScyllaDB, a reimplementation of the same design in C++ with the same data model and the same consistency levels.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Read, modify, write at QUORUM and calling it safe. Quorums give recency, not isolation.
  • Assuming an error means nothing was written. A timed-out write may be on some replicas and may spread.
  • Choosing levels by habit. Work out which pairs overlap for where your readers and writers are.
  • Using deletes heavily, or as a queue. Tombstones pile up in exactly the rows you read most.
  • Letting clients set timestamps from unsynchronised clocks. The fastest clock wins every conflict.
  • Skipping repair because the cluster looks healthy. Drift is invisible until a node is replaced or data reappears.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How does a Dynamo-style database stay available when nodes fail?

How a strong answer sounds
It has no leader, so there is no single node whose failure stops writes. Keys are placed on a consistent-hash ring and each key is stored on N nodes, typically three. Any node can coordinate a request: it sends the write to all N replicas and waits for only W acknowledgements, or R replies for a read. So with N of 3 and W of 2, one replica can be down and writes still succeed. If a replica is unreachable, the coordinator can hand its copy to another node with a hint to pass it on later. The price is that replicas can disagree. A replica that missed a write is brought up to date by hinted handoff when it returns, by read repair when a read notices it is stale, and by background anti-entropy using Merkle trees. Concurrent writes to the same key are both accepted and reconciled afterwards, by timestamp, by returning both versions to the application, or with CRDTs. Membership and failure detection use gossip, so there is no central registry either. The result is a system that chooses availability and low latency, and offers eventual consistency by default, with per-request quorum settings to tighten it.

Deeper: Cassandra, two datacentres, replication factor three in each. What consistency levels would you use, and what do you give up?

How a strong answer sounds
My default would be LOCAL_QUORUM for both reads and writes. Two of three local replicas must answer, so within a datacentre two plus two exceeds three: a read sees any write acknowledged in that datacentre, giving read-your-writes for users pinned to one region. Latency stays local, a couple of milliseconds, and the datacentre keeps working if the other one, or the link, is lost, and through one local node failure. What I give up: replication to the other datacentre is asynchronous, so a reader there may see stale data, normally by milliseconds, and during a partition by the length of the partition; and if I fail users over to the other region, some recent writes may not be there yet. If cross-region consistency matters for some operation, the options are QUORUM, four of six, which overlaps everywhere but puts a WAN round trip on every request and fails when the link is cut; or EACH_QUORUM for writes with LOCAL_QUORUM reads, which makes reads fast and consistent in both regions but makes writes slow and unavailable if either datacentre is unreachable. For anything with an invariant, uniqueness, a balance, I would not rely on levels at all: a quorum is not isolation, so I would use a lightweight transaction with SERIAL or LOCAL_SERIAL, accepting several round trips. And I would be explicit that LOCAL_QUORUM tolerates one node down per datacentre, not two; with two down, that datacentre's requests fail unless I downgrade, which I would not do automatically for writes.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • The Dynamo design is leaderless: consistent hashing, N replicas, quorums, hints, version vectors, hash-tree repair and gossip, combined.
  • Any node coordinates a request: send to all N replicas, wait for W or R, let the rest catch up.
  • Concurrent writes are accepted and settled afterwards: by timestamp (losing data), by siblings, by CRDTs, or prevented with a conditional write.
  • Consistency is chosen per request. With several datacentres the question is how many replicas and where.
  • LOCAL_QUORUM is consistent within a datacentre and eventually consistent between them.
  • A quorum is not a transaction, a failed write may persist, and deletes and repair need active care.

Check your understanding

Why does the Dynamo design have no leader?

"Always writable" was the requirement. Everything else in the design follows from removing the single writer.

Three replicas in each of two datacentres. A write at LOCAL_QUORUM succeeds in London. Which read is certain to see it?

Two of London's three hold it, and any two of London's three include at least one of those.

Two clients each read a counter at QUORUM, see 100, and write 90 at QUORUM. What is stored?

Both writes are valid alone. Use an increment operation or a conditional write.

A write needs two acknowledgements, gets one, and the client receives a timeout. What is true afterwards?

There is no rollback in this design. The outcome is unknown, so the write should be safe to repeat.

Why must a delete be stored as a tombstone, and kept for a time?

Absence can't be told apart from "never heard of it". The marker has to outlive the next full repair.
