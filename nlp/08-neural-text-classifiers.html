Neural Text Classifiers

The first neural text classifiers were small. An embedding bag averages word vectors and adds a linear layer; a convolutional network slides filters over the word sequence and keeps the strongest match of each, so it can detect phrases like “not good” wherever they occur. Both train in seconds on a GPU, both can start from pretrained embeddings, and both have to answer one awkward question: are they better than TF-IDF?

This chapter builds them, trains each three times on three datasets, and compares them with the TF-IDF baseline of Chapters 3–4. TF-IDF won on long reviews and on news headlines. A GloVe-initialised CNN beat it only on SST-2’s short sentences, by 0.8 points. Frozen GloVe embeddings were the worst neural option on every dataset. With 1,000 training reviews, TF-IDF led the best neural model by 4.5 points.

  • Build an embedding-bag classifier and a 1-D CNN over text in PyTorch
  • Use pretrained embeddings frozen or fine-tuned, and know when each helps
  • Regularise small neural text models
  • Report results over several seeds
  • Recognise when TF-IDF still wins

Embedding bags

An embedding bag looks each token up in an embedding table, averages the vectors of the real (non-padding) tokens, and feeds the average to a linear layer. It is a bag of words in which each word is a learned dense vector instead of a column, so excellent and superb can share what they learn. fastText (Joulin et al., 2017) adds hashed bigrams to the bag, recovering the short phrases that matter for sentiment, and is famously fast.

class Bag(nn.Module):
    def __init__(s, V, K, d=100, emb=None, freeze=False):
        super().__init__(); s.e = nn.Embedding(V, d, padding_idx=0); s.o = nn.Linear(d, K)
        if emb is not None: s.e.weight.data[:] = emb                  # GloVe rows; unknown words random
        s.e.weight.requires_grad = not freeze
    def forward(s, x):
        m = (x != 0).float()[..., None]                               # mask out padding (P2)
        return s.o((s.e(x) * m).sum(1) / m.sum(1))

CNNs over text

A 1-D convolution over text slides a filter across windows of k consecutive word vectors and outputs a score per position; max-over-time pooling keeps each filter’s highest score anywhere in the text. With 100 filters each of widths 3, 4 and 5 (Kim, 2014), the model learns 300 phrase detectors and asks only whether each phrase appeared, not where. That is closer to an n-gram model than to a reader, but with phrases that generalise: a filter that fires on “not good” can also fire on “not great”.

s.c = nn.ModuleList([nn.Conv1d(100, 100, k, padding=k // 2) for k in (3, 4, 5)])
h = s.e(x).transpose(1, 2)                                       # (batch, 100 dims, length)
z = [F.relu(c(h))[:, :, :L].masked_fill(~mask, -1e4).max(2).values for c in s.c]   # max over time, pads excluded
logits = s.o(s.dp(torch.cat(z, 1)))                                   # 300 features → classes, dropout 0.5

On IMDB the TF-IDF baseline (90.94%) beat every neural model by at least two points; the best neural model was the fastText-style bag with hashed bigrams (88.89%), which is to say the neural model that looks most like TF-IDF. On AG News TF-IDF won again (92.70%), with the GloVe CNN 0.4 behind. Only on SST-2 did neural models lead: the GloVe CNN reached 82.87% against 82.11%, a gap about the size of one seed’s spread. SST-2’s phrases are short, so a bag of n-grams has few clues per example, and the CNN’s ability to match “not good” to “not great” through embeddings is worth something. IMDB reviews average over 230 words; a bag of 400,000 n-grams sees plenty, and the CNN, which reads only the first 400 tokens and keeps the strongest 300 phrase matches, throws much of it away.

These models are small and fast: most took 2–5 seconds an epoch on a GPU (the hashed-bigram bags, with two million extra embedding rows, took 14–51). The honest conclusion for 2014-style neural classifiers is the one the field reached at the time: they match a well-tuned bag of n-grams, beat it on short texts, and are not worth their complexity on long documents. What changed that was pretraining whole networks, not just embeddings (Chapters 16–17).

Pretrained vs learned embeddings

Three ways to use GloVe were compared in the embedding bag. Frozen GloVe was by far the worst on every dataset: 72.86% on IMDB against 87.52% for embeddings learned from random initialisation, and 73.47% against 81.04% on SST-2. GloVe places good and bad close together, because they appear in the same contexts (Chapter 6), and averaged frozen vectors cannot separate what the vectors do not. Fine-tuning GloVe was the best start, but only by 0.3–0.6 points over random initialisation, because 25,000 to 120,000 labelled examples are enough to learn word vectors for the task from scratch. Coverage mattered too: GloVe had vectors for 97.9% of SST-2’s vocabulary but only 84.2% of IMDB’s, whose reviews are full of names and misspellings. For the CNN, GloVe initialisation helped most on AG News (+1.6 points), where it brings knowledge about the many named entities in headlines.

Regularisation

With dropout 0.5 on the pooled features, the GloVe CNN on SST-2 finished at 82.45% ± 0.19 after 8 epochs; without dropout, 81.08% ± 0.93. The curves show why: without dropout, validation accuracy peaked at 83.7% in epoch 5 and fell to 79.9% by epoch 8 as the model memorised, while with dropout it stayed between 81 and 84 throughout. Dropout also cut the spread between seeds by a factor of five. Adding weight decay on top changed nothing measurable (82.49%). With models this small, dropout plus stopping at the best validation epoch is the whole regularisation story.

A linear model over sparse n-grams has far fewer effective parameters to fit and nothing to initialise badly. TF-IDF still wins when labels are few, texts are long, and the signal is in specific words and phrases, which describes most first versions of most text classifiers. Measure it first.

Use case: intent detection in a banking chatbot

A bank’s chatbot must map short messages (“freeze my card”, “why was I charged twice”) to 60 intents, with 150 labelled examples per intent. The team tries TF-IDF with logistic regression, an embedding bag and a CNN, each with three seeds. With messages this short, bigrams matter and data is limited, so TF-IDF and the GloVe-initialised CNN finish within a point of each other; the CNN is better on paraphrases with no shared words (“lost my wallet with my card in it”), TF-IDF on exact phrases. They ship TF-IDF first because it explains itself, and later replace both with a fine-tuned encoder (Chapter 17) once they have 20,000 labelled messages, keeping the TF-IDF model as a fallback.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Freezing pretrained word embeddings in a bag model: frozen GloVe lost 4–15 points to embeddings learned from scratch, because it cannot separate words like “good” and “bad” that share contexts.
  • Training without dropout: the CNN peaked at epoch 5 and then lost almost 4 points by epoch 8.
  • Assuming neural beats TF-IDF: it did not on IMDB or AG News, nor at any training size on IMDB.
  • Reporting one seed: runs of the same model differed by up to 3.1 points here.
  • Forgetting the mask in pooling: padding leaks into averages and maxima (P2).
  • Skipping the TF-IDF baseline: it is the number every neural model must beat.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How does a CNN classify text?

How a strong answer sounds
It embeds each word, then slides a set of learned filters over windows of consecutive word vectors, say widths 3, 4 and 5, producing a score for every position; each filter acts as a soft phrase detector. Max-over-time pooling keeps each filter’s highest score anywhere in the text, so the model knows which phrases occurred but not where, and a linear layer on those few hundred features makes the prediction. Because filters work on embeddings, a filter that fires on “not good” also fires on “not great”. Measured on SST-2’s short phrases, a GloVe-initialised CNN beat TF-IDF by 0.8 points; on long IMDB reviews TF-IDF still won by 3.6.

Deeper: Should you initialise a text classifier with pretrained word embeddings, and should you freeze them?

How a strong answer sounds
Initialise with them, usually, and let them be fine-tuned. Frozen GloVe in an embedding bag was the worst option on all three datasets I measured, 4 to 15 points below learning embeddings from scratch, because distributional vectors put opposites like good and bad together and a frozen model cannot pull them apart. Fine-tuning from GloVe beat random initialisation by only 0.3 to 0.6 points with tens of thousands of labels, and by more for the CNN on news headlines, where the vectors know about named entities. With little data, freezing can reduce overfitting, but compare it; with plenty of data, initialisation matters less than you expect. Check vocabulary coverage too: GloVe covered 98% of SST-2’s words but 84% of IMDB’s.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • An embedding bag is a learned, dense bag of words; a text CNN is a bank of learned phrase detectors with max-over-time pooling.
  • Measured on three datasets: TF-IDF won on long reviews (IMDB) and news (AG News); a GloVe CNN won only on short SST-2 phrases, by 0.8 points.
  • Frozen GloVe was the worst neural option everywhere; fine-tuned GloVe beat random initialisation by only 0.3–0.6 points with enough data.
  • Dropout kept the CNN from overfitting (82.45% vs 81.08%) and cut seed spread fivefold; with 1,000 examples TF-IDF led by 4.5 points.
  • Report the mean and spread over seeds, and always beside the TF-IDF baseline.
