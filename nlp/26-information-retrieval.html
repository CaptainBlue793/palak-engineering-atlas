Information Retrieval: BM25 to Dense

Search engines, help-centre search and the “R” in retrieval-augmented generation all solve one problem: given a query, rank a large collection so the relevant documents come first. For thirty years the answer was word matching with BM25. Dense retrieval embeds queries and documents into vectors and matches meaning instead. Neither wins everywhere, and the best systems combine them.

This chapter builds both from scratch on a scientific-claims benchmark and measures every stage. BM25, written in 20 lines, scored nDCG@10 66.6 on SciFact in 0.27 ms per query. Of three small dense retrievers one beat it clearly (bge-small, 70.4), one narrowly (e5-small, 67.7) and one not at all (MiniLM, 64.8). Fusing BM25 with bge gave the best recall (96.8% of relevant abstracts in the top 100), and a general-purpose cross-encoder re-ranker made the fused ranking slightly worse.

  • Build an inverted index and BM25, and understand k1 and b
  • Retrieve with dense bi-encoders and their query and passage prefixes
  • Combine lexical and dense rankings with reciprocal-rank fusion
  • Re-rank with a cross-encoder and pay for it in latency
  • Compute nDCG, MRR and recall by hand, and know which one to report

The inverted index and BM25

An inverted index maps each word to the list of documents containing it, with counts: the “postings”. A query only touches the postings of its own words, which is why lexical search over billions of documents is fast. BM25 (Robertson et al., 1994) scores a document by summing, over query words, an IDF weight times a saturating term-frequency:

score(d, q) = Σw ∈ q idf(w) · tf·(k1 + 1) / (tf + k1·(1 − b + b·|d| / avgdl))

k1 controls saturation: the tenth occurrence of a word adds far less than the first. b controls length normalisation: long documents contain more words by chance, so their counts are discounted. The lab’s first view runs BM25 live on eight short documents; move k1 and b and watch the ranking change.

Dense retrieval and re-ranking

A bi-encoder embeds every document once, offline, and the query at search time; retrieval is a nearest-neighbour search over vectors. It matches “heart attack” to “myocardial infarction”, which BM25 cannot, but it compresses a document into one vector and can miss rare exact terms like gene names. A cross-encoder reads query and document together and outputs a relevance score; it is far more accurate and far too slow to run over a whole corpus, so it re-ranks the top 100 from a cheaper retriever. Reciprocal-rank fusion (Cormack et al., 2009) merges two rankings by summing 1 / (60 + rank) for each document, needing no score calibration.

Measuring a ranking

Recall@100: the share of relevant documents in the top 100, which bounds what a re-ranker can recover. MRR@10: one over the rank of the first relevant document. nDCG@10: each relevant document in the top 10 counts 1 / log2(rank + 1), divided by the best achievable; it is the BEIR standard. All three are written from scratch in this chapter’s script.

BM25 is hard to beat on scientific text, where exact technical terms carry the meaning, and its two classic parameter settings gave the same score (66.6 and 66.9). The dense retrievers depended heavily on how they were trained. bge-small, trained with a retrieval instruction on large retrieval datasets, beat BM25 by 3.8 points of nDCG@10; e5-small by 1.1; and all-MiniLM-L6-v2, trained mainly for sentence similarity, fell 1.8 points below BM25. Every dense model had better recall@100 than BM25 (92.8–94.7 against 88.6): they rarely miss entirely, even when their ordering at the top is worse.

Fusion gave the best of both: reciprocal-rank fusion of BM25 and bge matched bge’s nDCG@10 (70.4) and found 96.8% of relevant abstracts in its top 100, the highest of any method. The cross-encoder, ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2, trained on web-search questions, improved BM25’s ranking (66.6 → 68.2) but lowered the hybrid’s (70.4 → 69.3), at about 45 ms per query for 100 candidates. A re-ranker is a model trained on someone else’s notion of relevance; on claims about biomedical findings that notion transferred only partly. Measured costs: BM25 about 0.27 ms per query on the CPU; dense retrieval about 3.8 ms on the GPU, plus 2.9 s to encode the corpus once into a 7.6 MB index.

Where lexical and dense disagree

Per query, the two kinds of retrieval fail differently. bge beat BM25 by more than 0.2 nDCG@10 on 63 of the 300 claims, and BM25 beat bge on 45: they are complementary, which is why fusion works. Dense retrieval won where the claim and the abstract use different words for the same thing: for “Vitamin D deficiency effects the term of delivery.”, BM25’s top hit was an abstract that shares the words “vitamin D” but is about osteoporosis. BM25 won where one rare term decides relevance: for “Ivermectin is used to treat onchocerciasis.”, the dense retriever’s top hit was about blood-pressure treatment, while BM25 matched the rare word “onchocerciasis” exactly. Claims that negate a finding (“LDL cholesterol has no involvement in the development of cardiovascular disease.”) also went BM25’s way here; dense embeddings are notoriously weak at negation (Chapter 20).

Use case: search over an internal knowledge base for a RAG assistant

A company’s assistant answers staff questions from 200,000 wiki pages. The first version used a dense retriever alone and kept missing pages about specific product codes and error numbers. The team labels 200 real questions with their correct pages, measures recall@20 for BM25, dense and a hybrid, and finds the hybrid recovers both the paraphrased questions and the exact-code ones. A cross-encoder re-ranks the top 50, adding 40 ms. They log every query whose top results the user ignored, and those become next quarter’s evaluation set.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Assuming any embedding model beats BM25: all-MiniLM-L6-v2 scored 64.8 nDCG@10 against BM25’s 66.6 on SciFact.
  • Adding an off-the-shelf re-ranker without measuring: the MS MARCO cross-encoder lowered the hybrid’s nDCG@10 from 70.4 to 69.3.
  • Reporting nDCG without recall: BM25 and the hybrid had similar orderings at the top but recall@100 of 88.6 against 96.8, which is what a re-ranker or a reader can work with.
  • Forgetting a model’s query and passage prefixes: e5 and bge are trained with them; dropping them quietly costs points.
  • Evaluating on a handful of hand-picked queries: retrieval quality varies hugely per query; use hundreds, with judged relevance.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is the difference between BM25, a bi-encoder and a cross-encoder?

How a strong answer sounds
BM25 scores a document by the query words it contains, weighted by how rare each word is (IDF) with term frequency saturating and long documents discounted; it runs over an inverted index in under a millisecond and needs no training, but cannot match synonyms. A bi-encoder embeds the query and each document separately into vectors and retrieves by cosine similarity, so it matches meaning across different words; documents are embedded once in advance, so it scales. A cross-encoder reads the query and one document together through a transformer and outputs a relevance score: the most accurate in principle, but one forward pass per pair, so it is used only to re-rank a short list. On SciFact: BM25 66.6 nDCG@10, the bge bi-encoder 70.4, and the hybrid of the two 70.4; the off-the-shelf cross-encoder helped BM25 but not the hybrid.

Deeper: Design the retrieval for a RAG system over 10 million documents with a 200 ms budget.

How a strong answer sounds
Two stages, measured on a few hundred real queries with judged relevance. First stage, in parallel: BM25 over an inverted index (Elasticsearch, OpenSearch) and a dense bi-encoder over an approximate-nearest-neighbour index (HNSW, possibly with int8 or product quantisation to fit 10 million vectors in memory), each returning about 100 candidates in tens of milliseconds, fused by reciprocal-rank fusion; here fusion gave the best recall@100 (96.8%). Second stage: a cross-encoder re-ranks the top 50–100 within the remaining budget (about 45 ms for 100 here on one GPU, so batch carefully or re-rank fewer), but only if it is fine-tuned for or measured on the domain, because an off-the-shelf one lowered nDCG here. Track recall@100 for the first stage and nDCG@10 after re-ranking, and log queries where users ignore the results as future test cases.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Measured: BM25 66.6 nDCG@10 in 0.27 ms; bge-small 70.4, e5-small 67.7, MiniLM 64.8: the dense model’s training decides whether it beats BM25.
  • Measured: BM25 + bge fusion reached recall@100 of 96.8%, the best of any method; each beat the other by a wide margin on different queries (63 vs 45).
  • Measured: an MS MARCO cross-encoder helped BM25 (66.6 → 68.2) and hurt the hybrid (70.4 → 69.3), at about 45 ms per query.
  • BM25 is a strong, cheap, explainable baseline; always measure it.
  • Report nDCG@10 for ranking quality and recall@k for what a re-ranker can recover.

Check your understanding

What does BM25’s b parameter control?

b = 0 ignores length; b = 1 normalises fully. Try it in the lab.

Why not run a cross-encoder over the whole corpus?

Measured: re-ranking 100 candidates took about 45 ms per query; scoring all 5,183 abstracts would take about 2.3 s per query.

What does reciprocal-rank fusion need from the two systems it merges?

Each document gets Σ 1 / (60 + rank), so BM25 scores and cosine similarities never need to be on the same scale.

Why is recall@100 important when you add a re-ranker?

First-stage recall is the ceiling on the whole pipeline.

Which query is BM25 most likely to win over a dense retriever?

Measured: “Ivermectin is used to treat onchocerciasis.”: BM25 nDCG@10 1.0, dense 0.0.
