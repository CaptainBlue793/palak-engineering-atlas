Long Documents & Hierarchical Models

BERT-style encoders read at most 512 tokens, because self-attention compares every token with every other and its cost grows with the square of the length. Contracts, papers, support threads and long reviews do not fit. You can truncate, split into chunks and combine, build a hierarchy, or use a model with sparse attention that reads thousands of tokens. Which works best depends on where in the document the answer lives.

This chapter tries all of them on the IMDB reviews that overflow 512 tokens. 12.3% of IMDB test reviews exceed BERT’s limit (median 685 tokens, longest 2,873). On those, a DistilBERT fine-tuned on the first 512 tokens scored 90.99% reading the start, 92.22% reading the end and 93.07% averaging overlapping chunks. Longformer, reading up to 2,048 tokens, scored only 84.83%, but it was trained on 4,000 reviews against DistilBERT’s 25,000.

Why 512?

Self-attention builds an n × n matrix of scores per head per layer. Doubling the length quadruples that matrix. BERT was also pre-trained with 512 learnt position embeddings, so position 513 has no embedding at all. Longformer (Beltagy et al., 2020) replaces full attention with a sliding window (each token attends to 256 neighbours on each side here) plus a few global tokens such as [CLS] that attend everywhere, making the cost linear in length, and extends the position embeddings to 4,096.

Five ways to fit a long document

  • Head: keep the first 510 tokens. Standard, and blind to conclusions.
  • Tail: keep the last 510. Reviews often end with the verdict.
  • Head + tail (Sun et al., 2019): the first 128 and last 382 tokens.
  • Chunk and aggregate: split into overlapping 510-token windows, classify each, and average the logits (or trust the most confident chunk).
  • Hierarchical: encode each chunk to a vector, then train a small attention layer that weights the chunks and classifies the weighted sum.

Truncation at the head cost the most: 90.99% on long reviews, against 93.51% for the same model on reviews that fit. Reading the last 512 tokens instead raised it to 92.22%, and head plus tail to 92.03%: reviewers tend to give their verdict at the end, after the plot summary. Chunking with a 255-token stride and averaging the chunks’ logits was best (93.07%), recovering almost all the gap to short reviews at about 2.6× the compute of head truncation. Trusting the single most confident chunk was nearly as good (92.94%): in reviews, one strongly worded passage usually carries the verdict. The small hierarchical head over frozen chunk vectors reached 92.0%, no better than head plus tail, because the chunk vectors came from a model trained only on review openings and the head was trained on just 3,319 long reviews.

Longformer read up to 2,048 tokens and scored 84.83%, the worst here. That is not a fair verdict on long-context models: it was fine-tuned for one epoch on 4,000 reviews because training at 2,048 tokens is slow (4.5 minutes for those 4,000, with gradient checkpointing), while the DistilBERT behind every other row had all 25,000. The honest lesson is about cost: the long-context model was too expensive to train properly in this budget, and simple chunking with a short-context model, trained on plenty of data, was better and cheaper.

Measured: what length costs

For one sequence at a time, BERT-base took about 3.39 ms at 128, 256 and 512 tokens alike: at batch 1 the GPU is mostly idle, and the time is spent launching kernels, not on arithmetic, so the n² cost of attention is not yet visible. Longformer took 15.26 ms at 1,024 tokens, 25.84 at 2,048 and 50.8 at 4,096, roughly doubling with length, the linear growth sliding-window attention promises. (Its 512-token figure, 29.37 ms, is out of line with the rest and is probably a first-shape warm-up effect we did not exclude.) Peak memory grew from 1,932 MB to 2,280 MB between 1,024 and 4,096 tokens, mostly model weights; at batch 1 activations are small. The quadratic cost of full attention bites in training and at large batches, which is where the lab’s chunking calculator applies.

Use case: classifying contracts by risk

A legal-tech team classifies 40-page supplier contracts by whether they contain unusual liability terms. Truncation is useless: the clauses are in the middle. They chunk each contract into overlapping windows, score each window with a fine-tuned encoder, and flag the contract if any window scores high, a “most confident chunk” rule, because one risky clause is enough. The flagged windows double as the explanation shown to the lawyer. A long-context model was tested and was slower without being better at finding single clauses.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Truncating to the first 512 tokens by default: it scored 90.99% on long IMDB reviews, against 92.22% for the last 512.
  • Concluding long-context models are worse from an under-trained one: Longformer scored 84.83% after one epoch on 4,000 reviews; the comparison was about budget, not architecture.
  • Measuring attention cost at batch 1: BERT-base took 3.39 ms at every length from 128 to 512; the quadratic cost only shows when the GPU is busy.
  • Training on truncated text, testing on chunks: a chunk from the middle of a review looks unlike any training example; say so, or train on chunks too.
  • Choosing the aggregation without thinking about the task: mean suits overall tone; max suits “does any part contain X”.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Why can’t BERT read a 3,000-token document, and what can you do about it?

How a strong answer sounds
BERT was pre-trained with 512 learned position embeddings, so position 513 has no embedding, and full self-attention compares every token with every other, so cost and memory grow with the square of the length. Options: truncate, keeping the start, the end or both, which works when the signal is in one place (on long IMDB reviews the last 512 tokens scored 92.22% against 90.99% for the first); split into overlapping chunks, classify each and combine (mean of logits gave 93.07%, best here); a hierarchical model that encodes chunks and attends over them; or a long-context model such as Longformer, whose sliding-window attention is linear in length, at the price of slower training and the need to fine-tune it properly.

Deeper: Design a classifier for 50-page legal documents where one clause decides the label.

How a strong answer sounds
Chunk the document into overlapping windows of about 512 tokens along clause or paragraph boundaries where possible, score each window with a fine-tuned encoder trained on clause-level labels (window-level labels from annotated clauses, not document labels, because one clause decides), and flag the document if any window scores above a threshold: max aggregation, since one risky clause is enough, with the flagged windows shown as the evidence. Train on windows that contain the clause and plenty that do not, including near-misses, and set the threshold for high recall on a held-out set of documents, because a missed clause costs more than a review. A long-context model is worth testing if clauses depend on definitions far away in the document, but measure it against this baseline at equal training budget.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Measured: on IMDB reviews over 510 tokens, first 512 90.99%, last 512 92.22%, chunk averaging 93.07% (short reviews: 93.51%).
  • Measured: a Longformer trained on 4,000 reviews at 2,048 tokens scored 84.83%; long-context models need a training budget to match.
  • Measured: Longformer’s time per sequence roughly doubled with each doubling of length (15.26 ms at 1,024 tokens, 50.8 at 4,096); BERT at batch 1 was flat at 3.39 ms up to 512.
  • Full attention costs grow with the square of the length; sliding windows make it linear.
  • Where the answer lives in the document decides which strategy wins.

Check your understanding

Why does doubling the input length roughly quadruple self-attention’s cost?

The feed-forward layers grow linearly; attention grows quadratically.

How does Longformer read 4,096 tokens?

Linear cost in length, and position embeddings extended to 4,096.

Why use overlapping chunks (stride smaller than the window)?

The price: about twice as many chunks at stride = window / 2.

For IMDB long reviews, why might the last 512 tokens beat the first 512?

Measured: first 512 tokens 90.99%, last 512 92.22%.

When should you aggregate chunks by the most confident chunk rather than the mean?

For overall sentiment, mean of chunk logits (93.07%) and most confident chunk (92.94%) were close here; for “any clause”, max is the natural rule.
