Encoder Variants: RoBERTa to DeBERTa

Within two years of BERT, a family of successors changed its training recipe, its objective and its attention, each claiming a gain on benchmarks. For a practitioner the question is simpler: on my task, with my budget, which one? This chapter answers it the only reliable way, by fine-tuning five of them with one recipe on the same tasks.

DeBERTa-v3 came first on both tasks (95.87 on SST-2, 81.35 on RTE) and ELECTRA a close second, while the original BERT trailed RoBERTa by 8.2 points on the small RTE task. DeBERTa was also the slowest, at 3,977 SST-2 sentences a second against BERT’s 6,429. And on the first attempt DeBERTa scored 49.08%, worse than guessing, because of how its checkpoint loads.

RoBERTa’s training changes

RoBERTa (Liu et al., 2019) kept BERT’s architecture and changed how it was trained: ten times more text (160 GB against 16), larger batches, longer training, dynamic masking (a new mask each time a sequence is seen), no next-sentence prediction, and a byte-level BPE vocabulary of 50,265 pieces instead of WordPiece. Its point was that BERT had been under-trained, and its gains came from the recipe, not a new idea.

DistilBERT and distillation

DistilBERT (Sanh et al., 2019) is a 6-layer student trained to imitate 12-layer BERT: during pre-training its loss combines MLM with matching the teacher’s output distribution and hidden states. It has 66 million parameters instead of 110 million and runs about twice as fast, and the claim is that it keeps most of BERT’s accuracy. Chapter 37 measures what that speed is worth in serving.

ELECTRA’s replaced-token detection

ELECTRA (Clark et al., 2020) replaced MLM with a discriminative task. A small generator fills in masked tokens with plausible guesses; the main model, the discriminator, sees the whole corrupted sequence and predicts for every token whether it is original or replaced. Every position gives a training signal, not just the 15% masked ones, so ELECTRA learns more per unit of compute.

DeBERTa’s disentangled attention

DeBERTa (He et al., 2021) represents each token with two vectors, content and relative position, and computes attention from content-to-content, content-to-position and position-to-content terms separately, instead of adding a position embedding to the input once. Version 3 also adopted ELECTRA-style training. At the base size it is the strongest encoder in most published comparisons, and it is also the slowest of the five here.

On SST-2, with 67,349 training phrases, the five spread over 5.4 points: DeBERTa-v3 95.87, ELECTRA 95.18, RoBERTa 94.27, BERT 92.66, DistilBERT 90.48. On RTE, an entailment task with only 2,490 training pairs, the differences grew to 17.0 points, which is where the better pre-training recipes show: ELECTRA and DeBERTa, trained with replaced-token detection, gained most where labels were scarce. BERT’s three RTE seeds spanned 9.8 points (62.09 to 71.84), more than the gap between some models. At the learning rates their authors suggest, ELECTRA at 5·10−5 scored 80.14 (no change), DistilBERT 63.9, and DeBERTa at 3·10−5 84.6, 3.2 points better than with the shared recipe: one recipe is fair, but not best for every model.

The first DeBERTa run is worth describing. It scored 49.08% on SST-2 and exactly 52.71% on all three RTE seeds, which is a model predicting one class. The cause: transformers 5 loads each checkpoint in its stored precision, DeBERTa-v3’s is stored in fp16, and AdamW updating fp16 weights produced NaNs after the first step (ε = 10−8 underflows to zero in fp16). The other four models are stored in fp32. Loading it with dtype=torch.float32 fixed it. A result that is exactly the majority-class score, or identical across seeds, is a bug until proven otherwise.

Accuracy vs size

Accuracy and speed trade off, but not in a straight line. DistilBERT has 67.0M parameters, ran 2.0× faster than BERT (12,689 against 6,429 SST-2 sentences a second), and lost 2.2 points on SST-2. ELECTRA-base has exactly BERT-base’s size and speed (109.5M, 6,630 a second) and was 2.5 points better on SST-2 and 12.3 on RTE: the same architecture, trained better. DeBERTa-v3 is the biggest (184.4M, most of the extra in its 128,000-piece embedding table) and the slowest (3,977 a second, 1.6× slower than BERT, from its extra attention terms), for the best accuracy. For most tasks ELECTRA-base is the sweet spot here; DeBERTa-v3 when accuracy matters most, DistilBERT when speed does.

Use case: picking an encoder for an insurance-claims classifier

A claims team has 30,000 labelled claim descriptions and a latency budget of 20 ms on one GPU. They fine-tune BERT, RoBERTa, DistilBERT and DeBERTa-v3 with one recipe and three seeds. DeBERTa is most accurate by a point but slowest; DistilBERT is two points behind and twice as fast as BERT. Because the claims queue is large but not latency-critical, they ship DeBERTa for the batch process and DistilBERT for the live triage screen, and they record the seed spread next to every number, because on their smallest claim category two of DeBERTa’s three runs failed to learn it at all.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Comparing published numbers from different recipes: learning rate, epochs and seeds move results as much as the architecture.
  • Fine-tuning a checkpoint in the precision it was stored in: DeBERTa-v3 loads as fp16 in transformers 5 and collapsed to 49.08% until loaded in fp32.
  • Ranking models on one seed of a small dataset: BERT’s RTE seeds ranged from 62.09 to 71.84.
  • Forgetting the tokeniser goes with the model: RoBERTa’s byte-level BPE and DeBERTa’s SentencePiece are not BERT’s WordPiece.
  • Passing token_type_ids to models that have none (RoBERTa, DistilBERT): they are ignored or rejected.
  • Choosing by accuracy alone when latency, memory or cost per text is the real constraint.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What did RoBERTa, DistilBERT, ELECTRA and DeBERTa each change about BERT?

How a strong answer sounds
RoBERTa kept BERT’s architecture and fixed its training: ten times more data, bigger batches, longer, dynamic masking and no next-sentence prediction, showing BERT was under-trained. DistilBERT shrank it: a 6-layer student distilled from BERT, about 40% smaller and here 2.0× faster, for a couple of points. ELECTRA changed the objective: instead of predicting 15% masked tokens, it detects which tokens a small generator replaced, learning from every position. DeBERTa changed the attention: separate content and relative-position vectors with disentangled attention terms, and v3 adds ELECTRA-style training. With one recipe on RTE, which has only 2,490 training examples: BERT 67.63, RoBERTa 75.81, ELECTRA 79.9, DeBERTa-v3 81.35.

Deeper: How would you compare encoders fairly for your task?

How a strong answer sounds
Same data split, same recipe (learning rate, schedule, epochs, batch size, max length), several seeds per model with the spread reported, and, because one recipe is not best for every model, a small learning-rate sweep per model; here DeBERTa gained 3.2 points on RTE at 3·10−5 instead of 2·10−5. Measure what production needs alongside accuracy: latency at the real batch size and sequence length on the real hardware, memory, and cost per million texts. Check each model’s quirks (tokeniser, token_type_ids, the dtype its checkpoint loads in). And sanity-check results: identical scores across seeds, or exactly the majority-class score, mean something broke.

Why does ELECTRA learn more per unit of compute than BERT?

What is distillation in DistilBERT?

The student learns from the teacher’s soft predictions as well as from the data.
