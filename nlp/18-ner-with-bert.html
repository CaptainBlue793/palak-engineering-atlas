Token Classification: NER with BERT

Named-entity recognition with BERT is Chapter 10’s tagging problem with Chapter 17’s model: a linear layer on every token’s final vector instead of only on [CLS]. One complication is new. BERT does not see words; it sees WordPiece sub-words, so “Schwarzenegger” is several tokens, and the word-level labels have to be mapped onto pieces for training and back onto words for scoring.

This chapter fine-tunes BERT on CoNLL-2003 three ways and compares it with the BiLSTM-CRF of Chapter 11. Fine-tuned cased BERT reached 91.38 span F1 on the CoNLL-2003 test set, 5.1 points above Chapter 11’s BiLSTM-CRF and level with the public dslim/bert-base-NER model (91.29). Labelling every sub-word instead of only the first made no difference (91.41); throwing away capitalisation with the uncased model cost 0.9 points.

Sub-word–label alignment

Tokenise each sentence with the words pre-split (is_split_into_words=True), and the tokeniser reports which word each piece came from (word_ids()). Two common ways to label the pieces: give the word’s label to its first piece only and mark the rest −100 so the loss ignores them, or label every piece, continuing a B-X word with I-X on its later pieces. Either way, predictions are read back from each word’s first piece. Special tokens ([CLS], [SEP]) are always −100.

enc = tok(words, is_split_into_words=True, truncation=True)
labels, prev = [], None
for w in enc.word_ids():
    if w is None: labels.append(-100)                          # [CLS], [SEP], padding
    elif w != prev: labels.append(tag2id[tags[w]])            # first piece of a word
    else: labels.append(-100)                                  # later pieces ignored (or I-X, the other scheme)
    prev = w

In CoNLL-2003’s test set 17.8% of words are split into more than one piece by the cased tokeniser, and the split ones are disproportionately names: “Schwarzenegger” becomes Sc ##hwa ##rz ##ene ##gger, “Ahmadinejad” Ahmad ##ine ##ja ##d, “Lyonnais” Lyon ##nai ##s, while frequent names such as “Washington”, “Bundesliga” and “UEFA” stay whole. The uncased tokeniser splits fewer words (13.5%), because lower-casing merges variants into commoner pieces. The lab shows both tokenisers on one sentence.

Token classification heads

AutoModelForTokenClassification is BERT plus dropout and one linear layer from 768 dimensions to the 9 CoNLL tags, applied at every position. Training is per-piece cross-entropy over the labelled positions, three epochs at 3·10−5 with warm-up here. No CRF is needed for good results, though one can be added on top.

Decoding spans

After prediction, each word takes the label of its first piece, and BIO decoding turns word labels into spans exactly as in Chapter 10. Reading from the first piece means the model must put the decision there; labelling every piece in training gives it more training signal per word, but also asks it to label pieces such as “##neg” that carry little evidence on their own. Which works better is measured below.

Three epochs of fine-tuning, 84 seconds each run, took the BiLSTM-CRF’s 86.26 to 91.38. The error counts show where it came from. Against Chapter 10’s BiLSTM tagger on the same test set, cased BERT made 224 type errors instead of 406, 161 boundary errors instead of 361, and missed 68 entities instead of 229; only spurious entities did not improve (156 against 141). Pre-training brought knowledge of which strings are names and what kinds of thing they name, which 14,041 labelled sentences cannot teach.

The two labelling schemes were indistinguishable (91.38 and 91.41, with seeds 0.2–0.4 apart). The uncased model was 0.9 points lower, and its boundary errors rose from 161 to 209: without capitals, “new york times” no longer looks different from “new york”. For English NER, use a cased model.

Errors by entity type

By type (cased, seed 0): PER 96.0, LOC 93.3, ORG 89.8, MISC 79.6. MISC, the catch-all for nationalities, events, titles and products, is hardest for every model, because its members have little in common. ORG and LOC are confused constantly in sports news, where a country name is a team: in “JAPAN GET LUCKY WIN, CHINA IN SURPRISE DEFEAT” the model tagged JAPAN as an organisation (gold: location). The same sentence shows the dataset’s own noise: the gold label for CHINA is PER, a known annotation error first met in P7. All-capital headlines are a weak spot in general: in “RUGBY UNION - CUTTITTA BACK FOR ITALY” the model called the player an organisation and missed ITALY.

Use case: tagging companies and people in financial news

A research desk wants every company and executive named in 10,000 news items a day, linked to its own database. A fine-tuned cased BERT does the tagging; its errors are mostly type confusions between ORG and LOC (“Washington said”: the city or the government?) and boundary errors on long company names (“Bank of America Merrill Lynch”). The team adds a few hundred labelled examples of exactly those patterns, uses a cased model because capitals carry much of the signal, and passes every span through a gazetteer lookup so a predicted ORG that matches a known city is reviewed. They measure span F1 per type each release, because overall F1 hid a drop in executive names when a model was retrained.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Labelling [CLS] and [SEP]: they are not words; mark them −100.
  • Reading predictions from every piece and getting more labels than words: read one per word.
  • Using an uncased model for English NER: it lost 0.9 F1 points here and made 48 more boundary errors.
  • Truncating long sentences without noticing: words past the limit get no prediction; count them.
  • Trusting the gold labels completely: CoNLL-2003 has errors (CHINA tagged PER); read the top errors before chasing the last point of F1.
  • Mixing tokenisers between training and inference: word_ids from a different tokeniser misalign every label.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How do you train BERT for NER when it splits words into sub-words?

How a strong answer sounds
Tokenise with the words already split and is_split_into_words=True, so the tokeniser returns word_ids(): for every piece, the index of the word it came from, or None for [CLS], [SEP] and padding. Give each word’s label to its first piece and −100 to the others and to special tokens, so the loss ignores them; then a token-classification head, one linear layer over BERT’s vectors, is trained with cross-entropy on the labelled pieces. At prediction time read each word’s label from its first piece and decode BIO tags into spans. Labelling every piece with I- continuations is the alternative; on CoNLL-2003 I measured 91.38 with first pieces and 91.41 with every piece, no real difference. Use a cased model: uncased lost 0.9 points.

Deeper: Would you add a CRF on top of BERT for NER?

How a strong answer sounds
Probably not by default. A CRF helped a BiLSTM a lot (Chapter 11: from 84.07 to 86.26, and almost no invalid tag sequences), because the BiLSTM made independent per-token decisions. BERT’s self-attention already conditions every token on the whole sentence, so its predictions are far more consistent; plain fine-tuned BERT here reached 91.38, and reported gains from adding a CRF to BERT are usually small. I would first check how many invalid sequences (an I- tag after O) the model produces; if there are many, add constrained decoding, which forbids invalid transitions at no training cost, or a CRF layer if the domain has long, structured entities and the extra training time is acceptable.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • NER with BERT is a linear head on every piece, with word labels mapped to pieces via word_ids().
  • Measured: fine-tuned cased BERT 91.38 span F1 on CoNLL-2003, against 86.26 for the BiLSTM-CRF; most of the gain is fewer missed entities and type errors.
  • Measured: first-piece and every-piece labelling scored the same (91.38 vs 91.41).
  • Measured: the uncased model lost 0.9 points; capitals carry real signal for names.
  • Report span F1 by entity type; MISC and ORG are where models and annotators disagree most.
