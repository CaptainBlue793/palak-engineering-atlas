Machine Translation & BLEU

Machine translation was the task that drove most of the ideas of the last three chapters, and it raised a problem that every generation task now shares: there are many correct outputs, so how do you score one? BLEU, a count of n-grams shared with a human reference, has been the answer for twenty years, and it is wrong in ways worth knowing exactly.

This chapter writes BLEU from scratch, checks it against the standard implementation, compares it with chrF and a learned metric on our translators and a pretrained one, and then perturbs reference translations to see what each metric notices. Our BLEU matched sacrebleu’s to two decimals (35.08). Against the original references, a version with “not” inserted into 366 of them still scored 91.8 BLEU, about the same as one with harmless synonym swaps (91.4); COMET, a learned metric, separated them (90.25 against 95.0).

Parallel corpora

A translation model learns from parallel text: the same content in two languages, aligned sentence by sentence. Sources include parliament proceedings (Europarl), the United Nations, subtitles, software localisation files and crawled multilingual websites, which are filtered for misaligned or machine-translated pairs. Multi30k, used here, has 29,000 English image captions translated into German by professionals: clean but small and narrow, so a model trained on it knows about dogs, bikes and people in red shirts, and little else.

Our vocabularies, words seen at least twice in training, have 7,882 German and 5,898 English types; everything else becomes <unk>. That is why 192 of the attention model’s 1,000 test translations contain an <unk>. The comparison system, Helsinki-NLP’s Marian model, is a transformer trained on the OPUS collection, many millions of sentence pairs, with a subword vocabulary that has no unknown words.

BLEU from scratch

BLEU (Papineni et al., 2002) compares a system’s output with one or more references, over the whole test set at once. For n = 1 to 4 it computes the modified n-gram precision: of the system’s n-grams, how many also appear in the reference, with each reference n-gram usable only as many times as it occurs there (“clipping”, so “the the the the” does not score). The four precisions are combined by a geometric mean, and a brevity penalty exp(1 − r/c), applied when the output is shorter than the references, stops a system from scoring well by writing only the words it is sure of.

for n in range(1, 5):
    hn, rn = ngrams(hyp, n), ngrams(ref, n)
    num[n] += sum(min(c, rn[g]) for g, c in hn.items())       # clipped matches
    den[n] += max(0, len(hyp) - n + 1)
bp = 1 if c > r else math.exp(1 - r / c)                              # corpus-level lengths
bleu = 100 * bp * math.exp(sum(math.log(num[n] / den[n]) for n in range(1, 5)) / 4)

Run over the attention model’s 1,000 test translations, this from-scratch BLEU gave 35.08, and sacrebleu, the reference implementation, gave 35.08 on the same tokens. The parts: n-gram precisions of 69.0%, 43.9%, 29.1% and 19.8% for n = 1 to 4, and a brevity penalty of 0.965 because the outputs were slightly shorter than the references. The settings matter as much as the formula: the same translations scored with sacrebleu’s default tokeniser, against the original mixed-case references, give 29.2, 5.9 points lower, simply because our output is lower-cased.

chrF and learned metrics

chrF (Popović, 2015) computes an F-score over character n-grams (up to 6) instead of words, so “bike” and “bikes” share most of their credit and morphology-rich languages are scored more fairly. Learned metrics such as COMET (Rei et al., 2020) are neural models trained to predict human quality ratings from the source, the system output and the reference; they recognise paraphrases that share no words, but they are slower, opaque, and only as good as the human ratings they were trained on.

The three metrics agree on the order: no attention, then attention, then Marian. Marian was never trained on Multi30k and still beats a model trained on nothing else, by 5.4 BLEU, because it has seen vastly more German and has no unknown words; it translated the 1,000 sentences in 6.3 s. Two details keep the comparison honest. Marian’s BLEU is computed on the same lower-cased tokens as ours (40.47; with sacrebleu’s defaults on its cased output it is 39.7). Its chrF is computed on cased text, ours on lower-cased, which can only count against Marian. COMET spreads the systems differently from BLEU (62.47, 70.04 and 86.93): attention adds 7.6 and Marian 16.9 more, where BLEU had 12.7 and 5.4. By COMET, the pretrained model’s lead is the bigger step. COMET reads the German source too, and lower-cased, tokenised output with <unk> tokens is out of the style it was trained on, which may cost our systems a little.

Human vs automatic scores

To see what each metric notices, every reference was perturbed and scored against the original as if it were a system output:

The two middle rows are the lesson. Synonym swaps touched 419 references and kept their meaning; inserting “not” touched 366 and reversed it. BLEU scored them almost the same (91.4 and 91.8), and chrF scored the reversed version higher (97.6 against 94.7), because adding a short word preserves more characters than swapping nouns. COMET put the negated references at 90.25 and the synonym swaps at 95.0: it noticed which change mattered. COMET is not even 100 for an identical copy (96.03): it is a regression model, not a match count, so its scores are only meaningful relative to each other. Shuffled word order kept every unigram yet scored 3.8 BLEU but 58.4 chrF: chrF’s character n-grams survive inside words even when the sentence is nonsense.

Common translation errors

Reading the test translations side by side shows the typical failures of small models, and one of the references:

  • Hallucination. “Ein hell gekleideter Mann fotografiert …” (a brightly dressed man photographs a group of men …) came out of our attention model as “a man dressed in a blue uniform is taking a group of men in dark suits … and a woman wearing a bright green dress”: fluent, plausible for the domain, and partly invented.
  • Unknown words. 192 of its 1,000 translations contain <unk> (“a <unk> terrier” for “Boston Terrier”); Marian’s subword vocabulary produced none.
  • Repetition. “… in front of a white fence in front of a white fence.”: the decoder came back to words it had already translated.
  • Lost words. “Schneemobilen” (snowmobiles) became “snow”, and “bricht ein Brett” (breaks a board) became “is holding a board”.
  • Wrong references. The reference for “Ein Mädchen … bricht ein Brett mit einem Tritt” says “breaking a stick”, and the first reference says “starring at” for “staring at”. Marian’s correct “breaks a board” loses BLEU for being right. Test sets contain mistakes, and every metric that compares with a reference inherits them.

Use case: choosing a translation vendor for product listings

An online marketplace compares three translation engines for German product listings. Engine A scores 2 BLEU points above B, which the team at first takes as decisive. A small human evaluation on 200 listings, rated by bilingual staff for adequacy and fluency, reverses the order: A copies English product terms that German shoppers do not search for, which BLEU rewards because the references kept some of them, while B uses the German terms. They switch to reporting COMET plus a 200-sentence human check per release, and keep BLEU only as a regression alarm. A drop of five points on the same test set still means something broke.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Comparing BLEU computed with different tokenisation: report sacrebleu’s signature; our own tokenisation and sacrebleu’s defaults gave different numbers for the same output.
  • Reading equal BLEU as equal meaning: references with “not” inserted scored 91.8 BLEU, the same as harmless synonym swaps (91.4).
  • Comparing scores under different casing or tokenisation: the same translations scored 35.08 on our lower-cased tokens and 29.2 with sacrebleu’s defaults against cased references.
  • Reporting sentence-level BLEU as if it were reliable: BLEU is designed for corpora; on one sentence it is mostly noise.
  • Testing on the training domain only: a Multi30k model has never seen a contract or a recipe.
  • Trusting a learned metric blindly: it inherits the biases of its training ratings; spot-check with humans.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How does BLEU work, and what are its main weaknesses?

How a strong answer sounds
BLEU compares a system’s output with human references over a whole test set. For n from 1 to 4 it counts how many of the output’s n-grams appear in the reference, clipped so a repeated word only counts as often as the reference has it, takes the geometric mean of those four precisions, and multiplies by a brevity penalty so short outputs cannot win on precision. It is cheap, language-independent and correlates with quality at the corpus level. Its weaknesses: it only rewards exact overlap, so paraphrases score badly; it barely notices a single meaning-changing word, and when I inserted “not” into references they still scored 91.8; it depends on tokenisation and casing (35.08 against 29.2 for the same output); it is unreliable for single sentences; and it inherits errors in the references.

Deeper: How would you evaluate a new translation system before deploying it?

How a strong answer sounds
On a held-out test set from the real domain, not just a public benchmark, I would report several metrics with their exact settings: BLEU with the sacrebleu signature, chrF, and a learned metric such as COMET, and compare against a strong existing system, which here was a pretrained Marian model at 40.47 BLEU against our 35.08. Then human evaluation on a few hundred sentences, rating adequacy and fluency blind to the system, and targeted checks for the failures that matter in the product: negation, numbers, names, terminology, and hallucination on long inputs. I would also spot-check the references themselves, because here at least two of the first eight references contained errors. Deployment would start with a shadow or A/B test that measures what users actually do, such as edits to the translation or support contacts.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • BLEU is clipped n-gram precision (n = 1–4), geometric mean, times a brevity penalty, computed over a whole test set.
  • Measured: our BLEU and sacrebleu’s agreed (35.08); the same output scored 29.2 under different casing and tokenisation, so always state the settings.
  • Measured: a pretrained Marian model reached 40.47 BLEU against 35.08 for our attention model and 22.36 without attention; chrF and COMET agreed on the order.
  • Measured: references with “not” inserted scored 91.8 BLEU and 97.6 chrF, as high as meaning-preserving synonym swaps; COMET gave 90.25 against 95.0.
  • Report the metric’s exact settings, and check important comparisons with people.

Check your understanding

What does “clipping” in BLEU’s n-gram precision prevent?

“the the the the” against “the cat” gets 1 of 4 unigrams, not 4 of 4.

What is the brevity penalty for?

Without it, a system writing only its few most confident words would score high precision. Here the attention model’s outputs were slightly short, giving a penalty of 0.965.

Inserting “not” after the first “is” or “are” of each reference barely lowered BLEU. What does that show?

Measured: with “not” inserted into 366 of the 1,000 references, BLEU was 91.8, the same as for synonym swaps that keep the meaning (91.4).

Why does chrF give partial credit for “bikes” against “bike”?

That makes chrF fairer for languages with rich inflection.

What does a learned metric like COMET use that BLEU does not?

It is also slower and less transparent; neither replaces human checks for important decisions.
