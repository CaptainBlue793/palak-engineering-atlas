Encoders vs LLMs: Choosing the Tool

A prompted large language model can do almost any text task with no training data at all. A small fine-tuned encoder needs labels, does one task, and runs hundreds of times faster on cheaper hardware. Neither is “better”: the right choice depends on how many labels you have, how many texts you must process, how fast, and how much a mistake costs.

This chapter puts both on the same three tasks and measures accuracy and throughput on the same machine. With all the labels, fine-tuned DistilBERT won AG News (94.45%) by ten points and tweet emotion (80.23%) by three, but on SST-2 a 1.5B-parameter LLM given five examples beat it (92.66% against 90.25%). On speed there was no contest: DistilBERT classified about 8,002 SST-2 sentences a second on the GPU; qwen3:14b through Ollama about 8.33.

  • Compare a fine-tuned encoder with zero- and few-shot LLMs on identical test sets
  • Measure throughput and convert it into cost per million texts
  • Recognise tasks where label data beats model size, and the reverse
  • Use an LLM to bootstrap labels for a cheaper model
  • Make the choice explicit: a decision table, not a habit

The contenders

  • Fine-tuned DistilBERT (66M parameters): two epochs on each task’s full training set.
  • Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct (1.5B): an instruction prompt naming the labels, scored by the logit of each label’s first token at the answer position, zero-shot and with five demonstrations.
  • qwen3:14b through Ollama (14B, 4-bit): the same instruction, greedy generation of up to six tokens, answer parsed from the text, 300 test examples per task.

Scoring label tokens (rather than generating) gives the small LLM its best case: it cannot answer off-list. The 14B model generates, as it would in production, so its answers can miss the label set.

With its full training set (67,349 to 120,000 labels), the fine-tuned encoder was clearly best on topic and emotion classification: 94.45% on AG News against 84.0% for the 14B model and 78.3% for the 1.5B model with five examples, and 80.23% on tweet emotion against 76.92% and 68.33%. Sentiment was the exception: Qwen2.5-1.5B with five demonstrations scored 92.66% against DistilBERT’s 90.25%, a task the LLM’s pre-training and instruction tuning cover well. Five demonstrations helped the small LLM on every task, by 4.4, 7.3 and 9.4 points, and cost 2.4–4.2× the time per text, because every prompt now carries five examples.

The 14B model was not reliably better than the 1.5B one. It generated its answer, and on SST-2 it answered off the label set 31 times out of 300 (“mixed” 27 times), each counted as an error; on tweet emotion 23 answers missed the labels (“sad”, “rage”). Scoring label tokens, as for the 1.5B model, avoids that problem entirely, which is a fair advantage of the method, not the model. Note that the 14B figures are on the first 300 test examples only, so they carry a few points of sampling error.

Measured: speed and cost

Measured on one GPU, DistilBERT classified 8,002 SST-2 sentences a second, 4,654 AG News articles, and 9,676 tweets; on 8 CPU threads, 140.1 sentences a second. Qwen2.5-1.5B managed 212 a second zero-shot and 89 with five examples; qwen3:14b, generating one answer at a time through Ollama, about 8.33. In GPU-hours per million texts that is 0.03 for DistilBERT, 1.31–7.49 for the 1.5B model and 26.53–33.35 for the 14B model: the encoder is about 961 times cheaper than the 14B LLM on the same hardware. Training the encoder took 77 s on SST-2 and 211 s on AG News, a one-off cost recovered within the first few thousand texts. The 14B timing is for sequential requests; a batched LLM server would be several times faster, but the gap is orders of magnitude, not a constant factor.

Use case: routing 3 million support messages a day

A telecom routes incoming messages to 12 teams. A prototype prompted a hosted LLM and worked well; the bill for 3 million messages a day did not. The team runs the LLM over 20,000 historical messages to produce labels, has agents correct a 2,000-message sample (the LLM was wrong on about one in eight), fine-tunes a DistilBERT on the corrected labels, and serves it on two CPU machines. Accuracy on a held-out human-labelled set ends within a point of the LLM, and messages the encoder is unsure of, about 5%, still go to the LLM.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Assuming a bigger LLM is a better classifier: qwen3:14b scored 87.0% on SST-2, below the 1.5B model with five examples (92.66%), partly from 31 off-list answers.
  • Dismissing LLMs for classification without testing: on SST-2 five examples took a 1.5B model past a fine-tuned DistilBERT.
  • Ignoring cost per text: the 14B model needed about 33.35 GPU-hours per million SST-2 sentences; DistilBERT 0.03.
  • Comparing on different test sets: evaluate every option on the same examples, scored the same way.
  • Forgetting unparsed answers: a generating LLM sometimes answers off the label list; count those as errors and report how many.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: When would you fine-tune a small encoder instead of prompting an LLM?

How a strong answer sounds
When there are enough labels (hundreds to thousands per class), the label set is stable, and volume or latency matter. A fine-tuned encoder is then usually as accurate or more so on the specific task and hundreds of times cheaper: on AG News a fine-tuned DistilBERT scored 94.45% against 84.0% for qwen3:14b, and classified about 4,654 articles a second on one GPU against about 10.47 for the LLM. I would reach for an LLM when there are no labels yet, the labels change often, the output is open-ended, or the volume is small enough that the cost does not matter, and I would test both, because on SST-2 a 1.5B LLM with five examples beat the fine-tuned encoder (92.66% vs 90.25%).

Deeper: You have no labels and must classify 10 million documents a day. Plan it.

How a strong answer sounds
Use an LLM to create the labels and an encoder to serve. Write a clear label guide; have an LLM label a sample of, say, 20,000 documents, scoring label tokens or constraining the output to the label set so every answer parses; have people correct a few thousand of those to measure and fix the LLM’s errors, and label a separate gold test set by hand. Fine-tune a small encoder on the corrected labels and check it against the LLM on the gold set. At ten million documents a day, the cost difference decides it: here the encoder needed about 0.03 GPU-hours per million texts and the 14B LLM about 33.35. Route the encoder’s least confident few percent to the LLM or to people, log disagreements as new training data, and re-label and retrain as drift appears.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Measured: with full labels, DistilBERT led on AG News (94.45%) and tweet emotion (80.23%); on SST-2 a 5-shot 1.5B LLM led (92.66% vs 90.25%).
  • Measured: five demonstrations added 4.4–9.4 points to the 1.5B LLM at 2.4–4.2× the time per text.
  • Measured: GPU-hours per million SST-2 texts: DistilBERT 0.03, Qwen2.5-1.5B 1.31, qwen3:14b 33.35.
  • Convert throughput into cost per million texts before choosing.
  • An LLM is often the best way to create labels for a small model.

Check your understanding

Why is scoring label tokens a best case for a small LLM classifier?

Generated answers have to be parsed, and some will not match a label.

What decides “GPU-hours per million texts”?

Measured for DistilBERT on SST-2: 8,002 a second → 10⁶ ÷ 8,002 ÷ 3,600 ≈ 0.03 GPU-hours.

When is a prompted LLM usually the right choice for classification?

Its advantage is needing no training; its cost grows with every text.

What is the “LLM labels, encoder serves” pattern?

LLM-level quality on the task at encoder cost, if the labels are checked.
