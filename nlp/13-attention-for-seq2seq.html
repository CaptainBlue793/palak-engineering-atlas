Attention for Seq2Seq

Chapter 12’s translator had to squeeze a whole German sentence into one vector before writing a word of English. Attention (Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio, 2014) removes the squeeze: at every output step the decoder looks back at all of the encoder’s states, scores how relevant each is right now, and reads a weighted average of them. The weights form a soft alignment between output and input words, learned with no alignment labels at all.

This chapter adds two kinds of attention to the same translator, trains them on the same Multi30k data, and measures what changed. Additive attention lifted test BLEU from 22.36 to 35.08, more than ten times what beam search added in Chapter 12, and the gain grew with sentence length: on sentences of 20 words or more the score went from 11.58 to 24.92.

Additive attention

Keep every encoder state h1 … hn (one per source word, each a forward and backward GRU state concatenated). At decoder step t, with decoder state s, compute a score for every source position, turn the scores into weights with a softmax, and take the weighted average as the context vector ct, which is fed to the decoder alongside the previous word:

score(s, hi) = v · tanh(W hi + U s),   αi = softmaxi(score),   ct = Σi αi hi

Bahdanau’s additive score uses a small feed-forward network, so the query and the keys can live in different spaces. The context vector changes at every step, so the decoder never has to carry the whole source in its state.

Multiplicative attention

Luong, Pham and Manning (2015) simplified the score to a dot product, score(s, hi) = s · W hi (“general”), or s · hi when the sizes match (“dot”). It is cheaper, a single matrix product for all positions at once, and it is the form the transformer kept, scaled by 1/√d (Chapter 15). The lab lets you move a query and watch both kinds of score and the resulting weights.

if attn == "additive": sc = s.va(torch.tanh(s.Wa(enc_out) + s.Ua(h)[:, None])).squeeze(-1)
else:                   sc = (s.Wd(enc_out) * h[:, None]).sum(-1)             # Luong "general"
a = sc.masked_fill(~src_mask, -1e9).softmax(-1)                         # never attend to padding
ctx = (a[..., None] * enc_out).sum(1)                                    # this step's view of the source
h = s.dec(torch.cat([s.et(y_prev), ctx], -1), h)

Additive attention added 12.7 BLEU over the same translator without it. By source length the gain was 10.0 points on sentences under 10 words and 16.5 on 15–19-word ones, and on the 53 sentences of 20 words or more it more than doubled the score (11.58 → 24.92, ×2.2 against ×1.4 on the shortest). Up to 20 words the attention model’s BLEU barely moves with length (35.25, 36.25, 36.64); without attention it falls steadily. The longest bucket is hard for every model, and with 53 sentences its numbers carry more noise than the others.

Two caveats keep this honest. The attention models also have more parameters (20.5M against 12.1M, because the decoder input and output layers take the context vector), so not all of the gain is attention as such. And multiplicative attention, the cheaper form, reached only 29.87. Its validation BLEU was still climbing at the end (28.34 → 29.37 between epochs 8 and 12) while the additive model had levelled off by epoch 8 (36.19, 35.98), so with one seed and 12 epochs this says dot-product attention learnt more slowly here, not that it is worse. Luong et al. (2015) reported their multiplicative scores working as well as or better than the additive one.

Alignment maps

Attention weights, plotted as a grid of output words against source words, form a soft alignment learnt without any alignment labels. On the four test sentences in the lab the weights mostly run down the diagonal, as German and English word order mostly agree: “fünf” → “five” with 96% of the weight, “schwamm” → “sponge” with 98%. The off-diagonal cases are the interesting ones:

  • Right place, wrong word. In sentence 2, while writing “is catching” the model attends to “nicht” (does not), the word that should have produced “missing”. It looked at the negation and dropped it: attention shows where the model read, not whether it understood.
  • Looking twice. In sentence 3 the output says “apron apron”: at the second “apron” the weight is still 59% on “schürze”, and the verb “putzt” (cleans), attended one step later, never makes it into the output.
  • Right place, missing word. In sentence 1 “schneemobilen” is attended correctly but comes out as “snow”: the rare compound is outside what the model learnt, which attention cannot fix.
  • Looking one word ahead. Articles often attend to the next noun (“a” → “pinken”), a common pattern: the decoder needs the noun’s gender and number to choose the article.

From attention to transformers

Attention in a seq2seq model is cross-attention: decoder queries, encoder keys and values. The transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017, Chapter 15) made three moves. It applied attention within a sequence (self-attention), so every word attends to every other word in the same sentence; it used several attention heads in parallel; and it removed the recurrence entirely, so all positions are computed at once and the slow token-by-token loop of Chapter 9 disappears. The dot-product score of this chapter, divided by √d, is the score transformers use today.

Use case: auditing a translator for a legal team

A law firm translates German contracts with an attention-based model and wants evidence for where each English clause came from. The alignment maps help: a translated clause whose attention points at the wrong German clause, or at nothing in particular, is flagged for a human. The team learns two cautions from testing. Attention weights are a useful pointer but not a guarantee: the model sometimes attends correctly and still mistranslates a negation, and sometimes attends diffusely and translates correctly. And they check flagged clauses against the source with a separate word-alignment tool rather than trusting attention alone, a theme Chapter 33 returns to.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Forgetting the source padding mask in attention: the decoder attends to padding and translations of short sentences degrade.
  • Reading attention weights as explanations: they show where the model looked, not why it chose a word.
  • Crediting attention with all of a gain: here the attention models had 20.5M parameters against 12.1M; compare at matched size where you can.
  • Unscaled dot products with large vectors: scores grow with dimension and the softmax saturates; transformers divide by √d.
  • Declaring one attention form better from one short run: multiplicative attention scored 29.87 against additive’s 35.08, but it was still improving at epoch 12.
  • Comparing models on average BLEU only: break it down by length; attention’s gain is concentrated where the bottleneck bites.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What problem did attention solve in sequence-to-sequence models?

How a strong answer sounds
Without attention the encoder squeezes the whole source into one fixed vector, and the decoder must write every word from it; long sentences overflow it. Here a GRU translator without attention scored 24.5 BLEU on 10–14-word sentences and 11.58 on 20-plus. Attention keeps all the encoder’s states and, at every output step, scores each one against the decoder’s current state, turns the scores into weights with a softmax, and feeds the weighted average in as a fresh context. The decoder no longer has to remember the source; it looks it up. On the same data that lifted BLEU from 22.36 to 35.08, and the weights double as a soft word alignment learnt without labels.

Deeper: Compare additive and multiplicative attention. Why did transformers use scaled dot products?

How a strong answer sounds
Additive (Bahdanau) scores each encoder state with a small feed-forward network, v·tanh(W h + U s), so query and keys can have different sizes and the scorer can learn non-linear matches. Multiplicative (Luong) scores with a bilinear form s·W h, or a plain dot product: one matrix multiplication for all positions, much cheaper and easy to batch. In my measurement additive reached 35.08 BLEU and multiplicative 29.87, but the dot model was still improving, so I would not read much into it. Transformers use dot products because they are fast on GPUs at scale, and they divide by √d because the dot product of two random d-dimensional vectors has variance proportional to d: without scaling, scores in high dimensions are large, the softmax saturates to one-hot weights, and its gradients vanish.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Attention lets the decoder read a fresh weighted average of all encoder states at every step.
  • Measured: additive attention lifted Multi30k test BLEU from 22.36 to 35.08, and on 20-plus-word sentences from 11.58 to 24.92.
  • Measured: multiplicative attention reached 29.87 in the same 12 epochs and was still improving; it is cheaper and is the form transformers use, scaled by 1/√d.
  • Attention weights form soft alignments learned without labels; useful pointers, not explanations.
  • Self-attention plus multiple heads minus recurrence is the transformer.
