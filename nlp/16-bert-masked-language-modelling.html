BERT & Masked Language Modelling

Chapter 15’s encoder learned everything from 67,000 labelled phrases. BERT (Devlin et al., 2018) learns first from unlabelled text, billions of words of it, with a simple game: hide 15% of the tokens and predict them from both sides. To win, the encoder has to learn grammar, word meaning, and a surprising amount about the world. Then it is fine-tuned on the small labelled task.

This chapter plays the game at small scale, pre-training the same 4-layer encoder on WikiText-103 and measuring what it buys, then probes the real BERT. Thirty thousand steps on 62M tokens of Wikipedia took 25 minutes and taught the small model to fill 40.6% of masked pieces correctly. It helped a classifier trained on 2,000 labelled sentences by 2.8 points, and not at all one trained on all 67,349. The real BERT, pre-trained on 3.3 billion words, answered 55% of 20 simple fact questions with its first guess, and still completed “a bird cannot [MASK].” with “fly”.

Masked language modelling

A left-to-right language model (Chapters 5 and 9) predicts each token from the ones before it, so its representation of a word never sees the words after. BERT’s masked language model (MLM) instead picks 15% of the tokens in each sequence and asks the encoder to predict them from everything else. Of the chosen tokens, 80% are replaced by [MASK], 10% by a random token and 10% left unchanged. The last two matter because [MASK] never appears when the model is fine-tuned or used, so the model must also produce good vectors for real, possibly wrong, tokens. Try it in the lab.

prob = torch.full(x.shape, 0.15); prob[special] = 0; chosen = torch.bernoulli(prob).bool()
labels = x.clone(); labels[~chosen] = -100                     # loss only on chosen positions
r = torch.rand(x.shape)
x[chosen & (r < 0.8)] = MASK                                         # 80% → [MASK]
x[chosen & (r >= 0.8) & (r < 0.9)] = random_token()                # 10% → random, 10% unchanged
loss = F.cross_entropy(mlm_head(encoder(x)).view(-1, V), labels.view(-1), ignore_index=-100)

Next-sentence prediction and its removal

The original BERT had a second objective: given two segments, predict whether the second really followed the first in the corpus or was a random segment, through the [CLS] vector. The idea was to teach relations between sentences for tasks like question answering and entailment. RoBERTa (Liu et al., 2019) tested removing it and found results equal or better without it, provided training used long contiguous text; ALBERT replaced it with a harder sentence-order task. Most encoders since use MLM alone, which is what this chapter trains (Chapter 21 compares the variants).

WordPiece

BERT’s vocabulary is 30,522 WordPiece units (Chapter 2): whole common words, word pieces marked ##, and characters, so it can spell any word. MLM masks individual pieces, which made some predictions too easy (“phil ##har [MASK] ##ic”); BERT’s later “whole word masking” version masks all pieces of a word together. The small model here uses BERT’s tokeniser unchanged, so its vocabulary and the real BERT’s are identical, and the comparison below is between what the two learned, not how they cut text.

Pre-training a small BERT

The Chapter 15 encoder, with an MLM head whose output layer shares the token-embedding matrix, was pre-trained for 30,000 steps of 128 blocks of 128 tokens: 492M tokens, about 8 passes over 62.5M WordPiece tokens of WikiText-103, in 25 minutes. Masked accuracy on held-out blocks rose from 8.1% at step 500 to 15.1% at 3,000, 34.4% at 12,000 and 40.6% at the end, and was still creeping up (40.3 → 40.6 over the last 3,000 steps).

One thing went wrong at the start, and it is worth knowing. At step 500 the loss was 22.101, above ln 30,522 ≈ 10.3, the loss of guessing uniformly. The output layer is tied to the embedding matrix, and PyTorch initialises nn.Embedding from a standard normal, so the first logits had a standard deviation around 16 and the softmax was confidently wrong about everything. Training pulled it back to 6.637 by step 3,000, but BERT avoids the problem by initialising embeddings with a standard deviation of 0.02; a from-scratch MLM should do the same.

Our pre-training helped where labels were scarce: +1.6 points with 500 phrases, +2.8 with 2,000 and +1.6 with 10,000, with three seeds each and seed standard deviations of 0.5 to 2.5 points, so the gains are real but modest. With all 67,349 labelled phrases it made no difference (78.98 against 78.82). That is the textbook shape, pre-training substitutes for labels, but at a tiny scale: real BERT-style models fine-tuned on SST-2 reach about 91–93%. The gap has three causes, all of size. Our model has 11M parameters against BERT-base’s 110M; it read about 0.5 billion tokens against BERT’s 3.3 billion words for roughly 40 passes; and it still predicts only 40.6% of masked pieces. Our fill-mask results below show what that much pre-training does and does not learn.

What BERT knows

Fill-mask probes ask a pre-trained model to complete sentences, which shows what the MLM objective put into it. Our 4-layer model mostly learnt to copy: “paris is the capital of [MASK].” → “paris” (42%), “the cat sat on the [MASK].” → “cat” (79%). Repeating a word from the context is a cheap way to lower the MLM loss, and with this little training it is much of what the model does. Its most likely completion of “water boils at 100 degrees [MASK].” is “@”, an artefact of the raw WikiText format, which writes hyphens and thousands separators as “ @-@ ” and “ @,@ ”.

The real BERT answered 55% of 20 simple fact prompts with its first guess and had the answer in its top 10 for 75% (the LAMA style of probe, Petroni et al., 2019). Its misses are instructive: “the capital of canada is ___.” → “montreal”, “dante was born in ___.” → “rome”, “einstein was born in ___.” → “london”, “the currency of india is the ___.” → “indian”, “the largest planet in the solar system is ___.” → “pluto”. It completed “paris is the capital of [MASK].” with “france” at 95%, but “i had a [MASK] time at the party, it was awful.” with “great” (50%), ignoring the clause after the blank. Negation barely registered: “a bird can [MASK].” → “fly” 64%, “a bird cannot [MASK].” → “fly” 56% (Kassner and Schütze, 2020, found the same). And the corpus’s associations come along: “the nurse said that [MASK] would be late.” → “she” 48%, “he” 18%; for “the doctor”, “he” 34% and “she” 30%. Chapter 31 measures such bias properly.

Use case: domain-adaptive pre-training for legal text

A legal-tech company fine-tunes BERT to classify contract clauses and gets good but not great results: “consideration”, “assignment” and “warranty” mean something specific in contracts that general text does not teach. They continue MLM pre-training of BERT on 2 GB of unlabelled contracts for a day on one GPU, then fine-tune exactly as before, and the clause classifier improves by several points, most on the rare clause types with few labels. This is the pattern measured in this chapter: pre-training helps most where labels are scarce, and it can be applied a second time, on in-domain text, before fine-tuning.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Masking only with [MASK]: the model never sees [MASK] after pre-training; the 10% random and 10% unchanged cases keep its vectors useful for real tokens.
  • Initialising tied embeddings at PyTorch’s default scale: our first MLM loss was 22.101, worse than uniform guessing (10.3); use a small standard deviation such as 0.02.
  • Expecting small-scale pre-training to help with plenty of labels: ours added +2.8 points at 2,000 labelled phrases and nothing at 67,349.
  • Treating fill-mask as a knowledge base: BERT got 55% of simple facts right at the top rank here, said “pluto” for the largest planet and completed “a bird cannot [MASK].” with “fly”.
  • Masking special tokens ([CLS], [SEP], padding): they should never be prediction targets.
  • Forgetting to tie or train the output layer: an MLM head over 30,522 pieces is a large share of a small model’s parameters.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How is BERT pre-trained, and why does that help a classifier trained later?

How a strong answer sounds
BERT is a transformer encoder pre-trained on billions of words of unlabelled text with masked language modelling: 15% of tokens are chosen, most replaced by [MASK], and the model predicts them from the words on both sides. To do that well it has to learn word meanings, syntax and a fair amount about the world, so its vectors for each token encode that context. Fine-tuning then adds a small head and trains on a labelled task, starting from those vectors instead of from random ones, so far fewer labels are needed. I measured this at small scale: a 4-layer encoder pre-trained for 25 minutes on Wikipedia text gained 2.8 points on SST-2 with 2,000 labels and nothing with all 67,349, while the full-size, fully pre-trained DistilBERT reaches 91%. Pre-training helps most when labels are scarce, and its value grows with model and corpus size.

Deeper: Why does BERT replace only 80% of the chosen tokens with [MASK]?

How a strong answer sounds
Because [MASK] only exists during pre-training. If every chosen token became [MASK], the model would learn to produce good predictions only at [MASK] positions and would never need useful vectors for ordinary tokens, which is all it sees when fine-tuned. Replacing 10% with a random token forces it to check every token against its context, since any word might be wrong, and leaving 10% unchanged teaches it that a real word can be the answer, biasing its representation of observed words towards their true identity. The loss is computed on all chosen positions, including the unchanged ones. Later models such as ELECTRA removed the mismatch differently, by training a discriminator to spot replaced tokens at every position.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • MLM hides 15% of tokens (80% [MASK], 10% random, 10% unchanged) and predicts them from both sides.
  • Next-sentence prediction was dropped by later models with no loss; MLM alone is enough.
  • Measured: 25 minutes of MLM on 62M Wikipedia tokens reached 40.6% masked accuracy with an 11M-parameter encoder.
  • Measured: that pre-training added +1.6, +2.8 and +1.6 points with 500, 2,000 and 10,000 labels, and nothing with all 67,349.
  • Measured: bert-base got 55% of 20 fact prompts at the top rank, kept “fly” after “cannot”, and links nurses with “she”: fill-mask shows associations, not reasoning.
