Bias & Fairness in Text Models

A text classifier learns whatever correlations its training data holds. If identity words such as “gay” or “Muslim” appear mostly in abusive training examples, a toxicity model learns that the word itself is toxic and flags “I am a proud gay man” as abuse. The harm lands on the people the model was meant to protect. These failures are measurable, and some mitigations work, at a price that should be measured too.

This chapter measures bias in sentiment and toxicity models with templates, finds its source in the training data, and tests two fixes. Asked to score “I am a gay person.”, the Jigsaw toxicity model gave a probability of 0.29, against 0.001 for “I am a straight person.”, and it flagged non-toxic sentences mentioning gay or Black identity outright. In the offensive-tweet training data, 58.3% of tweets mentioning “gay” were labelled offensive, against 33.1% overall. Adding counter-examples changed nothing measurable; blinding identity terms removed every false positive at no cost in accuracy.

  • Measure bias with templates that differ only in a name or identity term
  • Distinguish fairness criteria: demographic parity, equal opportunity, equalised odds
  • Trace a model’s bias to its training data
  • Apply data augmentation and term blinding, and measure what they cost
  • Report fairness results honestly: what was tested, and what was not

What “fair” means

There is no single definition. For a classifier that flags content and two groups A and B, three common criteria:

  • Demographic parity: both groups are flagged at the same rate.
  • Equal opportunity: among truly positive items (truly toxic), both groups are caught at the same rate: equal true-positive rates.
  • Equalised odds: equal true-positive and false-positive rates. For toxicity the false-positive rate is often what matters most: innocent posts by or about a group wrongly removed.

When the groups have different base rates, a classifier generally cannot satisfy all of these at once (Kleinberg et al., 2016; Chouldechova, 2017). The lab’s first view lets you try: one classifier, two groups with different score distributions, a threshold per group.

Measured: names in sentiment templates

Eight sentiment templates (“{} found the film moving.”, “{} made me feel angry.” …), each filled with names associated with different genders and origins. For DistilBERT the group averages of P(positive) ranged from 0.7154 (West African origin) to 0.7451 (South Asian origin), small differences, but on a single template two groups could differ by up to 0.1779, and individual names ranged from 0.6456 (“Abena”) to 0.7584 (“Grace”) on average for identical sentences. A model whose output moves with the name in an otherwise identical sentence has learnt something about names, and it is not sentiment.

TF-IDF behaved differently for a revealing reason. Most non-European names here never appear in SST-2’s movie reviews, so the model has no weight for them and every such group scored exactly 0.6518; European names that do appear (often as actors’ or characters’ names) moved the score, and “Grace” scored 0.8059 because grace is also a word. A bag of words is only biased about what it has seen; a pre-trained model has seen everything. Names are a crude proxy for gender or origin, which is why this test measures the model’s reaction to names, not to people.

Each identity term appeared in eight clearly non-toxic sentences (“I am a {} person.”, “My {} colleague got a promotion.” …). The Jigsaw model flagged 25% of the “gay” sentences and 12% of the “Black” ones, and none for the other twelve terms. Its probabilities show the bias more fully than the 0.5 threshold does: “I am a person.” scored 0.001, “I am a Muslim person.” 0.008, “I am a straight person.” 0.001, and “I am a gay person.” 0.29, hundreds of times higher and only a lower threshold away from removal. The TF-IDF model trained on offensive tweets flagged one sentence each for gay, lesbian, Black, white, blind. With eight templates per term each flag is 12.5%, so these rates are coarse; the pattern, which identities attract false positives, is the finding.

Where the bias comes from

Counting identity terms in the 11,916 tweet_eval training tweets shows where the TF-IDF model’s associations come from. Overall, 33.1% of training tweets are labelled offensive. Of the identity terms mentioned in at least 20 tweets, “gay” (58.3% of 24), “straight” (50.0% of 26), “Black” (48.7% of 113), “white” (48.1% of 133) were labelled offensive well above that rate (“straight” and “white” are often used in other senses, so their counts are mixed). Most other terms appear in only a handful of tweets (“lesbian” 5, “trans” 9, “Jewish” 8, “Hindu” 2, “Asian” 4, “Mexican” 4, “deaf” 3), too few for any rate to mean much, and too few for a model to learn anything reliable about them. Abuse is often aimed at identity groups, so tweets that mention them are disproportionately abusive; a model that learns from those labels learns that the words themselves predict abuse. The model is a fair summary of a biased sample.

Mitigation, and what it costs

Two mitigations on the TF-IDF model. Adding 252 non-toxic sentences with identity terms (different templates from the test ones, three copies each) to the 11,916 training tweets changed nothing measurable: the same five terms were flagged, and accuracy moved from 79.53 to 79.3. Next to thousands of real tweets, a few hundred short synthetic sentences did not shift the weights that mattered; why exactly, this experiment cannot tell. Replacing every identity term with the placeholder IDENTITY, in training and at prediction, removed all false positives (0.0%) with accuracy and macro-F1 unchanged (79.42 and 71.98). Blinding works when the list of terms is complete and the terms carry no legitimate signal; it fails on terms not on the list, on misspellings, and on slurs that are themselves identity words, where the term is the abuse. These results are one small model on one dataset; the lesson that transfers is to re-measure both the fairness metric and overall quality after any fix.

Use case: a comment-moderation audit

A news site’s comment filter removes about 4% of comments. A reader complains that comments mentioning being Muslim keep disappearing. The trust-and-safety team builds a template test set like this chapter’s and samples 500 real removed comments per identity term for human review. The false-positive rate for comments mentioning Muslim, gay or Black identity is several times the overall rate. They retrain with in-group, non-toxic comments sampled from approved threads, re-measure the gaps and overall precision, publish the audit internally, and add the identity test set to the release checklist so a future model cannot quietly regress.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Reading a below-threshold score as unbiased: “I am a gay person.” scored 0.29 against 0.001 for “straight”; a threshold change away from a false positive.
  • Assuming counter-examples fix the problem: 252 added sentences changed no false positive here; measure after the fix.
  • Drawing conclusions from tiny counts: “Mexican” appeared in 4 training tweets and “Hindu” in 2; their offensive rates are noise.
  • Treating names as identity: a name is a weak proxy for gender or origin; say so, and test real text from the groups where you can.
  • Declaring a model “fair”: you can only say it passed the tests you ran; list what was not tested.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Why might a toxicity classifier flag “I am a gay man” as toxic?

How a strong answer sounds
Because the training data teaches it that the word predicts toxicity. Abuse is often directed at identity groups, so in toxicity datasets comments that mention “gay” are disproportionately labelled toxic; in the offensive-tweet data I counted, 58.3% of tweets with “gay” were labelled offensive against 33.1% overall. A classifier rewarded for predicting those labels uses the identity term itself as evidence. The result: “I am a gay person.” scored 0.29 toxicity from a widely used Jigsaw model, against 0.001 for “straight”. The harm is that the people being discussed, or speaking about themselves, are the ones silenced.

Deeper: How would you audit and mitigate bias in a production content-moderation model?

How a strong answer sounds
Define the harm first: wrongly removed comments from or about a group, so the metric is the false-positive rate per group at the operating threshold, alongside recall on real abuse. Build an evaluation set: templates like these (identity terms in clearly non-toxic sentences), plus real comments mentioning each group, labelled by trained annotators including members of those groups. Measure per-group false-positive and false-negative rates and their gaps, and trace sources by counting how often each term co-occurs with the toxic label in training data. Mitigate and re-measure everything: collect real non-toxic in-group text (here 252 synthetic sentences did nothing), blind or normalise identity terms where they carry no signal (here it removed every false positive at no accuracy cost), or adjust thresholds per slice where legal and appropriate. Then add the identity tests to the release checklist so a new model cannot regress silently, and monitor appeals by group in production.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Measured: the Jigsaw model scored “I am a gay person.” 0.29 toxic and “I am a straight person.” 0.001; it flagged non-toxic sentences about gay and Black identity.
  • Measured: 58.3% of tweets mentioning “gay” in the training data were labelled offensive against 33.1% overall: the bias is learnt from the labels.
  • Measured: 252 counter-examples changed nothing; blinding identity terms removed every false positive with accuracy unchanged (79.53 → 79.42).
  • Fairness criteria conflict when base rates differ; choose the one that matches the harm.
  • Every mitigation is re-measured on fairness and on overall quality.
