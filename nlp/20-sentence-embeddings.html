Sentence Embeddings & Semantic Similarity

Search, deduplication, clustering and retrieval-augmented generation all need one vector per sentence such that similar meanings land close together. The obvious recipe, take BERT and average its outputs, works badly: off-the-shelf BERT vectors are all crowded into a narrow cone, so every pair of sentences looks similar. The fix is to train for the job, with a contrastive objective that pulls paraphrases together and pushes everything else apart.

This chapter measures the problem and the fix on STS-B, training both kinds of contrastive model here. Raw BERT [CLS] vectors correlated with human similarity ratings at only 20.32 (Spearman ρ × 100), below averaged GloVe vectors (43.03). Five minutes of supervised contrastive training on NLI pairs took the same BERT to 81.15, near the pretrained all-MiniLM-L6-v2 (82.04); a cross-encoder reading both sentences together reached 90.19.

Pooling BERT outputs

A sentence vector can be BERT’s [CLS] output, or the mean of all its token outputs (excluding padding). Quality is measured on STS-B: 1,379 test sentence pairs, each rated by people from 0 (unrelated) to 5 (same meaning); the score is the Spearman correlation between the model’s cosine similarities and the human ratings.

h = model(**enc).last_hidden_state                         # (batch, tokens, 768)
m = enc["attention_mask"][..., None]
cls_vec  = h[:, 0]
mean_vec = (h * m).sum(1) / m.sum(1)                        # mean over real tokens only
score = spearmanr(F.cosine_similarity(e1, e2), human_scores)

Without any sentence-level training, BERT’s [CLS] vector scored 20.32 on STS-B and mean pooling 47.29; plain averaged GloVe vectors scored 43.03. The [CLS] vector was trained only for next-sentence prediction and is then reused by fine-tuning; on its own it is a poor summary of meaning. Mean pooling is better because it at least reflects every word, but it is still barely ahead of a bag of 2014 word vectors.

Anisotropy

A large part of the problem is geometry. Averaged over 5,000 random pairs of test sentences, the cosine similarity between unrelated sentences was 0.734 for BERT [CLS] vectors and 0.571 with mean pooling: the vectors sit in a narrow cone, so almost everything looks similar to almost everything (Ethayarajh, 2019, called this anisotropy). The pairs view shows it: [CLS] gives “A man is slicing a tomato.” and “A man is slicing a carrot.” a cosine of 0.993. Contrastive training spreads the space out: 0.192 after unsupervised SimCSE, 0.123 after supervised training, and 0.025 for all-MiniLM-L6-v2, where an unrelated pair really does score near zero.

Contrastive training

Contrastive learning trains an encoder so that each sentence is closer to its positive partner than to every other sentence in the batch. With a batch of n anchors and n positives, the loss is a softmax cross-entropy over the n×n similarity matrix divided by a temperature τ, with the diagonal as the correct answers; the other sentences in the batch serve as negatives for free. Unsupervised SimCSE (Gao et al., 2021) uses the same sentence twice as anchor and positive, relying on dropout to make the two passes differ. Supervised versions use NLI data: a premise as anchor, an entailed hypothesis as positive and a contradiction as an extra hard negative. Move the temperature in the lab.

za, zp = encode(anchors), encode(positives)                    # unsupervised SimCSE: positives == anchors, dropout differs
sim = F.normalize(za, dim=1) @ F.normalize(zp, dim=1).T / 0.05       # (n, n), temperature 0.05
loss = F.cross_entropy(sim, torch.arange(n))                       # row i should pick column i

Supervision is the big lever. With the same 1,500 steps of 64 pairs, unsupervised SimCSE, where the positive is the same sentence passed through dropout twice, reached 56.43; supervised training, where the positive is a sentence that the first entails and a contradicting sentence is an extra hard negative, reached 81.15, and got to 79.63 within the first 250 steps. The SimCSE paper reports about 76 for unsupervised training on a million sentences, so our 56.43 reflects a short run on 100,000 sentences and was still rising (53.04 → 56.43 over the last 500 steps). The pretrained models, trained on over a billion pairs, are a little better again: all-MiniLM-L6-v2 82.04 with a fifth of BERT’s parameters, all-mpnet-base-v2 83.42.

On the retrieval check, finding a highly rated sentence’s partner among all 2,552 test sentences, raw [CLS] vectors put the partner first only 50.6% of the time against 80.8% for MiniLM. Averaged GloVe managed 72.2%: shared words carry a long way when the partner sentence is a close paraphrase.

Bi-encoders vs cross-encoders

A bi-encoder embeds each sentence separately; similarity is a cosine between precomputed vectors. A cross-encoder feeds both sentences into one transformer and outputs a score, so attention can compare them word by word. It is far more accurate, 90.19 against 83.42 for the best bi-encoder here, and far more expensive. Measured on the same GPU, MiniLM encoded about 11,492 sentences a second and the cross-encoder scored about 653 pairs a second. Finding the nearest neighbour of every sentence among STS-B’s 2,552 sentences takes one pass per sentence for the bi-encoder, about 0.2 s, and 6,510,152 pairs for the cross-encoder, about 2.8 hours. The standard answer is both: a bi-encoder retrieves a shortlist, a cross-encoder re-ranks it (Chapter 26).

🧾
Use case: finding duplicate questions on a community forum

A forum wants to show “this may already have been answered” as people type a question, across 2 million past questions. A pretrained sentence-embedding model encodes every past question once (a few hours on one GPU) into an approximate nearest-neighbour index; each new question is encoded in milliseconds and matched. A cross-encoder re-scores only the top 20 matches, because scoring 2 million pairs per keystroke is impossible. Testing on 500 known duplicate pairs shows the failure this chapter predicts: “How do I stop my laptop overheating?” and “How do I make my laptop overheat less?” match well, but so do “Is it safe to delete system32?” and “Is it unsafe to delete system32?”. The cross-encoder catches most negations, and the team adds them to a test set that every model update must pass.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Using raw BERT [CLS] vectors as sentence embeddings: 20.32 on STS-B, worse than averaged GloVe (43.03).
  • Reading a high cosine as high similarity on an untrained model: random pairs of sentences already averaged 0.734 with [CLS] vectors.
  • Comparing cosine values across models: their scales differ; only rankings and correlations are comparable.
  • Using a cross-encoder for retrieval: it must score every pair; use it to re-rank a bi-encoder’s shortlist.
  • Expecting embeddings to capture negation: MiniLM gave “The cat is not on the mat.” and “The cat is on the mat.” a cosine of 0.944.
  • Using the wrong pooling for a pretrained model: each sentence-transformer is trained with one (mean for MiniLM and MPNet).

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Why can’t you just average BERT’s outputs to get a good sentence embedding?

How a strong answer sounds
Because BERT was never trained to make sentence vectors comparable by cosine. Its pre-training predicts masked words, so its token vectors are tuned for that, and the space they live in is anisotropic: most vectors point in a similar direction, so unrelated sentences get high cosines. I measured raw [CLS] vectors at 20.32 Spearman on STS-B and mean pooling at 47.29, while averaged GloVe got 43.03; random pairs had an average cosine of 0.734. The fix is contrastive training, pulling paraphrases together and pushing other sentences apart: five minutes on NLI pairs took the same model to 81.15, and a pretrained sentence-transformer such as MiniLM reaches 82.04.

Deeper: Design semantic search over 10 million documents. Bi-encoder, cross-encoder, or both?

How a strong answer sounds
Both, in two stages. Offline, a bi-encoder embeds all 10 million documents once (at the 11,492 sentences a second I measured for MiniLM on one GPU, roughly 15 minutes for short texts, longer for long documents split into passages) into an approximate-nearest-neighbour index such as HNSW. At query time, embed the query, retrieve the top 100 or so by cosine in milliseconds, then re-rank those with a cross-encoder, which scored 90.19 on STS-B against 83.42 for the best bi-encoder but costs one forward pass per pair (653 pairs a second here, so 100 candidates is about 153 ms). Add BM25 alongside the dense retriever for exact names and codes (Chapter 26), evaluate on real queries with recall@100 for the first stage and nDCG@10 after re-ranking, and fine-tune the bi-encoder on in-domain pairs if recall is the weak point.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Measured: raw BERT [CLS] 20.32 and mean pooling 47.29 on STS-B, below or near averaged GloVe (43.03); random pairs averaged a cosine of 0.734.
  • Measured: supervised contrastive training on NLI pairs reached 81.15 in 5 minutes; unsupervised SimCSE 56.43 in the same steps.
  • Measured: a cross-encoder reached 90.19 but scored about 653 pairs a second against 11,492 sentences a second for a bi-encoder.
  • Bi-encoders embed once and compare cheaply; cross-encoders read both sentences together and are more accurate but cost one pass per pair.
  • Similarity models still put negations and antonyms close together; test for it.
