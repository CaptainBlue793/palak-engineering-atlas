Low-Resource & Few-Shot NLP

A new task often starts with a few dozen labelled examples, not thousands. With that little data the choice of method matters more than the choice of model, and so does luck: which 16 examples you happened to label can move accuracy by more than the difference between methods. Few-shot results reported from a single draw are close to meaningless.

This chapter trains five methods on 8 to 64 examples, five draws each, on two tasks. Pattern-based training with roberta-base (PET) led at every size on both tasks: from no labels at all it scored 79.01% on SST-2, and with 8 labelled examples 88.6%, while plain fine-tuning of the same model managed 60.71% and TF-IDF 50.82%, a coin toss. With 16 examples, plain fine-tuning’s five draws ranged from 54.47% to 76.95%.

Why tiny datasets are hard

Fine-tuning adds a freshly initialised classification head to a pre-trained encoder and trains both on the labels. With 16 examples the head starts from random, the gradients are noisy, and 125 million parameters can memorise the examples in a few steps. Two families of methods avoid starting from scratch:

  • Reuse the pre-training objective. PET (Schick and Schütze, 2021) rewrites the task as a fill-in-the-blank the masked LM already knows: “A gripping, beautiful film. It was <mask>.”, with a verbaliser mapping “great” to positive and “terrible” to negative. Zero labels already give a classifier; a few labels fine-tune it.
  • Reuse a good embedding space. SetFit (Tunstall et al., 2022) fine-tunes a sentence encoder contrastively on pairs, same-label pairs pulled together and different-label pairs pushed apart, which turns 16 examples into 320 training pairs, then fits logistic regression on the embeddings.

PET was the clear winner with few labels because it starts from something that already works: the masked-LM head, asked whether a review “was great” or “was terrible”, is a 79.01% sentiment classifier before any training. Eight labelled examples tuned it to 88.6%, and more examples barely helped (88.79% at 64). On AG News the same happened from a weaker start (70.1% → 83.65% at 8 → 84.8% at 64), and by 64 labels SetFit had caught up (84.21%).

SetFit beat logistic regression on the same frozen MiniLM vectors at every size: by 3.7 points on SST-2 and 5.6 on AG News at 64 labels. Topic classification (AG News) suits sentence embeddings better than sentiment, where a general-purpose embedding puts “a great film” and “a terrible film” close together (Chapter 20). Plain fine-tuning of roberta-base improved fastest with more data (60.71% → 79.7% on SST-2) but was the least reliable method throughout. TF-IDF needs far more than 64 examples to learn which words matter; on SST-2 it stayed near chance.

The lottery of the draw

Which examples you happened to label mattered as much as the method. With 16 SST-2 examples, plain fine-tuning’s five draws spanned 22.5 points (54.47 to 76.95), and with 8 AG News examples 18.6 points; SetFit’s spread was 14.1 and 13.8. PET was the most stable (2.0 and 4.1 points), again because it starts from a working classifier rather than a random head. A paper reporting one draw of each method could rank them in almost any order. Report the mean and the spread over several draws, and treat a few-shot result from a single draw as anecdote.

Active learning: which examples to label

If you can choose which examples get labelled, uncertainty sampling picks those the current model is least sure about (predicted probability closest to 0.5), on the theory that they teach the most.

Starting from the same 8 random labels and adding 8 per round from a 5,000-sentence pool, uncertainty sampling was worse than random at 16 labels (57.98% against 60.96%), clearly better at 32 (68.1% against 63.42%), and level by 64 (70.39% against 69.49%). With a model this weak, its uncertainty is a poor guide at first: the examples it is least sure about may be odd or ambiguous rather than informative. Active learning tends to pay off once the model is decent, and the benefit at a fixed budget is often a few points, not a multiple; it is worth doing when labels are expensive, and worth measuring against random selection on your data.

Use case: a classifier for a new product category

A marketplace adds a “sustainable” filter and needs to tag listings by sustainability claim (recycled, organic, second-hand, none). A merchandiser labels 40 listings in an afternoon. The ML engineer runs frozen embeddings + LR and SetFit, each over five different 40-example draws, and reports the worst draw as well as the mean, because the merchandiser’s picks were not random. The model goes live as a suggestion that staff confirm; every confirmation becomes a label, uncertainty-sampled listings are queued for review first, and at 500 labels the team switches to full fine-tuning.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Fine-tuning a full model on 8–16 examples and trusting the number: roberta-base ranged from 54.47% to 76.95% across five draws of 16.
  • Using TF-IDF with a handful of labels: it scored 54.31% on SST-2 even with 64.
  • Assuming active learning always beats random: at 16 labels uncertainty sampling was 3.0 points behind.
  • Tuning hyperparameters on a large validation set: a real few-shot project has no such set; the numbers will not reproduce.
  • Unbalanced draws: with 8 examples, a draw with one class missing is a different experiment. Draw per class.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: You have 20 labelled examples. Why not just fine-tune BERT?

How a strong answer sounds
Because with so few examples the new classification head starts random and the gradients are noisy, so a 125M-parameter model overfits or fails depending on which 20 examples you drew. I measured roberta-base fine-tuned on 16 SST-2 examples: five draws ranged from 54.47% to 76.95%. Better options reuse what pre-training learnt: PET rewrites the task as a fill-in-the-blank for the masked-LM head (“It was ___.” with “great” or “terrible”) and reached 88.6% with 16 examples; SetFit fine-tunes a sentence encoder on pairs and fits logistic regression (65.64% on SST-2, 75.4% on AG News); or freeze an encoder and train only a linear head. And spend an hour labelling a few hundred more examples, which usually beats all of them.

Deeper: How would you report a few-shot result so that someone else can trust it?

How a strong answer sounds
Several random draws of the labelled examples (five here), each method trained on the same draws, with the mean, the spread and ideally every run reported, because the spread can exceed the gap between methods (up to 22.5 points for one method here). Balanced draws per class, stated explicitly. No large validation set for tuning, since a real few-shot user would not have one; fix hyperparameters in advance or tune on the few labels with cross-validation, and say which. The full test set for scoring, a zero-label baseline (PET’s pattern alone scored 79.01% on SST-2), and the obvious baseline of simply labelling more, so readers can see what the method buys.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Measured: PET reached 88.6% on SST-2 and 83.65% on AG News with 8 labels; from zero labels it already scored 79.01% and 70.1%.
  • Measured: five draws of 16 examples moved plain fine-tuning by up to 22.5 points; PET by 2.0.
  • Measured: uncertainty sampling lost to random at 16 labels and won at 32 (68.1% vs 63.42%).
  • With tens of labels, reuse what pre-training learnt: an embedding space or the MLM head.
  • Report the spread over several draws, not one number.
