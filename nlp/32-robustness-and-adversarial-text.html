Robustness & Adversarial Text

Real users make typos, write in capitals, and say the same thing a dozen ways. Adversaries do worse: they search for the smallest edit that flips a spam filter or a moderation model. A classifier that is 91% accurate on clean benchmark text can be far weaker on either, and different model families break in different ways.

This chapter attacks three sentiment classifiers with natural noise, paraphrase and a word-substitution search, then trains them to resist. With typos in 30% of words DistilBERT fell 7.2 points, word TF-IDF 4.6 and character TF-IDF only 1.0, yet DistilBERT stayed the most accurate of the three. The word-substitution attack flipped 70.7% of DistilBERT’s correct predictions by changing fewer than two words on average, but many of its “synonyms” were antonyms: “bleak” became “rosy”.

  • Measure accuracy under typos, casing and paraphrase
  • Explain why character n-grams and subwords degrade differently from words
  • Build a greedy word-substitution attack and read what it changes
  • Judge whether an “adversarial example” still means the same thing
  • Use noisy training data as a defence, and measure its cost on clean text

Natural noise

A typo changes a word into one the model may never have seen. A word-level TF-IDF model loses the feature entirely: “brilliant” and “brillaint” share nothing. A character n-gram model keeps most of the word’s 2–5-character pieces. A subword transformer splits the misspelling into smaller, rarer pieces and has to reconstruct the meaning from context. Each model lost accuracy as typos increased, at different rates. From clean text to 30% of words misspelt, DistilBERT dropped from 91.06 to 83.83, word TF-IDF from 82.8 to 78.21, and character TF-IDF from 81.08 to 80.05: the character model barely noticed, as the lab predicts, because most of a misspelt word’s 2–5-grams survive. But the curves did not cross. DistilBERT’s subword tokeniser breaks a misspelt word into unfamiliar pieces and the model recovers much of the meaning from context, so even at 30% typos it was 3.8 points ahead. Casing changed nothing for any model, for a dull reason worth checking in your own pipeline: the uncased DistilBERT tokeniser and scikit-learn’s TF-IDF both lower-case before anything else.

A word-substitution attack

A TextFooler-style attack (Jin et al., 2020) needs only the model’s output probabilities. Rank words by how much deleting each one lowers the probability of the true label; then, most important first, replace each with the nearby word in an embedding space that hurts the model most, and stop when the prediction flips. The catch is in “nearby”: GloVe neighbours are words that appear in similar contexts, and antonyms do. “Good” and “bad” are close neighbours.

Run on 300 sentences each model classified correctly, the attack succeeded against 70.7% for DistilBERT (changing 1.76 words on average when it succeeded), 76.3% for word TF-IDF (2.03 words) and 79.7% for character TF-IDF (1.91 words). Those numbers overstate the problem, and reading the swaps shows why. GloVe’s nearest neighbours are words used in similar contexts, and opposites are used in similar contexts: the attack turned “bleak” into “rosy”, “slow” into “faster”, “poised” into “unlikely”, “refreshingly” into “deceptively”. “Unflinchingly bleak and desperate” became “unflinchingly rosy and desperate”, and the model, reasonably, changed its mind. Other swaps did preserve the meaning (“astounding” → “unbelievable”, “tedious” → “exhausting”), and those are genuine robustness failures. A credible attack needs a meaning check, such as a sentence-similarity threshold or an NLI model, and human review of a sample; counting label flips alone measures how often the attack changed the sentence’s sentiment as much as how often it fooled the model.

Paraphrase consistency

Round-tripping every validation sentence through German gives a paraphrase that mostly keeps the meaning (“although laced with humor and a few fanciful touches , the film is a refreshingly serious look at young women .” → “although the film has humor and a few imaginative touches, it is a refreshingly serious look at young women.”). DistilBERT gave the same prediction for 94.5% of sentence–paraphrase pairs and kept 88.53% accuracy on the paraphrases; the TF-IDF models were consistent only 87.8% and 88.0% of the time and lost 3–4 points of accuracy. A bag of n-grams is tied to the exact wording; a pre-trained encoder generalises across rewordings. Some inconsistency is the translation’s fault, not the classifier’s, so these rates are a lower bound on true consistency.

Defence: train on noise

Training on the SST-2 training set plus a copy with typos in 20% of words: DistilBERT kept the same clean accuracy (90.14 → 90.14) and gained on noisy text, from 84.75 to 86.93 at 20% typos and 82.68 to 86.12 at 30%. For the TF-IDF models the change was about a point at most. Noise-augmented training is cheap and, for a fine-tuned encoder, close to free on clean data here; it should match the noise you actually expect (keyboard typos, OCR errors, phone autocorrect), because it does not protect against the attack’s word substitutions, which are a different kind of change.

Use case: a spam filter under attack

An email provider’s spam classifier is gamed within a week: spammers write “fr33 m0ney”, insert zero-width spaces and swap in synonyms until messages get through. The team adds a normalisation step (Unicode NFKC, zero-width characters stripped, common leetspeak mapped), a character n-gram model alongside the subword transformer, and noisy copies of known spam in training. They track two numbers per release: clean precision, and the success rate of an in-house attack like this chapter’s on last month’s spam. A release that lowers the second at the cost of the first is a decision, not an accident.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Counting every label flip as a successful attack: swaps such as “bleak” → “rosy” change the meaning; the model was right to flip.
  • Assuming the most noise-tolerant model is the best one: character TF-IDF lost only 1.0 points at 30% typos and still finished 3.8 behind DistilBERT.
  • Testing casing on a pipeline that lower-cases anyway: both models here gave identical results for lower case and capitals because they never saw case.
  • Reporting robustness on one noise level: show the curve; models cross over.
  • Forgetting input normalisation: Unicode tricks are cheaper to strip than to learn.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is an adversarial example for a text classifier?

How a strong answer sounds
An input changed in a way that should not change the answer, such as a typo, a synonym or a paraphrase, that nevertheless changes the model’s prediction. For text the hard part is “should not change”: a word-substitution attack I ran flipped 70.7% of a sentiment model’s correct predictions with under two word swaps, but many swaps were embedding neighbours that are opposites, “bleak” to “rosy”, so the model was right to change its mind. Real adversarial examples preserve meaning, which needs checking with a similarity model or people.

Deeper: How would you make a content classifier robust to users trying to evade it?

How a strong answer sounds
Normalise inputs first: Unicode normalisation, strip zero-width characters, map common obfuscations (leetspeak, homoglyphs), since that is cheaper than learning them. Train with realistic noise: typo augmentation here cost nothing on clean SST-2 and recovered 3.4 points at 30% typos. Combine models that fail differently, such as a subword encoder with a character n-gram model, which barely moved under typos (1.0 points). Red-team regularly with attacks that check meaning is preserved, add the successful ones to training, and track attack success rate per release alongside accuracy. And keep humans in the loop for borderline cases, because evasion adapts to whatever you deploy.
