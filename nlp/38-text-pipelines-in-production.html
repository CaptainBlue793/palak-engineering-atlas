Text Pipelines in Production

A text model is trained on a snapshot of language; production text keeps moving. New products, new slang, a new customer segment, a marketing campaign: the input distribution drifts and accuracy falls, usually without anyone noticing, because in production nobody hands you the labels. The job is to detect drift from signals you can compute without labels, decide when to label and retrain, and keep sensitive data out of logs and training sets along the way.

This chapter simulates ten weeks of drifting traffic and tests which label-free signals actually track the hidden accuracy. As the stream moved from movie reviews to restaurant and product reviews, the IMDB-trained TF-IDF model fell from 91.0% to 84.2%; every label-free signal moved with it, and mean confidence tracked accuracy almost perfectly (r = 0.969). The SST-2 DistilBERT, by contrast, did not get worse at all. A thousand well-chosen new-domain labels closed 63% of the gap that 8,000 labels close; an off-the-shelf PII detector found only 36.1% of the personal data in synthetic customer messages.

  • Simulate domain drift and measure its effect on accuracy
  • Monitor label-free signals: confidence, unseen words, prediction rates, embedding drift
  • Check which signals actually track accuracy
  • Retrain with a labelling budget, chosen at random or by uncertainty
  • Detect personal data in text, and measure what the detectors miss

Drift without labels

The simulation: a sentiment model is trained on IMDB movie reviews. For three weeks it sees IMDB reviews; then, week by week, the traffic shifts to restaurant reviews (Yelp) and product reviews (Amazon) until by week 8 there are no movie reviews at all. Each week has 1,000 reviews. True accuracy is computed but hidden from the monitors, which see only:

  • Mean confidence: average max(p, 1 − p). Models are often less sure on unfamiliar text.
  • Unseen-word rate: share of words not in the training vocabulary.
  • Predicted-positive rate: a sudden change can mean the world changed, or the model broke.
  • Embedding drift: cosine distance between this week’s mean sentence embedding and the training set’s.

Measured: what drift does, and what sees it

Over the ten weeks, TF-IDF + logistic regression trained on IMDB lost 6.8 points, from 91.0% in the first weeks of movie reviews to 84.2% in week 8, when the traffic was half Yelp and half Amazon. All four label-free signals saw it. Across weeks, accuracy correlated with mean confidence at r = 0.969, with the unseen-word rate at r = -0.928 (it rose from 1.28% to 3.41%), with embedding drift at r = -0.892 (the cosine distance from the training centroid rose from 0.0109 to 0.5117), and least well with the predicted-positive rate (-0.678), which wandered by a few points either way. Two caveats. The new reviews are also much shorter (227 words on average in week 1, 104 by week 10), which lowers a bag-of-words model’s confidence on its own, so the confidence signal is partly measuring length; and ten weekly points make any correlation fragile.

The SST-2 DistilBERT, scored on the same stream, did not degrade: 88.2% in week 1 and 90.2% in week 10, peaking at 91.3%, with mean confidence above 97% throughout. It was trained on short movie-review phrases and handles short reviews of anything well. The same drift hurt one model and not the other, and embedding drift, which is model-independent, raised the same alarm for both. A drift alarm says the inputs changed; only labels say whether this model got worse.

Measured: retraining with a budget

Retraining the TF-IDF model on IMDB plus new-domain labels, scored on 2,000 held-out Yelp and Amazon reviews: no retraining 86.25%; +200 random labels 86.65%; +200 labels chosen as the model’s most uncertain 86.45%; +1,000 random 87.35%; +1,000 most uncertain 88.75%; and +8,000 90.2%. At 200 labels nothing beyond noise happened. At 1,000, uncertainty sampling beat random by 1.4 points and recovered 63% of the gap to the full 8,000, against 28% for random labels. A few hundred labels are enough to measure drift; fixing it takes more, and choosing which ones to label pays off once the budget is in the thousands.

Personal data in text

Production text is full of names, emails, phone numbers and account numbers. They should not reach logs, analytics or training sets without a reason and a legal basis. Detection usually combines patterns for formatted identifiers and NER for names and places. The ai4privacy dataset is synthetic, which is the only responsible way to publish a PII benchmark, and its 50-odd types show how much falls outside both.

Six regular expressions and an NER model found 36.1% of the gold PII spans across 53 types. Formatted identifiers were caught every time (email, url, phonenumber, maskednumber, creditcardnumber, ipv4), and names 77.3% of the time through the NER model. Almost everything else was missed: dates 13.8% and dates of birth 9.7%, streets 1.5%, cities 1.6%, and buildingnumber, currencysymbol, jobarea, password, time, state, street, jobtitle not at all. Account numbers scored 0 because this dataset writes them as plain digit strings that the IBAN pattern does not match: a pattern is a guess about format, and each new format is a miss. Only 6 detected spans fell outside the gold annotation, so precision was high; the problem is recall. Production PII detection needs an NER model trained on PII types (addresses, dates of birth, account names), locale-aware patterns, and a measured recall per type on real data, plus the policy question of what to do when it misses.

Use case: a complaints classifier after a product launch

A bank’s complaint router was trained before it launched a new savings app. Within a month, complaints about the app (“the round-up feature”, “pots”) are routed to the wrong team. The monitoring dashboard had flagged a rise in unseen words and embedding drift two weeks earlier; nobody owned the alert. The fix is organisational as much as technical: an owner for each alert, a standing weekly sample of 200 complaints labelled by the routing team, uncertainty sampling for a further 200 after any drift alert, and a PII filter in front of the labelling tool so that customers’ account numbers never reach the annotators.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Treating a drift alarm as an accuracy drop: the same drift lowered TF-IDF by 6.8 points and left DistilBERT unchanged; label a sample before retraining.
  • Retraining on a couple of hundred new labels and expecting recovery: +200 labels moved accuracy from 86.25% to 86.65%; +8,000 reached 90.2%.
  • Trusting regexes for PII: they caught emails and card numbers perfectly and dates of birth 9.7% of the time; overall recall was 36.1%.
  • Logging raw text by default: scrub or hash personal data before it reaches logs and dashboards.
  • Testing a retrained model only on new data: check it has not got worse on the old domain.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How would you know a text classifier is getting worse in production if you have no labels?

How a strong answer sounds
Monitor signals that need no labels and compare them with the training period: the model’s mean confidence and the share of low-confidence predictions; the share of words, or tokens, not seen in training; the distribution of predicted labels; and the drift of the inputs’ embeddings from the training centroid. In a simulated move from movie to product reviews, a TF-IDF model’s accuracy fell 6.8 points and all four moved with it, mean confidence most closely (r = 0.969 with accuracy). Then confirm with labels: a small, regular random sample labelled by people, plus extra labels when an alarm fires. Signals say the inputs changed; only labels say the model got worse: on the same stream a DistilBERT model did not degrade at all.

Deeper: Design the monitoring, labelling and retraining loop for a production text classifier.

How a strong answer sounds
Logging: every input (with PII scrubbed first), prediction and confidence, with model version. Monitoring: weekly dashboards of confidence, out-of-vocabulary rate, prediction distribution and embedding drift against a training baseline, with alert thresholds and a named owner. Labelling: a standing random sample (a few hundred a week) for unbiased accuracy, plus uncertainty-sampled items when an alert fires; here, at 1,000 labels, uncertainty sampling recovered 63% of the accuracy gap against 28% for random. Retraining: scheduled, or triggered when the labelled sample shows a drop beyond the noise, training on old plus new data; evaluate on both old and new held-out sets, plus the behavioural tests, before promoting; shadow-deploy and compare; keep the previous model for rollback. And PII: detection before anything reaches logs or annotators, with measured per-type recall, because ours missed most dates, addresses and account numbers.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Measured: domain drift cost an IMDB TF-IDF model 6.8 points; confidence (r = 0.969), unseen words and embedding drift all tracked it, and a DistilBERT on the same stream did not degrade.
  • Measured: +1,000 uncertain new-domain labels recovered 63% of the gap, random 28%; +200 did nothing measurable.
  • Measured: regexes + NER found 36.1% of PII spans: 100% of emails, URLs and card numbers, 77.3% of names, almost no dates, addresses or account numbers.
  • Label-free signals tell you when to look; only labels tell you what you will find.
  • Keep personal data out of logs and training sets, and measure what your detectors miss.

Check your understanding

Why can’t production accuracy usually be monitored directly?

Hence label-free proxies, plus a regular labelled sample.

What does embedding drift measure?

Measured: it rose from 0.0109 to 0.5117 as the stream moved to new domains, for both models alike.

Why can mean confidence fail as a drift alarm?

Here confidence tracked the TF-IDF model closely (r = 0.969), but DistilBERT stayed above 97% confident throughout; on drift that does hurt a model, confident mistakes are common.

After drift, which labels help most per label?

Measured at 1,000 labels: uncertain 88.75%, random 87.35%, from 86.25% with none.

Why combine regular expressions with NER for PII?

Measured: regexes 100% on emails, URLs, IP addresses and card numbers; NER 77.3% on names; dates, addresses and job titles almost never found.
