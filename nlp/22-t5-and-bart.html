Encoder–Decoder Transformers: T5 & BART

An encoder reads, a decoder writes; put a transformer of each kind together and you have Chapter 12’s seq2seq model rebuilt with attention everywhere. T5 and BART pre-train such models by corrupting text and asking the decoder to repair it, and T5 goes further: every task, classification included, becomes “text in, text out”, so one model and one loss serve them all.

This chapter looks at what the two pre-training objectives teach, measures T5 on a task it was secretly trained on, and fine-tunes encoder–decoder, decoder-only and encoder-only models with one recipe. Asked “sst2 sentence: …” with no training here, the original t5-base was right 93.92% of the time, which turns out to be memory rather than skill: SST-2 was in its pre-training mix. Fine-tuned for one epoch, bart-base reached 93.35%, t5-small 91.74%, GPT-2 91.17% and DistilBERT 90.14%.

Text-to-text framing

T5 (Raffel et al., 2020) casts every task as mapping an input string to an output string. Translation is “translate English to German: That is good.” → “Das ist gut.”; sentiment is “sst2 sentence: a gem of a film” → “positive”; a regression task outputs the number as text, “3.8”. The model is an encoder–decoder transformer trained with the same cross-entropy on output tokens for every task. The price is that a classifier can now produce text that is not a label at all, so outputs must be checked.

Span corruption

T5 pre-trains by replacing random spans of the input (15% of tokens, mean span length 3) with sentinel tokens <extra_id_0>, <extra_id_1> …, and training the decoder to output each sentinel followed by the tokens it hid. The target is short, so pre-training is cheap. Build an example in the lab.

BART’s denoising

BART (Lewis et al., 2020) corrupts text more freely (masking spans with a single [MASK] whatever their length, deleting, shuffling sentences) and trains the decoder to regenerate the whole original. It is a denoising autoencoder, and it is especially good at generation tasks where the output resembles the input, such as summarisation (Chapter 23).

Asked to fill gaps, the two pre-trained models show their objectives. BART regenerates the whole sentence and fills the gap fluently: “Paris is the capital of <mask>.” → “Paris is the capital of France.”, “The film was <mask> and I loved it.” → “The film was really good and I loved it.”. The original t5-small, asked “Paris is the <extra_id_0> of France.”, answered “Paris est la capitale de France.”: it translated the sentence into French, because the released checkpoint was trained on a mixture of span corruption and supervised tasks including English–French translation, and the unfamiliar input pulled it towards a task it knew. Its other fills were poor or repetitive (“I was a,,,,,,,,”). A small multi-task model is not a reliable gap-filler.

Fine-tuning T5, and a contamination lesson

Before fine-tuning, the obvious first test: ask the released models directly. With T5’s own task prefix, “sst2 sentence: …”, t5-small scored 89.56% and t5-base 93.92% on SST-2 validation, with no invalid answers. That is not zero-shot ability. The released T5 checkpoints were pre-trained on a mixture that included the supervised GLUE tasks, SST-2’s training set among them, with exactly this prefix, so the model has seen the task and its labels. Asked the same thing in plain English (“Is this movie review positive or negative?”), the same models scored 1.38% and 0.34%: t5-base produced an invalid answer 869 times out of 872, most often “is this review”, echoing the question. Flan-T5, instruction-tuned on over a thousand tasks, answered the plain question well (87.96% small, 93.81% base), but the Flan collection also includes SST-2, so it is not a clean zero-shot test either. This is contamination: a benchmark result that measures memory of the test task rather than generalisation.

All four architectures land within about three points after one epoch, one seed each, so differences under a point are noise (Chapter 17 measured a half-point seed range for BERT on this task). The text-to-text t5-small, which writes the word “positive” or “negative”, matched a classification head: generating a one-word label costs nothing in accuracy and produced no invalid outputs after fine-tuning. Two caveats on t5-small: its pre-training mix included SST-2, which may help, and it ran at a learning rate 15 times higher than the others (3·10−4, the usual T5 setting). The decoder-only GPT-2, reading left to right and classifying from the last token, was competitive with the encoders. For a short classification task the architecture matters less than pre-training and size; the encoder is simply the cheapest way to get there (67M parameters and 40.4 s against BART’s 140M and 96.9 s).

Encoder–decoder vs decoder-only

An encoder–decoder spends its parameters in two places: a bidirectional encoder that reads the input fully and a decoder that writes. A decoder-only model (GPT, Llama) treats input and output as one sequence and reads the input only left to right. For tasks with a clear input and a short output, such as translation, summarisation and classification, encoder–decoders are efficient at small sizes; at very large scale decoder-only models won out because one stack is simpler to scale and train on raw text, and instruction tuning made them general. The measurements above compare them at roughly the same size on one task; the LLMs course takes the decoder-only side further.

Use case: one model for a support desk’s three tasks

A support team needs tickets classified by product, a one-line summary for the queue view, and the customer’s order number extracted. Instead of three models, they fine-tune one t5-base on all three as text-to-text tasks with prefixes (“classify:”, “summarise:”, “order number:”), mixing the examples in each batch. It matches three separate models within a point on classification and is simpler to deploy. Two lessons from this chapter shape the evaluation: outputs are parsed and anything that is not a known product name is counted as wrong, and they never benchmark a public checkpoint on a public dataset without checking whether that dataset was in its training mix.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Reporting “zero-shot” scores for a model whose pre-training included the task: t5-base scored 93.92% on SST-2 with T5’s own prefix because SST-2 was in its training mixture.
  • Prompting the original T5 with plain instructions: it is not instruction-tuned and echoed the question (869 invalid answers of 872 for t5-base); use Flan-T5 or the task prefixes it was trained with.
  • Not parsing generated labels: text-to-text classifiers can answer “neutral” or “the film”; count invalid outputs as errors.
  • Using BERT-style learning rates for T5: T5 is usually fine-tuned at around 10−4 to 3·10−4 with Adafactor or AdamW.
  • Feeding BART a mask token in the wrong format (<mask>, not [MASK]).
  • Assuming encoder–decoder means better: for short classification, an encoder alone is smaller and faster.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What does “text-to-text” mean in T5, and what are its pros and cons?

How a strong answer sounds
Every task is written as an input string and a target string, and one encoder–decoder is trained with one loss, cross-entropy on the target tokens. Sentiment is “sst2 sentence: …” → “positive”, translation “translate English to German: …” → the German, summarisation “summarize: …” → the summary. The advantages: one model and training loop for classification, generation and extraction, easy multi-task training, and labels can be natural words the model already knows. The costs: outputs must be parsed (a model can answer off the label set), classification needs a decoding step, and an encoder–decoder is larger than an encoder for the same job. In practice it worked: t5-small fine-tuned as text-to-text reached 91.74% on SST-2 against 90.14% for DistilBERT with a classification head, with no invalid outputs.

Deeper: A pretrained model scores suspiciously well on a public benchmark with no fine-tuning. How do you check for contamination?

How a strong answer sounds
First check the model’s documented training data against the benchmark, including multi-task and instruction-tuning mixtures, not just the pre-training corpus: the original T5 mixture included GLUE, so its 93.92% on SST-2 is memory. Then test sensitivity to format: the same model asked in plain English scored 0.34%; a skill should survive a rephrasing, memorised prefixes do not. Look for n-gram overlap between the test set and any available training data, and compare performance on the official test set with a freshly written or newly collected set of the same kind: a large drop is the signature of contamination. Report results only on data the model cannot have seen, and say what you checked.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • T5 casts every task as text in, text out, with one encoder–decoder and one loss.
  • T5 pre-trains by span corruption (predict the hidden spans); BART by denoising (regenerate the whole text).
