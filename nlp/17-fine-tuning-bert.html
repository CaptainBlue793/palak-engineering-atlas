Fine-Tuning BERT for Classification

Fine-tuning is the second half of the BERT recipe: put a small classification head on top of the pretrained encoder and train everything, briefly, on the labelled task. It is a few lines of code and it took over NLP in 2019. It is also more fragile than it looks: the learning rate, the warm-up and even the random seed can each move the result by points.

This chapter fine-tunes bert-base-uncased over and over on SST-2 to measure each of those knobs, then compares it with the TF-IDF baseline from 100 labelled examples to 25,000 full reviews. At learning rates from 10−5 to 10−4 it reached 91.51–92.55% on SST-2; at 3·10−4 it collapsed to 50.92%, the score of always answering “positive”. With 100 labelled examples it beat TF-IDF by 16.4 points; with all 25,000 IMDB reviews it scored 93.2% against TF-IDF’s 90.97%.

  • Add a classification head and fine-tune a pretrained encoder
  • Choose a learning rate and warm-up from measurements
  • Quantify seed variance and report it
  • Decide when to freeze layers
  • Know how much fine-tuning gains over baselines, and where

Classification heads

For sequence classification, Hugging Face’s BertForSequenceClassification takes BERT’s final [CLS] vector, passes it through the “pooler” (a dense layer with tanh, pre-trained with next-sentence prediction), applies dropout and a linear layer to the class logits. The head is new and randomly initialised; everything else starts from the pretrained weights, and all 110 million parameters are trained, usually for two to four epochs, with AdamW and a learning rate around 2·10−5, a hundred times smaller than for training from scratch.

m = AutoModelForSequenceClassification.from_pretrained("bert-base-uncased", num_labels=2)   # encoder + new head
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(m.parameters(), lr=2e-5, weight_decay=0.01)
sched = LambdaLR(opt, lambda s: min(1, s / warm) * max(0, (total - s) / total))    # linear warm-up, then linear decay
for x, mask, y in batches:
    loss = F.cross_entropy(m(input_ids=x, attention_mask=mask).logits, y)
    loss.backward(); clip_grad_norm_(m.parameters(), 1.0); opt.step(); sched.step(); opt.zero_grad()

Learning rate and warm-up

With a learning rate too low, the head barely trains in a couple of epochs; too high, and the first updates, driven by a random head, wreck the pretrained weights (“catastrophic forgetting”), after which the model may predict one class for everything. Warm-up ramps the learning rate up from zero over the first few percent of steps, so the head settles before the encoder takes large steps. Draw the schedule in the lab.

The usable range is wide: anything from 10−5 to 5·10−5 landed between 92.2 and 92.55, inside the seed spread measured below. At 10−4 accuracy slipped to 91.51, and at 3·10−4 training collapsed: the loss rose back to about 0.69 (ln 2, a coin flip) and the model predicted one class for every sentence, 50.92% being exactly the share of positive sentences in the validation set. Warm-up mattered where the rate was high: without it, 10−4 fell from 91.51 to 90.14. At 5·10−5 the run without warm-up scored 92.66, slightly above the one with it (92.55), a difference smaller than the seed noise. Warm-up is insurance against the high end of the range, not a source of accuracy.

Seed variance

Six runs at 2·10−5 with nothing changed but the random seed (which sets the head’s initialisation, dropout and the data order) scored between 91.97 and 92.55, a range of 0.58 points, mean 92.32. That is small for SST-2 with 67,349 training phrases. With little data it is not: with 100 labelled examples three seeds scored 74.54, 79.59, 72.59, a 7.0-point range (Dodge et al., 2020, found the same on small GLUE tasks). Any comparison between two fine-tuning setups that differ by less than the seed range, here half a point on the full data and several points on small data, is not a comparison at all.

Freezing layers

Freezing the whole encoder and training only the head (1,538 parameters on top of a fixed [CLS] vector) reached 79.93%: BERT’s pretrained [CLS] vector is not organised for sentiment, because pre-training never asked it to be. Unfreezing the top two layers, 14.2M trainable parameters, recovered most of the gap at 91.06%, and training all 109.5M gave 92.43%. Freezing saves time (29.6 s and 44.8 s against 148.2 s for two epochs here) and memory, and it guards against overfitting with very small data; with a full dataset, fine-tune everything.

BERT’s advantage is largest where labels are scarce: with 500 examples it scored 85.78 against TF-IDF’s 65.67, a 20.1-point gap, and with 100 examples it was already at 75.57, a level TF-IDF only passes somewhere between 2,000 and 10,000 labels (73.4 and 79.16). The gap narrows as data grows, to 10.2 points on all of SST-2 and 2.2 on IMDB’s long reviews, where a bag of words already captures most of the signal. The IMDB figure is on 5,000 of the 25,000 test reviews and the TF-IDF one on all of them, so compare them to within a few tenths. Against those points, the cost: the IMDB run took 3 minutes on a GPU, the TF-IDF model a few seconds on a CPU.

Use case: a complaint detector with 800 labels

A utility company wants to flag customer emails that are formal complaints, which have regulatory deadlines. It has 800 labelled emails. A TF-IDF model reaches 82% F1; fine-tuned BERT reaches 89% on one run and 84% on the next, with nothing changed but the seed, which alarms the team until they measure it properly. They settle on five seeds and choose the run with the best validation F1, use a learning rate of 2·10−5 with warm-up after a 3·10−4 run collapsed to “not a complaint” for everything, and report the median and range to the compliance team rather than the best number. The final model goes into production with the TF-IDF model as a fallback when the two disagree badly.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Picking a learning rate at the top of the range: 3·10−4 collapsed SST-2 to 50.92%, the majority-class score; 10−4 without warm-up lost 1.4 points.
  • Comparing two setups on one seed each: with 100 examples the same setup ranged from 72.59 to 79.59 across seeds.
  • Freezing the encoder to save time on a full dataset: head-only training reached 79.93% against 92.43%.
  • Fine-tuning with a from-scratch learning rate such as 10−3: the pretrained weights are destroyed in the first hundred steps.
  • Truncating long documents silently: 15% of IMDB reviews exceed 512 tokens (P2); Chapter 34 covers what to do.
  • Skipping the TF-IDF baseline: it decides whether the GPU bill is worth it.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How do you fine-tune BERT for a classification task?

How a strong answer sounds
Load the pretrained encoder with a classification head, here BertForSequenceClassification, which puts a pooler and a linear layer on the [CLS] vector. Tokenise with the model’s own tokeniser, pad per batch and pass the attention mask. Train all the weights with AdamW at a small learning rate, 2–5·10−5, with linear warm-up over the first 5–10% of steps and linear decay, batch 16–32, for two to four epochs, with gradient clipping. Evaluate on a validation set after each epoch. Run several seeds, because with little data the spread is large. On SST-2 that gave 92.32% (six seeds within 0.6 points) against 82.11 for TF-IDF, and with only 500 labels 85.78 against 65.67.

Deeper: Your BERT fine-tuning run sometimes collapses to predicting one class. Why, and how do you fix it?

How a strong answer sounds
Collapse to one class means the first updates destroyed the pretrained representation: the head starts random, its gradients at a high learning rate push large changes into the encoder, and the model falls into the trivial solution of predicting the majority class, with the loss stuck near ln 2 for two classes. I reproduced it: at 3·10−4 BERT went to 50.92% on SST-2, exactly the positive share, while 2·10−5 gave 92.43. Fixes, in order: lower the learning rate into the 10−5 to 5·10−5 range; add warm-up (without it, 10−4 lost 1.4 points here); clip gradients; check the labels are balanced and correctly mapped; and run several seeds, because on small datasets some seeds fail where others succeed.

  • Fine-tuning adds a head to a pretrained encoder and trains everything briefly at a small learning rate with warm-up.
