GloVe, fastText & Evaluating Embeddings

Word2vec learns from one window at a time. Two successors changed what is learned from. GloVe fits word vectors to the whole corpus’s co-occurrence counts at once; fastText builds each word’s vector from its character pieces, so it can make a vector for a word it has never seen. Both raise the question this chapter is really about: how do you know whether one set of embeddings is better than another?

GloVe is written here in PyTorch and trained on a 50,000 × 50,000 co-occurrence matrix built on the GPU in 9 seconds; fastText is trained on the same text. Then four sets of vectors, including pretrained GloVe from 6 billion words, are scored on three standard benchmarks and on a real task. The benchmark rankings did not predict the task ranking. And pretrained GloVe, asked man : doctor :: woman : ?, answered nurse.

  • Build a co-occurrence matrix and fit GloVe’s weighted least-squares objective
  • Explain how fastText composes vectors from character n-grams
  • Run similarity and analogy benchmarks, and know what each measures
  • Tell intrinsic from extrinsic evaluation, and trust the second
  • Measure social bias in embeddings

GloVe

GloVe (Pennington, Socher and Manning, 2014) starts from counts. Xij is how often word j appears near word i across the whole corpus, here within 10 words, each co-occurrence weighted by 1/distance. It then asks for vectors whose dot product predicts the log count:

J = Σi,j : Xij > 0 f(Xij) · (wi · w̃j + bi + b̃j − log Xij)²,   f(x) = min(1, (x / 100)0.75)

The weighting f stops very frequent pairs (“of the”) from dominating and gives rare pairs, whose counts are noisy, less say. Pairs that never co-occur are skipped entirely, which is what makes it trainable: on the first 50 million tokens of WikiText-103 with a 50,000-word vocabulary, only 78.0 million of the 2.5 billion possible pairs were non-zero (3.1%). Counting them took 8.8 seconds on the GPU, one torch.unique per window offset; 25 epochs of Adagrad over the non-zero pairs took 29.6 seconds. The final vector is w + w̃, the sum of the two sets.

for o in range(1, 11):                                        # co-occurrence on the GPU, both directions

fastText and subwords

Word2vec and GloVe give each word one vector and nothing to unseen words. fastText (Bojanowski et al., 2017) represents a word as a bag of its character n-grams of length 3 to 6, with boundary marks, plus the word itself: where becomes <wh, whe, her, ere, re>, <whe, … and <where>. The word’s vector is the sum of its pieces’ vectors, trained with the skip-gram objective of Chapter 6. Words that share pieces share information, and a word never seen in training still gets a vector from its pieces. Type a word into the lab to see its n-grams.

Trained on the same 50 million tokens (one pass, 33.7 seconds on 16 CPU threads), fastText gave the misspelt witherspooon, which it had never seen, the nearest neighbour witherspoon; the made-up transformerish landed next to transformer and transformers; unwatchable’s neighbours were watchable, untouchable, unshakable and unfilmable. The same mechanism misleads when spelling and meaning part ways: covid, absent from this pre-2020 corpus, came out nearest to ovid, the Roman poet, and blockchain nearest to blockage and roadblock. Subwords know form, not meaning.

Similarity and analogy benchmarks

Three standard tests, each measuring something different. Analogies (Google, 2013) test whether relations are consistent directions: capitals and countries, currencies, family (semantic); plurals, tenses, comparatives (syntactic). WordSim-353 asks people how related two words are, so coffee and cup score high. SimLex-999 asks how similar they are, so coffee and cup score low and antonyms score very low; it is the harder test for distributional vectors, which put antonyms close together (Chapter 6).

Each benchmark crowns a different winner. fastText scored 68.2% on syntactic analogies, as high as GloVe trained on 120 times more text, because “walked” and “walking” literally share pieces, but only 13.8% on semantic ones, where shared spelling is no help (Paris and France share nothing). Our word2vec had the best WordSim correlation, beating vectors trained on 6 billion words. Our GloVe trailed on most tests; it saw half the text of our word2vec, and GloVe tends to need large corpora to do well. SimLex stayed low for everything, around 0.2–0.3, because all four learn relatedness from context, not similarity.

Intrinsic vs extrinsic evaluation

Intrinsic evaluations test the vectors directly, as above. Extrinsic evaluations test them inside the system you care about. Here the system is the simplest one possible: represent each SST-2 sentence as the average of its word vectors and train a logistic regression. The rankings disagree. fastText was best of our three on analogies by 13 points, but 2.5 points behind word2vec on the task (69.4% against 71.9%) and only 0.3 ahead of our GloVe; word2vec, second on analogies, was best of ours on the task. Only the pretrained vectors won both, by having seen far more text. Analogy accuracy rewards consistent relational directions; sentiment needs good and bad to be far apart, which no distributional benchmark asks for. Choose embeddings on the task, not the benchmark. For scale, TF-IDF with logistic regression scored 82.8% on the same sentences (Chapter 1): averaging vectors throws away so much that static embeddings rarely beat a bag of n-grams on their own; Chapter 8 trains models that use them better.

Bias in embeddings

Embeddings learn whatever regularities the text contains, including social ones. A simple measurement (after Bolukbasi et al., 2016): take the direction she − he and project occupation words onto it. In pretrained GloVe the most female-leaning were dancer (+0.29), nurse (+0.28) and receptionist (+0.27); the most male-leaning were manager (−0.28), architect (−0.19), secretary (−0.18) and engineer (−0.16). Secretary leaning male is probably itself a corpus effect: in news text it is often “Secretary of State” or “general secretary”. The analogy test shows the same thing: pretrained GloVe completed man : doctor :: woman : ? with nurse and woman : nurse :: man : ? with doctor. Our word2vec showed the same ordering at the extremes (dancer and nurse most female-leaning, manager most male) despite a different corpus.

This matters when the vectors feed a system that makes decisions: a CV screener whose features place engineer nearer he has a measurable tilt before it sees a single CV. Projecting the direction out of the vectors (“hard debiasing”) reduces this measurement, but later work showed the bias largely remains recoverable from neighbourhoods, so removing one direction is not a fix. Use case: search over a parts catalogue full of codes and typos

A distributor’s search has to match “hydrolic pump”, “hydraulic pumps” and “hyd. pump”. Word2vec vectors trained on the catalogue have nothing for the misspelling and treat “pumps” separately from “pump”. fastText vectors, trained on the same catalogue, give every typo and variant a vector from its pieces, and recall on a test set of 500 logged queries rises sharply. The team also sees the failure mode from this chapter: part codes that look alike, such as “RX-4410” and “RX-4401”, end up as near neighbours although they are different parts, so codes are matched exactly and only descriptions go through embeddings. They judged each change by recall on the logged queries, not by any benchmark.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Choosing embeddings by an analogy score: fastText led our three on analogies by 13 points and trailed word2vec on the real task.
  • Reading WordSim as similarity: it measures relatedness; SimLex measures similarity, and every model scored 0.2–0.3 on it.
  • Assuming subword vectors understand new words: covid came out next to ovid.
  • Training GloVe on too little text: on 50M tokens it trailed word2vec on most tests.
  • Shipping pretrained vectors without a bias check: pretrained GloVe completed man : doctor :: woman : ? with nurse.
  • Trusting one-direction debiasing to remove bias; it changes the measurement more than the neighbourhoods.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How do GloVe and fastText differ from word2vec?

How a strong answer sounds
Word2vec learns from one context window at a time by predicting neighbours. GloVe first counts how often every pair of words co-occurs in the whole corpus, then fits vectors whose dot products predict the log counts, with a weighting that stops very common pairs dominating; it is a matrix factorisation in disguise. fastText keeps word2vec’s objective but builds each word’s vector from its character n-grams, so related forms share information and unseen words still get vectors: it mapped the misspelt “witherspooon” next to “witherspoon”. That also means it can be fooled by spelling, putting “covid” next to “ovid”. Measured on the same text, fastText was far better on syntactic analogies (68% against 44% for word2vec) and much worse on semantic ones.

Deeper: How would you choose between several sets of pretrained embeddings for a production classifier?

How a strong answer sounds
On the production task, not on benchmarks. I would check vocabulary coverage on my own text first, then plug each set into the same model and compare validation scores, ideally with a few seeds. Intrinsic benchmarks are useful for diagnosing problems but do not predict task performance well: in one comparison fastText led three sets of vectors on analogies by 13 points but trailed word2vec by 2.5 on sentence sentiment. If my text has typos or rare compounds, subword vectors help; if it is domain-specific, vectors trained on in-domain text often beat larger general ones. And I would run a bias check, such as projecting occupation or name lists onto gender or ethnicity directions, because pretrained vectors encode associations like doctor-to-nurse that a downstream model will inherit.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • GloVe fits vectors to log co-occurrence counts with a weighting function; only non-zero pairs are used (3.1% here).
  • fastText sums character n-gram vectors: unseen and misspelt words get vectors, but from spelling, not meaning.
  • Benchmarks disagree: fastText led syntactic analogies, word2vec led WordSim, pretrained GloVe led semantic analogies.
  • Intrinsic scores did not predict the real task; evaluate embeddings where you will use them.
  • Embeddings absorb social bias: pretrained GloVe completed man : doctor :: woman : ? with nurse.
