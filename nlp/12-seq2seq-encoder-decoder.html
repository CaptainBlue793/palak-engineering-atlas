Seq2Seq: Encoder–Decoder RNNs

Translation, summarisation and question rewriting all map one sequence to another of a different length. The sequence-to-sequence model (Sutskever et al., 2014; Cho et al., 2014) does it with two networks: an encoder reads the input and compresses it into a vector, and a decoder, a language model conditioned on that vector, writes the output one token at a time.

This chapter trains one on Multi30k, 29,000 German–English image-caption pairs, and measures the choices that every sequence generator makes. Without attention it reached 22.36 BLEU on the test set; beam search added 0.8. On sentences of 20 words or more it fell to 11.58, less than half its score on short ones, which is the single context vector running out of room.

Encoder and decoder

The encoder is a bidirectional GRU over the source tokens; its final forward and backward states are concatenated and projected into the decoder’s initial hidden state, the context vector. The decoder is a GRU language model over target tokens that starts from that state: at each step it reads the previous target token, updates its state, and predicts the next token with a softmax over the target vocabulary, stopping when it emits </s>. Everything is trained end to end with cross-entropy on the target tokens.

Teacher forcing

During training, the decoder could be fed its own previous prediction or the gold previous token. Feeding the gold token, teacher forcing, makes training stable and parallel across a batch, because one early mistake does not derail the rest of the sentence. The cost is a mismatch: at test time the model never sees gold tokens, only its own, which may be wrong. Training sometimes mixes the two (scheduled sampling). Both choices are measured below.

Trained for 8 epochs each, the model scored 21.84 BLEU with full teacher forcing, 22.27 when half the time it was fed its own previous prediction, and 15.13 when it was never given the gold token. Training only on its own guesses is much worse: early in training those guesses are nonsense, so the decoder learns from garbage. The half-and-half run’s 0.4-point edge is one seed and within the noise of this setup; the clear lesson is that some gold input is essential.

The bottleneck vector

The decoder knows the source only through one vector of 512 numbers, whatever the sentence’s length. Multi30k captions are short (12.6 German tokens on average), so it mostly copes: test BLEU 22.36. Split by source length, though, the score holds at 25.29 and 24.5 up to 15 words, slips to 20.12 at 15–20, and falls to 11.58 for the 53 sentences of 20 words or more, 53% below the 10–15-word level. The same architecture with attention (Chapter 13) scored 35.08 overall and 24.92 on those long sentences.

Greedy and beam search

Generating means choosing the output sequence with the highest probability, a product of one probability per token. Greedy decoding takes the single most likely token at each step; it is fast but can commit to a word that leads nowhere good. Beam search keeps the k most probable partial sequences at every step and extends each; with k = 1 it is greedy. Because every extra token multiplies in another probability below one, beam search favours short outputs, so scores are divided by lengthα (length normalisation). The lab shows a small case where greedy misses the best sentence and a beam of 2 finds it.

Exposure bias

Teacher forcing trains the decoder on gold prefixes; at test time it reads its own. Measured on the test set with the attention model, the per-token loss was 2.144 when fed the gold previous tokens and 6.363 when fed its own predictions, 3.0 times higher. Part of that gap is mechanical (once the model’s sentence drifts by one word, it is scored against gold words in the wrong positions), but it is the same mechanism that makes generation fail: an early wrong word puts the decoder into states it never saw in training. Remedies include scheduled sampling, sequence-level training, and simply a better model whose own prefixes are usually right.

Use case: turning customer messages into search queries

A shop rewrites long customer messages (“hi, I bought the blue kettle last month and the lid won’t close properly, what can I do”) into short search queries (“kettle lid won’t close”) for its help centre. With 40,000 message–query pairs from agents’ logs, a small seq2seq model works well for short messages and falls apart on long ones: the single context vector cannot hold a paragraph, and the decoder starts inventing products. Beam search with length normalisation stops it producing one-word queries; attention (Chapter 13) fixes the long messages; and greedy is kept for the live system because beam search triples the latency for a small gain.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Training without teacher forcing: fed only its own guesses, the model reached 15.13 BLEU against 21.84 with gold inputs.
  • Trusting one average for every length: 22.36 BLEU overall hid 11.58 on sentences of 20 words or more; always split by length.
  • Beam search without length normalisation: it prefers short outputs, because every extra token lowers the product.
  • Evaluating with teacher forcing: the loss looks good because the model is never fed its own mistakes.
  • Not reversing the backward GRU’s state correctly when building the context vector from a bidirectional encoder.
  • Reading BLEU as quality: see Chapter 14 for what it misses.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How does a sequence-to-sequence model translate a sentence?

How a strong answer sounds
An encoder, here a bidirectional GRU, reads the German sentence and its final states are squeezed into one context vector. A decoder, a GRU language model, starts from that vector and writes English one token at a time: read the previous word, update the state, predict a distribution over the next word, and stop at the end token. It is trained end to end with cross-entropy, feeding the gold previous word during training (teacher forcing). At inference you search: greedy takes the best word each step, beam search keeps the k best partial sentences. On Multi30k this reached 22.36 BLEU greedy and 23.17 with a beam of 5; its weakness is the fixed-size vector, which on 20-plus-word sentences fell to 11.58 BLEU. Attention fixes that.

Deeper: What is exposure bias, and what can you do about it?

How a strong answer sounds
During training the decoder is fed gold previous tokens; at inference it is fed its own, so it meets prefixes, including its own mistakes, that it never saw in training, and errors compound. On this model the test loss was 2.144 with gold prefixes and 6.363 with its own. Fixes: scheduled sampling, which gradually replaces gold tokens with the model’s own during training (half-and-half here gave 22.27 against 21.84, within noise); sequence-level objectives such as minimum-risk training or reinforcement learning on BLEU; beam search, which recovers from some early mistakes; and, most effective in practice, a stronger model, since exposure bias matters less when the model’s prefixes are usually right.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • An encoder compresses the source into a context vector; a decoder language model writes the target from it.
  • Measured: 22.36 BLEU greedy and 23.17 with beam 5; beam search is worth about a point.
  • Measured: by source length BLEU fell from 24.5 (10–15 words) to 11.58 (20+): the single vector is a bottleneck.
  • Measured: teacher forcing matters; with none the model reached 15.13 against 21.84, and test loss rose from 2.144 to 6.363 when it was fed its own predictions.
  • One fixed vector is a bottleneck for long inputs; attention, next chapter, removes it.
