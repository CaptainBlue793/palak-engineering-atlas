Data Augmentation & Weak Supervision

Labels are the expensive part of most NLP projects. Two families of tricks promise more training signal for less labelling: augmentation makes new examples from the labelled ones by perturbing the text, and weak supervision labels unlabelled data with cheap, noisy rules and then reconciles their votes. Both can help, and both can quietly teach the model something false.

This chapter measures both against the honest baseline: just label more. With 500 labelled phrases, the best augmentation, random deletion, lifted DistilBERT from 82.53% to 84.14%, while back-translation made one run collapse to 75.11%. Nine keyword rules labelled 41.8% of 25,000 IMDB reviews at about 89% accuracy, and a classifier trained on those weak labels scored 84.7%, between what 500 and 2,000 hand labels achieve.

  • Apply synonym replacement, random deletion and back-translation
  • Check whether an augmentation preserves the label
  • Write labelling functions and measure coverage, accuracy and conflict
  • Combine noisy votes with majority vote and a Dawid–Skene label model
  • Compare weak labels with a given number of gold labels

Augmenting text

Images survive a flip or a crop; text is fragile, because changing one word can reverse its meaning. Three standard augmentations, each applied once to every training phrase:

  • Synonym replacement (from EDA, Wei and Zou, 2019): swap 15% of words for a WordNet synonym. WordNet does not know the word’s sense, so “fine” may become “mulct”.
  • Random deletion: drop each word with probability 0.15.
  • Back-translation (Sennrich et al., 2016): translate into German and back, which paraphrases while usually keeping meaning.

A label-preserving check: run a strong sentiment model (a “teacher”) on each original and its augmentation and count how often its prediction flips.

Weak supervision

A labelling function (LF) is a rule that votes positive, negative or abstains: “if the review contains a rating of 7/10 or more, vote positive”. Each LF covers only some documents and is sometimes wrong; LFs disagree. Majority vote takes the most common vote. A label model such as Dawid and Skene’s (1979), the idea behind Snorkel, estimates each LF’s accuracy from how it agrees with the others, without any gold labels, and weights votes accordingly. A classifier trained on the resulting labels then generalises beyond the rules’ coverage.

For TF-IDF, augmentation did nothing useful: 66.97 without it and 66.28–67.43 with. A bag of words learns nothing new from a copy of a phrase with one word swapped or dropped. For DistilBERT the effects were small and noisy. Random deletion helped most (82.53 → 84.14, all three seeds above the unaugmented mean); synonym replacement barely moved it (83.02); back-translation had the best single run (86.12) and the worst (75.11), so its mean (81.72) is meaningless on three seeds. The label check explains part of it: the teacher model changed its prediction for 7.0% of back-translated phrases and 6.0% of shortened ones, so a few augmented examples now carry the wrong label. WordNet synonyms flipped only 1.8%, but produce oddities such as “a stirring visual sequence” → “a arouse visual sequence”; back-translation paraphrases more naturally (“a stirring visual sequence” → “a moving visual sequence”). Pre-trained models already know what augmentation tries to teach; the gains that survive are small.

Nine rules written in a few minutes each covered 41.8% of the reviews; only 3.4% of covered reviews got conflicting votes. Precision was high and coverage low: the rating rule (“7/10” or better) was right 97.3% of the time but fired on only 5.6% of reviews, while the broad word-count rule reached 25.8% at 90.3%. Majority vote labelled 40.8% of reviews at 89.7% accuracy. The Dawid–Skene label model, written here, labelled slightly more (41.8%) and was slightly less accurate (88.9%). Its accuracy estimates for each rule, learnt without any gold labels, were often far off: it put “ends with a recommendation” at 87.4% against a true 70.5%. With few, mostly non-overlapping rules there is little agreement to learn from, so a label model has nothing to add over majority vote; it pays off with many overlapping, unevenly reliable rules.

Weak labels against gold labels

The test that matters: train TF-IDF + logistic regression on the weak labels and score it on the 25,000 gold-labelled test reviews. Trained on 10,197 majority-vote labels it scored 84.7%, and on 10,448 Dawid–Skene labels 84.44%. The same classifier trained on 500 gold labels scored 81.18%, on 2,000 86.01%, and on all 25,000 90.62%. So nine rules were worth somewhere between 500 and 2,000 hand labels: real value, since the rules took less time to write than 2,000 reviews take to read, and the classifier generalised beyond the 42% of reviews the rules covered. But they were far from a full labelled set, and the ceiling of weak supervision is set by the rules’ blind spots, which all the weakly labelled reviews share.

Use case: flagging urgent emails in a legal team

A legal-operations team wants to flag emails that mention a court deadline. Nobody has time to label thousands. An engineer writes twelve labelling functions (dates near “hearing” or “filing”, court names, “urgent” from known counsel), labels 300 emails by hand as a test set, and measures each rule’s coverage and accuracy on them. A label model combines the rules over 80,000 unlabelled emails and a classifier trained on the result catches deadlines phrased in ways no rule mentions. When the classifier misses something, they write a new rule rather than relabelling, and the 300 gold emails stay the measure of progress.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Augmenting without checking labels: 7.0% of back-translated phrases changed the teacher’s prediction here.
  • Judging augmentation on one seed: back-translation gave 86.12 and 75.11 on two runs of the same setup.
  • Expecting a label model to beat majority vote by default: with nine sparse rules it was slightly worse (88.9% vs 89.7%).
  • Evaluating on augmented or weakly labelled data: the test set must be clean gold labels.
  • Writing labelling functions while looking at the test set: they will overfit it; develop on a separate labelled sample.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is weak supervision and when is it worth it?

How a strong answer sounds
Weak supervision replaces hand labels with cheap, noisy ones: write labelling functions (keywords, patterns, heuristics, existing models, knowledge bases), apply them to a large unlabelled set, combine their votes with majority vote or a label model, and train a normal classifier on the result, which then generalises beyond what the rules cover. It is worth it when labels are expensive, domain experts can state rules faster than they can label, and plenty of unlabelled data exists. A small gold set is still essential to measure it. Here nine rules took IMDB from no labels to a classifier at 84.7%, between what 500 and 2,000 gold labels gave (81.18% and 86.01%), and well short of 25,000 (90.62%).

Deeper: You have 500 labelled examples. Would you augment, write labelling functions, or label more? How would you decide?

How a strong answer sounds
Measure before deciding, on a held-out gold set and over several seeds. Augmentation is quick to try but usually small for pre-trained models: here random deletion added about 1.6 points to DistilBERT at 500 labels, back-translation was unstable, and TF-IDF gained nothing; check that augmented examples keep their labels. Labelling functions are worth it if there are cheap, reliable signals in the data (ratings, keywords, metadata) and lots of unlabelled text; here nine rules were worth roughly 500–2,000 labels. Labelling more is the most reliable: the classifier went from 81.18% at 500 labels to 86.01% at 2,000. I would estimate the cost per label, run a small learning-curve experiment, and usually do a bit of each: label a few hundred more, especially where the model is unsure, and use rules to extend coverage.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Measured: with 500 labels, random deletion lifted DistilBERT from 82.53% to 84.14%; synonym replacement and back-translation did not reliably help, and TF-IDF gained nothing.
  • Measured: nine labelling functions covered 41.8% of IMDB at about 90% accuracy; Dawid–Skene did not beat majority vote.
  • Measured: a classifier on weak labels scored 84.7%, against 81.18% for 500 gold labels, 86.01% for 2,000 and 90.62% for 25,000.
  • Check that an augmentation preserves the label before training on it.
  • Weak supervision still needs gold labels: to measure, not to train.
