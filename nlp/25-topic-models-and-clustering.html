Topic Models & Clustering Text

Sometimes nobody has labelled the documents and the question is simply “what is in here?”. Topic models and clustering answer it without labels: they group documents and describe each group by its words. The output always looks like an answer. Whether it is a good one, and whether you would get the same one twice, has to be measured.

This chapter runs three methods on 20 Newsgroups, where the human grouping is known, and scores them. The most coherent topic LDA found, by the standard NPMI score, was max · bhj · giz · bxn · air · qax: fragments of encoded binary files in 14 posts. Sentence-embedding clusters matched the human newsgroups far better than LDA (NMI 0.582 against 0.382), and rerunning LDA with a different seed reproduced only some of its topics.

LDA: documents as mixtures of topics

Latent Dirichlet Allocation (Blei, Ng and Jordan, 2003) imagines each document written like this: draw a mix of topics (70% space, 30% politics); for each word, pick a topic from that mix, then pick a word from that topic’s word distribution. A topic is just a probability distribution over the vocabulary. Inference runs the story backwards: given only the words, find topic–word distributions and per-document mixes that explain them. Word order is ignored entirely.

NMF factorises the TF-IDF matrix into documents × topics and topics × words with non-negative entries; it is faster and often crisper. Embedding clustering embeds each post with a sentence encoder, runs k-means, and describes each cluster by class-based TF-IDF (words frequent in that cluster and rare elsewhere), the recipe behind BERTopic.

Scoring topics without labels

NPMI coherence asks whether a topic’s top words actually occur together: for each pair, normalised pointwise mutual information over document co-occurrence, from −1 (never together) to 1 (always together). It tracks human judgements of topic quality better than perplexity does. Diversity is the share of unique words across all topics’ top-10 lists: low diversity means topics repeat each other. Because 20 Newsgroups has labels, we can also compute NMI between each post’s main topic and its newsgroup.

Measured: how many topics?

Across K = 5, 10, 20, 40, 80, held-out perplexity was lowest at K = 5 (3,338) and rose steadily to 5,064 at K = 80: by that measure fewer topics are always better here. NPMI coherence disagreed, rising from 0.199 at K = 5 to a peak of 0.288 at K = 40 and falling to 0.236 at K = 80, where 9 of the 80 topics had negative coherence (top words that rarely appear together). Diversity fell as K grew (0.84 → 0.76): more topics means more of them repeat each other’s words. Agreement with the 20 newsgroups barely moved (NMI 0.342–0.385). Perplexity, coherence and the human labels each point to a different K, which is the honest state of the art: choose K for what the topics will be used for, check coherence, and read them (Chang et al., 2009, found perplexity can even anti-correlate with human judgements of topics).

NMF produced the most coherent and least repetitive word lists (0.383 NPMI, 0.92 diversity) and was the fastest. Embedding clusters grouped the posts most like the human newsgroups (NMI 0.582 against about 0.39 for both word-count methods): a sentence encoder knows that a post about a carburettor and one about horsepower belong together even when they share few words. Neither number says the topics are useful, and the word lists show why you must read them. LDA’s highest-NPMI topic was max · bhj · giz · bxn · air · qax, from 14 posts containing uuencoded binaries: words that always appear together get high coherence even when they are noise. NMF found a topic made of a single person’s email signature (geb · pitt · cadre · dsl · chastity · shameful). The LDA topics that are genuinely clean, such as armenian · turkish · jews · armenians · war · people (79% of its posts from talk.politics.mideast) and key · chip · encryption · keys · clipper · use, sit beside muddled ones such as bike · dod · good · van · ride · bos (NPMI -0.089). Preprocessing (stripping headers, signatures, quoted replies and encoded attachments) would remove most of the junk; we used the posts as distributed, to show what happens without it.

Are the topics stable?

Run LDA again with a different random seed and the topics change. Matching each of the 20 topics from seed 0 to its most similar topic from seeds 1 and 2 (Hungarian matching on the overlap of their top-10 words), the mean Jaccard similarity was 0.412, and 30% of topics had no counterpart sharing even 30% of their words. The strong topics (cryptography, the Middle East, hard drives) come back every time; the weak ones are artefacts of one particular run. A topic that does not survive a change of seed should not be presented as a finding.

Use case: what are customers complaining about?

A product team has 40,000 free-text survey answers and asks for “the main themes”. An analyst runs a topic model, picks K by eye, and presents twelve topics. Before they go into a strategy deck, the analyst reruns with three seeds (and drops topics that do not reappear), reports NPMI per topic, reads 20 answers from each surviving topic, and names topics from those answers rather than from the word lists. Four of the twelve topics turn out to be artefacts (a signature template, two near-duplicates, a stop-word soup), and the deck shows eight themes with a count and real quotes for each.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Choosing K by perplexity: here it favoured the fewest topics (K = 5) while coherence peaked at K = 40.
  • Trusting coherence alone: the highest-NPMI LDA topic was encoded-file junk (max · bhj · giz · bxn).
  • Reporting topics from a single run: 30% of LDA topics had no close match after changing only the seed.
  • Naming topics from their word lists alone: read documents assigned to the topic before you name it.
  • Skipping preprocessing: headers, signatures and boilerplate become topics if you leave them in.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What does a topic model produce, and how would you know if it is any good?

How a strong answer sounds
A topic model gives each document a mixture of topics and each topic a distribution over words, summarised by its top words. Without labels, “good” has three parts. Coherence: do a topic’s top words co-occur in real documents? NPMI measures that, but it can reward junk: here the most coherent LDA topic was uuencoded binary fragments. Distinctiveness: do topics differ from each other (topic diversity)? Stability: do the topics come back with a different random seed? Here 30% did not. Then the real test is reading documents assigned to each topic, and, if any labelled data exists, checking agreement with it, which was NMI 0.382 for LDA against 0.582 for clustering sentence embeddings on 20 Newsgroups.

Deeper: LDA or embedding clustering for 100,000 short support tickets? Justify it.

How a strong answer sounds
Embedding clustering, with LDA as a baseline. Support tickets are short, and LDA’s bag-of-words statistics are weak on short texts: two tickets about the same problem may share almost no words. A sentence encoder puts them close anyway. On 20 Newsgroups, MiniLM embeddings plus k-means matched the human groups at NMI 0.582 against 0.382 for LDA. I would describe each cluster with class-based TF-IDF words plus a few representative tickets, merge or split clusters by inspection, rerun with several seeds and keep clusters that are stable, and track cluster sizes over time to spot new issues. LDA, or NMF, which gave the most coherent word lists here (0.383 NPMI), stays useful when you want each ticket to be a mixture of topics, or when explaining topics through words matters more than grouping.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Measured: perplexity favoured K = 5, NPMI coherence K = 40, and the newsgroup labels barely cared (NMI 0.342–0.385).
  • Measured: at K = 20, NMF had the most coherent topics (0.383), embedding clusters the best match to human groups (NMI 0.582 vs LDA 0.382).
  • Measured: the most coherent LDA topic was encoded-file junk, and 30% of topics did not survive a change of seed.
  • A topic is a distribution over words; its top words are a summary, not a definition.
  • Score coherence, diversity and stability, then read the documents.
