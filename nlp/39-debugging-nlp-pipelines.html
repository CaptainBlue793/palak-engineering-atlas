Debugging NLP Pipelines & Datasets

Most NLP bugs raise no error. A model loaded with the wrong tokeniser still returns predictions; a pipeline that truncates every review at 128 tokens still reports an accuracy; a label map with two classes swapped still trains. The symptoms are numbers that are slightly, or very, wrong, and the skill is knowing which bug produces which symptom.

This chapter plants seven common bugs in working pipelines and measures exactly what each one does. Three bugs turned working models into coin tosses: the wrong tokeniser (91.06% → 48.74% on SST-2), lower-casing text before a cased NER model (F1 91.29 → 28.93), and two swapped labels (92.36% → 50.66% on AG News). Others were quieter: truncating IMDB at 128 tokens cost 5.2 points, and test-set leakage added only 0.9 points overall while scoring 100.0% on the leaked reviews. Flipping 10% of SST-2’s training labels did no measurable harm at all.

  • Recognise the symptoms of tokeniser, casing and padding bugs
  • Detect silent truncation and measure what it costs
  • Find test-set leakage before it inflates a result
  • Spot a swapped label map from a confusion matrix
  • Find mislabelled training examples by their loss

Seven planted bugs

  • Tokeniser mismatch: an uncased DistilBERT fed IDs from a cased BERT tokeniser, or from RoBERTa’s, which uses a different vocabulary entirely.
  • Casing: text lower-cased by an over-eager preprocessing step before a cased NER model.
  • Forgotten attention mask: padded batches sent without the mask, so the model attends to padding.
  • Silent truncation: a max_length set for short sentences reused on long reviews.
  • Leakage: 2,000 test reviews accidentally included in training.
  • Swapped label map: Business and Sci/Tech exchanged when building training labels.
  • Label noise: 10% of training labels flipped, then hunted down by training loss.

A tokeniser mismatch produced no error: the IDs were valid integers and the model returned confident predictions, at 48.74% and 49.08%, chance on a balanced task. Decoding the cased tokeniser’s IDs for “it 's a charming and often affecting journey .” with the model’s own tokeniser shows why: “ɯ”, “[unused107]”, “[unused183]”, “[unused165]”, “lamps”, “ł”… The model was reading a different language. Lower-casing CoNLL text before a cased NER model was nearly as bad (F1 91.29 → 28.93): capital letters are most of how it finds names. Forgetting the attention mask cost little on SST-2 (91.06% → 90.6%), because its sentences are short and batches carry little padding; with no padding at all (batch 1) the mask makes no difference. On longer, more varied inputs the same bug does more damage, and it always makes a prediction depend on what else is in the batch (P2).

Measured: truncation

On 2,000 IMDB test reviews, max_length 512 truncated 11.4% of them and scored 89.4%; a max_length of 128, a common default copied from short-sentence tasks, truncated 86.8% and scored 84.2%; 32 truncated every review and scored 71.2%. None of these raised a warning in the pipeline. The check is cheap: count how many inputs come out exactly at max_length, and compare accuracy on those with the rest (Chapter 34 covers what to do about long inputs).

Measured: leakage

Adding 2,000 of the 25,000 IMDB test reviews to the training set of a TF-IDF model raised its overall test accuracy from 90.58% to 91.5%, a gain small enough to pass as a real improvement. Split by review, the leak is obvious: the leaked reviews scored 100.0% (the model, with weak regularisation, memorised them), the unseen ones 90.76%, and the clean model scored 90.55% on the same 2,000. The overall number was inflated by the 8% of the test set that had been seen. Leakage in real projects is usually subtler than copying (near-duplicates, the same user or product in both splits, test-period data in the training query, P8), so search for near-duplicates between splits and split by the unit that matters (user, document, time).

Measured: a swapped label map

Swapping Business and Sci/Tech when building the AG News training labels dropped accuracy from 92.36% to 50.66%. The confusion matrix makes the cause unmistakable: 1,695 of the Business articles were predicted Sci/Tech and 1,726 of the Sci/Tech articles Business, while World and Sports stayed at 91.4% and 97.9%. Two classes predicted almost entirely as each other is the signature of a label-map bug, not of a hard task; a model that confuses two classes for real spreads its errors far more evenly. Reading ten training examples per label would have caught it before training.

Measured: finding noisy labels

A model trained on noisy labels tends to learn the general pattern before it memorises the exceptions, so mislabelled examples keep a high training loss (Arazo et al., 2019; the idea behind confident learning, Northcutt et al., 2021). Sorting by loss and reading from the top is one of the highest-value hours in any NLP project.

Flipping 10% of SST-2’s training labels (6,848 phrases) and fine-tuning DistilBERT for one epoch gave 90.37% on validation, against 90.14% with clean labels: no measurable harm. With one epoch and a pre-trained model, the network learnt the consistent pattern and did not have time to memorise the random exceptions. The loss still found them: of the 6,848 highest-loss training examples, 82.1% were the flipped ones. Removing them and retraining gave 90.37%, the same accuracy, which is expected when the noise was doing no damage. The flagged examples that were not planted flips are the interesting ones: some are ambiguous phrases (“smart-aleck”), and some look like errors in SST-2 itself, such as “a damn fine and a truly distinctive and a deeply pertinent film” labelled negative. Sorting by loss is a quick way to audit a dataset’s labels, even when the noise is not hurting the model.

Use case: a model that was great in testing

A team’s new ticket classifier scored 96% offline and 81% in its first week live. The post-mortem finds two bugs: the training set had been built by a query that also pulled in a month of the test period, so near-duplicate tickets sat on both sides, and the production service loaded a newer tokeniser version whose vocabulary had changed. Neither raised an error. The team adds a deduplication check between splits to the data pipeline, pins the tokeniser to the model artefact, and adds a test that decodes a fixed input’s token IDs and compares them with a stored copy on every deploy.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Loading a tokeniser from a different checkpoint than the model: 91.06% became 48.74% with no error raised.
  • Lower-casing text in preprocessing for a cased model: NER F1 fell from 91.29 to 28.93.
  • Reading a small overall gain as an improvement without checking for leakage: +0.9 points overall hid 100.0% on leaked reviews.
  • Loading the tokeniser and model from different names or versions: save and load them together.
  • Never reading the data: twenty minutes reading inputs and high-loss examples finds bugs no metric will.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Your fine-tuned model scores near chance. What do you check first?

How a strong answer sounds
Check the data path before the model. Decode the token IDs the model actually receives with its own tokeniser and read them: a mismatched tokeniser gave 48.74% here, and its decoded input was nonsense. Check the label map: print ten examples per label and a confusion matrix; two swapped classes gave 50.66%. Check the first loss is about ln(number of classes) and that the model can overfit one small batch; if it cannot, the pipeline is broken. Then learning rate (Chapter 17: 3·10−4 collapsed BERT to the majority class) and preprocessing such as casing for cased models.

Deeper: Offline accuracy is 95%, production accuracy 80%. Walk through your investigation.

How a strong answer sounds
First reproduce: run a sample of production inputs through the offline pipeline and the serving path and compare token IDs exactly; tokeniser versions, casing, Unicode normalisation and truncation differ more often than models do. Then check whether the offline number is real: search for duplicates and near-duplicates between training and test sets, and check that the split respects users, time and documents; here 2,000 leaked reviews inflated a test score while scoring 100.0% themselves. Then label a few hundred production examples and slice errors by source, length and time to see whether the production data has drifted (Chapter 38) or contains classes or styles the test set lacked. Check lengths against max_length (86.8% of IMDB reviews exceed 128 tokens). Only then consider the model.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Measured: a mismatched tokeniser (48.74%), a lower-cased input to a cased NER model (F1 28.93) and a swapped label map (50.66%) all failed silently.
  • Measured: max_length 128 on IMDB cut 86.8% of reviews and 5.2 points; leakage added 0.9 points overall from reviews scored at 100.0%.
  • Measured: 10% flipped labels did not hurt one-epoch DistilBERT, and the highest-loss examples were 82.1% planted flips, with genuine dataset errors among the rest.
  • Most NLP bugs are silent; decode inputs, check lengths and read the data.
  • Sort training examples by loss and read the top: that is where label errors hide.
