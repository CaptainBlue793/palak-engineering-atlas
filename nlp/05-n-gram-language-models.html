N-gram Language Models & Smoothing

A language model gives a probability to any sequence of words, or equivalently predicts the next word from the ones before it. For thirty years the best way to do that was to count: how often does “Francisco” follow “San”? Counting works until it meets something it has never seen, which in language is almost always, and the clever part of an n-gram model is what it does then.

This chapter writes n-gram models with add-k and Kneser–Ney smoothing from scratch, trains them on WikiText, and measures them with perplexity, the same quantity used to judge GPT. Without smoothing, every model beyond unigrams gave the test text infinite perplexity. Add-one smoothing made trigrams worse than ignoring context altogether. Kneser–Ney brought the trigram to 242, and adding a fourth or fifth word of context made it worse, not better. Fifteen times more training text cut perplexity from 849 to 226.

N-gram probabilities

By the chain rule, P(w1 … wT) = Πt P(wt | w1 … wt−1). The full history is too long to count, so an n-gram model assumes each word depends only on the previous n − 1 (the Markov assumption): a bigram model uses one previous word, a trigram model two. The maximum-likelihood estimate is a ratio of counts, exactly as in P4:

P(w | u v) = count(u v w) / count(u v)

On WikiText-2, 2.1 million words of Wikipedia with a 33,278-word vocabulary (rarer words are already replaced by <unk>), the counts show how much a two-word context narrows the next word. After “one of”, the followed 63% of the time, and only 145 different words ever followed. After “in the”, 2,726 different words followed, and the most likely, United, had just 3.3%.

Perplexity

A language model is judged by how probable it finds real text it has not seen. Perplexity is the exponential of the average negative log-probability per word: PPL = exp(−(1/N) Σ log P(wt | history)). It is the cross-entropy loss of P6 and P4, exponentiated, and it reads as an effective branching factor: a perplexity of 150 means the model is, on average, as uncertain as if choosing uniformly among 150 words. Lower is better, and a single word given probability zero makes it infinite.

def perplexity(prob, test, n):
    lp = 0.0
    for i in range(4, len(test)):                          # same tokens scored for every order
        p = prob(tuple(test[i - n + 1:i]), test[i])
        if p == 0: return float("inf")                     # one unseen event and it is over
        lp += math.log(p)
    return math.exp(-lp / (len(test) - 4))

Add-k and Kneser–Ney smoothing

The trouble is that most n-grams in new text have never been seen. Of the test set’s bigrams, 20.6% never occur in the 2.1 million training words; of its trigrams, 59.1%; of its 4-grams, 84.5%; of its 5-grams, 94.4%. Every one of them gets probability zero under maximum likelihood, so the unsmoothed bigram, trigram and 4-gram models all gave the test set infinite perplexity. Smoothing takes some probability from seen events and gives it to unseen ones.

Add-k smoothing pretends every possible next word was seen k extra times: P(w | h) = (count(h w) + k) / (count(h) + kV). With k = 1 (Laplace) and a vocabulary of tens of thousands, that moves far too much probability to the unseen; tuning k on validation data helps but does not fix the shape.

Kneser–Ney smoothing fixes two things. It subtracts a fixed discount d (here 0.75) from every seen count and gives the saved mass to a lower-order model, so frequent events keep most of their probability and rare ones lend most of theirs. And its lowest-order distribution is not “how often does w occur” but “in how many different contexts does w occur”, its continuation count. “Francisco” is a common word, but it appears almost only after “San”, so as a guess for an unseen context it should be unlikely; continuation counts say exactly that. Interpolated KN mixes every order down to that base:

PKN(w | h) = max(c(h w) − d, 0) / c(h) + d · N1+(h •) / c(h) · PKN(w | h′)

where h′ drops the oldest word of h and N1+(h •) is the number of distinct words seen after h, so a history followed by many different words keeps more mass for the unseen. The lab below computes all three estimates live on any text you type.

Add-one smoothing is worse than useless for higher orders: the add-1 trigram (12,032) was 13 times worse than the unigram model (926), which ignores context entirely, because it hands every trigram context 33,278 imaginary counts, swamping the few real ones. Tuning k on validation data helped a lot (the bigram fell to 477) but still got worse with every order added. Kneser–Ney got better with order up to the trigram: 926, 272, 242. Then it got slightly worse, 248 for 4-grams and 252 for 5-grams. With only 2 million training words, 85% of test 4-grams are unseen, so the extra context is almost always backed off, and the discounting costs a little each time. More context only pays when there is enough data to see it.

Backoff and more data

Two ways to combine orders. Interpolation, as in Kneser–Ney above, always mixes all orders with weights. Backoff uses the highest order that has been seen and falls back only when it has not, with the lower order scaled so the probabilities still sum to one (Katz backoff). Google’s stupid backoff (Brants et al., 2007) drops the scaling and multiplies by a constant 0.4 at each step: the scores no longer form a probability distribution, which rules out perplexity, but with trillions of words it worked as well as Kneser–Ney for translation and is trivially parallel.

Data is the other lever. Kneser–Ney trigrams trained on growing prefixes of WikiText-103 and scored on its own test set (with its own, much larger, 267,000-word vocabulary, so these perplexities are not comparable with the WikiText-2 ones above):

Fifteen times more text cut perplexity by almost four times and the unseen-trigram share from 67% to 42.5%. But the model grew with the data: 15 million distinct trigrams at 30 million words, roughly one stored n-gram for every two training words. That is the n-gram model’s limit. Its memory grows with its data and it never generalises between similar contexts: seeing “the cat sat on the mat” says nothing about “the dog sat on the rug”. Neural language models fix exactly that; Chapter 9 trains an LSTM on the same WikiText-2 text to compare.

Generating text from counts

A language model can generate: sample a word from P(w | context), append it, repeat. The samples below were drawn from raw WikiText-2 counts, starting at a real line start.

  • Trigram: “Though Frank recovered from their honeymoon . ↵ The most common purposes of ordination . There is no longer had the sixth @-@ place in anticipation of progressive opinion " . This variation …”
  • 4-gram: “D.J. Cotrona as <unk> R. <unk> / Flint ↵ Élodie Yung as Kim Arashikage / Jinx ↵ Bruce Willis as General Joseph Colton , to overthrow the Cobra Commander and to avenge Duke . Roadblock proudl…”

The bigram text is grammatical for two or three words at a time and then wanders: “Nicole Kidman on evidence, selling it back in the opening sequence”. The trigram is locally fluent and globally meaningless. The 4-gram reads best, and that is the problem: 63.5% of its 8-word runs appear word for word in the training text, against 0.0% for the trigram. With 2 million words most 3-word contexts have only one continuation, so a 4-gram model mostly recites, here a G.I. Joe cast list. Fluency from counting comes from copying; Chapter 23 and the LLMs course return to the same tension in modern models.

🧾
Use case: keyboard suggestions on a low-end phone

A phone keyboard must suggest the next word in under 10 milliseconds, offline, in a few megabytes, for languages with little training data. A pruned trigram model with Kneser–Ney smoothing, stored in a compact trie, does exactly that, and it is what keyboards shipped for years. The engineers prune n-grams seen fewer than a few times, which shrinks the model tenfold for a small perplexity cost, keep a per-user cache of recent words interpolated with the global model so a user’s own names and slang appear quickly, and measure suggestion acceptance rather than perplexity, because users care whether the top three guesses include the right word. Neural models now do the same job on newer phones, but the counting model is still the fallback and the baseline.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Using maximum-likelihood n-grams on new text: 59% of test trigrams were unseen, so the unsmoothed model’s perplexity was infinite.
  • Add-one smoothing for anything beyond unigrams: the add-1 trigram scored 12,032, thirteen times worse than no context at all.
  • Assuming a longer context always helps: with 2 million words, Kneser–Ney 4- and 5-grams were worse than trigrams.
  • Comparing perplexities across different vocabularies or tokenisers: a model with a smaller vocabulary, or more <unk>, gets a lower perplexity for free.
  • Using raw frequency as the lowest-order distribution: it over-predicts words like “Francisco” that are common but only in one context.
  • Computing perplexity with stupid backoff: its scores are not probabilities.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is perplexity, and why is it used to evaluate language models?

How a strong answer sounds
Perplexity is the exponential of the average negative log-probability a model assigns to each word of held-out text. It is the cross-entropy loss, exponentiated, and reads as a branching factor: 242 means the model is as uncertain as choosing among 242 equally likely words. It is used because a language model’s job is to assign high probability to real text, and perplexity measures exactly that without needing labels. Two cautions: a single zero-probability word makes it infinite, so it forces you to smooth, and it is only comparable between models with the same vocabulary and test text. On WikiText-2, a unigram model scored 926 and a Kneser–Ney trigram 242.

Deeper: Why does Kneser–Ney smoothing beat add-one smoothing, and what is a continuation count?

How a strong answer sounds
Add-one adds one imaginary count to every possible next word, so with a 33,000-word vocabulary a context seen ten times is swamped by 33,000 fake counts; on WikiText-2 the add-one trigram scored 12,032, worse than a unigram model. Kneser–Ney instead subtracts a small fixed discount from each seen count, so frequent events keep most of their probability, and gives the saved mass to a lower-order model in proportion to how many different words followed the context. Its lowest-order distribution uses continuation counts, the number of distinct words a word follows, rather than raw frequency. That fixes the “Francisco” problem: a word that is frequent but appears in only one context is a bad guess for a new context. The result here was a trigram perplexity of 242, against 2,720 for the best add-k.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • An n-gram model predicts each word from the previous n − 1 by counting; perplexity is exp of the average negative log-probability on unseen text.
  • Most n-grams in new text are unseen (59% of test trigrams here), so unsmoothed models give infinite perplexity.
  • Kneser–Ney (discounting plus continuation counts) reached 242 with trigrams; add-1 made trigrams worse than unigrams; 4- and 5-grams did not help on 2M words.
  • More data helps a lot (849 → 226 from 2M to 30M words) but the model grows with the data and never generalises between similar contexts.
  • Text sampled from counts is locally fluent and globally aimless; higher orders copy their training text.
