Evaluating NLP Models Beyond Accuracy

A test-set accuracy is an average over whatever the test set happens to contain. It says nothing about how the model handles negation if the test set has little negation, and nothing about whether it changes its mind when you swap a name or add a typo. Behavioural testing probes those capabilities directly, with targeted inputs, the way unit tests probe code.

This chapter tests two sentiment models whose SST-2 accuracies differ by about eight points, and finds where each one breaks. DistilBERT passed every minimum-functionality test at 100%; TF-IDF judged “The plot was not bad.” negative in 100% of 80 variations. But appending “I really loved it.” to a negative review flipped DistilBERT’s verdict 75.3% of the time, a hypothesis-only classifier got 62.32% on SNLI without reading the premise, and DistilBERT’s 99.5%-confident predictions were right 93.4% of the time.

  • Write minimum-functionality, invariance and directional tests (CheckList)
  • Slice a test set and find where accuracy collapses
  • Build contrast pairs that differ only in the feature that should matter
  • Detect annotation artefacts with a partial-input baseline
  • Measure and fix calibration with temperature scaling

Behavioural testing with CheckList

CheckList (Ribeiro et al., 2020) borrows three test types from software engineering:

  • Minimum functionality tests (MFT): simple inputs with a known answer, generated from templates. “The {noun} was not {positive adjective}.” should be negative, for every filling.
  • Invariance tests (INV): a change that should not change the prediction: a different name, a typo, a URL.
  • Directional tests (DIR): a change that should move the prediction one way: appending “I really loved it.” should not make a review more negative.

Templates generate hundreds of cases in seconds, and the failure rate per test is a far more specific diagnosis than one accuracy number. Write a template in the lab and test a toy word-counting model with it.

The template tests separate the models far more sharply than their 8-point accuracy gap. DistilBERT passed all six minimum-functionality tests perfectly, including both contrast templates (“I thought it would be bad, but it was great.”). TF-IDF + LR, a bag of word pairs, failed exactly where word order matters: it called “The film was not bad.” and its variations negative 100% of the time (it sees “bad”), and got “I thought it would be great, but it was dull.” right only 31.2% of the time. Neither model changed its predictions when names were swapped across two groups of first names, and typos, a URL or an @handle changed 0–4% of predictions.

The directional tests found DistilBERT’s weakness. Adding “Never again.” to a positive review raised its positive probability 4.3% of the time, a small violation. More striking: appending “I really loved it.” to a negative review turned the prediction positive in 75.3% of cases, against 18.7% for TF-IDF. That is not strictly a violation, since the sentence is positive, but it shows how much one strong final sentence outweighs a whole review for the fine-tuned model; a mixed review ending on praise will be read as praise. CheckList is useful precisely because it turns such hunches into rates.

Slices and contrast pairs

Slicing SST-2 validation by simple properties shows where accuracy drops. For DistilBERT: 91.1% overall, 92.8% on sentences of 10 words or fewer, 90.2% on those over 25, 87.0% on the 184 sentences with a negation and 86.1% on the 108 with “but”. TF-IDF fell further on the same slices (75.5% with negation, 78.7% on long sentences). Slices with tens of sentences carry several points of noise, so they flag where to look rather than prove a weakness.

Contrast pairs test the same thing more directly: take a sentence, insert “not” before its sentiment adjective, and check that the prediction flips. The automatic rule found only 10 suitable sentences in the validation set, too few to measure much, but the pattern was the same: DistilBERT got both halves right in 90% of pairs and TF-IDF in 40%. Contrast sets written by people (Gardner et al., 2020) are the serious version of this test.

Annotation artefacts

Sometimes the dataset, not the model, is the problem. SNLI hypotheses were written by crowdworkers told to produce an entailed, a neutral and a contradicting sentence for each premise. People write contradictions with “not”, “nobody”, “sleeping”, and neutral sentences with “tall”, “first”, “because”. A classifier that never sees the premise can then score far above chance (Gururangan et al., 2018; Poliak et al., 2018), and a full model can lean on the same shortcuts.

Trained on 100,000 SNLI hypotheses with their premises removed, TF-IDF + logistic regression scored 62.32% on the test set, against 34.28% for always predicting the commonest label. The words it relied on are the annotators’ habits: contradiction from “no”, “nobody”, “nothing”, “cat”, “alone”; neutral from “joyously”, “for”, “sad man”, “to”, “tall” (adding a detail the premise cannot confirm); entailment from vague words such as “something”, “human”, “are near”, “is near” (generalising the premise). A model trained on full SNLI can score well partly by learning the same shortcuts, which is why later datasets (ANLI, MNLI’s mismatched sets) were built to break them.

Calibration

A model is calibrated if, of the predictions it makes with 90% confidence, 90% are right. Fine-tuned transformers are usually over-confident. Temperature scaling (Guo et al., 2017) divides the logits by one scalar T, fitted on held-out data; it cannot change any prediction, only how confident each one is.

On a held-out half of SST-2 validation, DistilBERT put 422 of its 436 predictions in the 90–100% confidence bin, with average confidence 99.5%, and those were right 93.4% of the time: over-confident. Its expected calibration error was 6.24%. A single temperature, T = 2.549, fitted on the other half, cut it to 2.5% without changing any prediction (verified: none changed). TF-IDF + logistic regression, trained with a likelihood objective on simple features, was already better calibrated (3.66%). If anything downstream uses the confidence, a threshold, a review queue, a cost, fit a temperature first.

Use case: a review-moderation model before launch

A marketplace’s model flags abusive reviews and scores 94% on its test set. Before launch, the ML team writes forty CheckList tests: negated insults (“not an idiot at all”), name swaps across seller names from different countries, typos and emoji, and quoted abuse in a complaint (“he called me a ‘liar’”). Three tests fail badly: negated insults are flagged, quoted abuse is flagged, and some seller names shift the score. The fixes are targeted training examples for each failure, and the tests become a regression suite that runs on every new model, alongside a slice report by product category.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Trusting accuracy to reveal negation handling: TF-IDF scored 82.8% overall and 0% on “The X was not NEG.”
  • Assuming a strong model reads the whole input: one appended sentence flipped 75.3% of DistilBERT’s negative verdicts.
  • Ignoring partial-input baselines: SNLI hypotheses alone gave 62.32% accuracy, almost double the majority class.
  • Testing only what you expect to work: write tests for the failures you fear, from real user complaints.
  • Thresholding uncalibrated confidences: measure ECE first; fit a temperature on held-out data.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Your model has 92% accuracy. Why is that not enough to ship it?

How a strong answer sounds
Because accuracy is an average over whatever the test set contains, and the failures that matter may be rare in it or hidden in the average. I would want behavioural tests for the capabilities the product needs, slices for the inputs that matter, and calibrated confidences. In my measurements, a TF-IDF model with 82.8% accuracy got “The film was not bad.” wrong every time, and a DistilBERT with 91.06% passed every template test but let one appended positive sentence flip 75.3% of negative reviews, and was over-confident until temperature-scaled (calibration error 6.24% → 2.5%). None of that shows in the accuracy.

Deeper: How would you discover that a dataset has annotation artefacts, and what would you do about them?

How a strong answer sounds
Train a model on partial input that should not be enough: hypotheses without premises for NLI, questions without passages for QA, answers without questions for multiple choice. If it scores well above the majority class, the dataset leaks the label; on SNLI a hypothesis-only TF-IDF model scored 62.32% against 34.28%. Then inspect what it uses (its top features: “nobody”, “never”, “sad”, “tall” here) and read examples. To deal with it: report results on harder or adversarially collected test sets (ANLI, contrast sets), rebalance or filter the training data so the cues are not predictive, train with debiasing methods that down-weight examples a bias-only model gets right, and say in any report which artefacts were checked.
