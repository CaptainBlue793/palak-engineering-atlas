Sequence Labelling: POS Tagging & NER

Many NLP tasks need a label for every token rather than one for the whole text: the part of speech of each word, or which words form a person’s name, a place or a company. The tagger reads the sentence and outputs one tag per token, and the tags are then decoded into the units people care about, such as “European Union”, ORG.

This chapter trains a BiLSTM tagger on CoNLL-2003 for both jobs and measures it the way the field does. For part of speech, the BiLSTM reached 93.92% against 88.41% for “give each word its usual tag”, and the gap was largest on words never seen in training (78.43% against 46.07%). For entities it reached 84.07 span F1 at 97.1% token accuracy. BIOES, the scheme said to help, did not (83.47). The model also produced about 140 invalid tag sequences per run, which Chapter 11 fixes.

  • Frame part-of-speech tagging and NER as one label per token
  • Encode entities with BIO and BIOES and decode them back to spans
  • Build and train a BiLSTM tagger with word and case features
  • Score taggers at the span level, and against a simple baseline
  • Classify a tagger’s errors

Part-of-speech tagging

Part-of-speech (POS) tags say what grammatical role each word plays: noun, verb, adjective and so on. CoNLL-2003 uses the 45-tag Penn Treebank set (NN for a singular noun, VBD for a past-tense verb, NNP for a proper noun) plus a few extras. Most words have one usual tag, which gives a strong baseline: tag each word with its most frequent training tag, and unseen words with the commonest tag overall. The hard cases are words with several (“book” as noun or verb, “that” as determiner or conjunction) and words never seen in training.

On the CoNLL-2003 test set the most-frequent-tag baseline was right 88.41% of the time; a BiLSTM tagger reached 93.92%. The difference is almost all in unknown words, 12.18% of test tokens: the baseline can only guess the commonest tag for them and got 46.07%, while the BiLSTM, reading the context and the word’s shape, got 78.43%. In one test sentence the baseline tagged “controlled” as a past participle (VBN) and “lob” as a noun, because those are their commonest tags; the BiLSTM read “China controlled most of the match” and “to lob the ball” and fixed both. It also disagreed with the gold label on “Igor”, tagged JJ (adjective) in the data. Both models said NNP, proper noun, which is right: the gold label is wrong.

BIO and BIOES

Entities span several tokens, so their tags must mark boundaries. In BIO, B-X begins an entity of type X, I-X continues it, O is outside: “European/B-ORG Union/I-ORG said/O”. Two adjacent entities of the same type are then distinguishable (B-PER I-PER B-PER). BIOES adds E-X for the last token and S-X for single-token entities, so every entity’s end is explicit. A tag sequence can be invalid, for instance I-PER straight after O; decoders either repair it (treat it as B-PER, as here) or forbid it (Chapter 11). Click the words in the lab to tag them and watch both encodings.

def spans(tags):                                   # BIO tags → {(start, end, type)}
    out, s, ty = set(), None, None
    for i, t in enumerate(tags + ["O"]):
        if t == "O" or t.startswith("B-") or (t.startswith("I-") and t[2:] != ty):
            if s is not None: out.add((s, i, ty)); s, ty = None, None
            if t != "O": s, ty = i, t[2:]                  # an I- with no matching B- starts a span
    return out

A BiLSTM tagger

The tagger is the bidirectional LSTM of Chapter 9 with a linear layer on every position instead of one at the end: each token’s output combines everything to its left and everything to its right, and a softmax over the tag set picks its label. Two input details matter for NER. Word embeddings from GloVe are lower-cased, which throws away the strongest single clue to a name, so a small embedding of each word’s shape (all capitals, capitalised, lower case, other) is concatenated to it. And the loss is ordinary cross-entropy per token, ignoring padding.

h = torch.cat([s.e(words), s.shape(shapes)], -1)               # 100-d GloVe + 8-d word shape
o, _ = s.lstm(s.dp(h))                                      # bidirectional, 2 × 200
logits = s.out(s.dp(o))                                     # (batch, length, n_tags): one prediction per token
loss = F.cross_entropy(logits[mask], tags[mask])

The BiLSTM tagger reached 84.07 span F1 while getting 97.1% of tokens right: the 3% of wrong tokens are concentrated in entities, and each wrong entity token breaks a whole span. BIOES, often reported to help by a few tenths of a point, scored 83.47 here, with three times the seed spread (±0.92 against ±0.18); with a softmax tagger, more tags mean more chances to output an inconsistent sequence, and the benefit of explicit ends appears mainly when a CRF enforces them. The model also produced 168 · 136 · 134 orphaned I- tags in the three runs, I-X with no B-X or I-X before it, such as “Nadim/O Ladki/I-ORG” for the person Nadim Ladki. A pretrained BERT is 7 points ahead, which Chapter 18 measures directly.

Common tagging errors

Of the BIO tagger’s span errors on the test set (one seed), 406 had the right boundaries but the wrong type, 361 overlapped a gold entity with the wrong boundaries, 229 were entities missed entirely and 141 were entities invented where there were none. Wrong type and wrong boundary together are two thirds of the errors. Wrong type is usually ORG against LOC or PER, where the word could be either (“Japan” the country or the national team); wrong boundary is usually a long name cut short or a title swallowed into it. The first needs more context than a sentence gives; the second is where consistent tag sequences, Chapter 11’s subject, help most.

Use case: pulling drug names and doses out of clinical notes

A hospital wants every medication, dose and frequency extracted from free-text discharge notes. The team defines three entity types, writes annotation guidelines, has two nurses tag 1,500 notes in BIO (measuring their agreement first, as in P7), and trains a BiLSTM tagger. It scores well on drug names but poorly on doses, and the error analysis explains why: most mistakes are boundaries (“500 mg twice daily” tagged as “500 mg”), because the guidelines were ambiguous about whether frequency belongs to the dose. Fixing the guidelines and re-annotating 200 notes gains more than any model change, and the team later moves to a CRF layer (Chapter 11) and then BERT (Chapter 18), each measured against this baseline.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Reporting token accuracy for NER: predicting O everywhere scores about 83% on CoNLL-2003 (P7).
  • Leaving invalid sequences in: about 140 orphaned I- tags per run here, each a probable boundary error.
  • Lower-casing words without adding a case feature: capitalisation is the strongest single clue to a name.
  • Switching to BIOES and expecting a gain: with a softmax tagger it scored 83.47 against 84.07, with three times the seed spread.
  • Changing the tag scheme without decoding back to spans: BIOES must be converted to the same spans before scoring.
  • Ignoring unknown words: a tagger’s accuracy on words it has never seen is the number that predicts how it will do on new text.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How do you turn named-entity recognition into a classification problem?

How a strong answer sounds
Label every token: B-X starts an entity of type X, I-X continues it, O is outside (BIO). Now each token is a multi-class classification, so any tagger, from a CRF to a BiLSTM to BERT, can predict one tag per token, and a simple decoder turns runs of B/I tags back into spans for scoring. The trick is the B tag, which separates adjacent entities of the same type. A BiLSTM tagger trained on CoNLL-2003 this way reached 84.07 span F1. Score spans, not tokens: the same model had 97% token accuracy.

Deeper: Your NER model’s span F1 is much lower than its token accuracy. Where would you look?

How a strong answer sounds
Token accuracy is dominated by the O tag, so it stays high even when entities are badly broken; one wrong token inside a three-word name loses the whole span, and a boundary error costs both a false positive and a false negative. I would break span errors into wrong type, wrong boundary, missed and spurious: on CoNLL-2003 with a BiLSTM, wrong type and wrong boundary were two thirds. Then count invalid tag sequences, I- tags with no preceding B- (about 140 per run here), since each is usually a boundary error, and look at per-type F1. Fixes follow from that: a CRF or constrained decoding for invalid sequences, more context or a pretrained encoder for type confusions, and annotation guidelines for boundary disagreements.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Sequence labelling predicts one tag per token; entities are encoded with BIO or BIOES and decoded back to spans.
  • Measured POS: BiLSTM 93.92% vs most-frequent-tag 88.41%; on unknown words 78.43% vs 46.07%.
  • Measured NER: 84.07 span F1 at 97.1% token accuracy; BIOES did not help a softmax tagger (83.47).
  • A softmax tagger produced about 140 invalid I- tags per run: independent decisions make inconsistent sequences.
  • Score entities as exact spans and break errors down by type: wrong type, wrong boundary, spurious, missed.

Check your understanding

In BIO tagging, how is “New York City” as a location tagged?

B begins the entity, I continues it. In BIOES it would be B-LOC I-LOC E-LOC.

Which BIO sequence is invalid?

I-PER must follow B-PER or I-PER. A softmax tagger can still output it; the BiLSTM tagger here produced 168 · 136 · 134 of them in three runs.

Why add a word-shape feature to a tagger that uses lower-cased GloVe embeddings?

“Apple” and “apple”, “US” and “us” look identical after lower-casing.

What does BIOES add over BIO?

Measured with a softmax BiLSTM, BIOES scored 83.47 against BIO’s 84.07: the extra tags did not help without constraints.

A POS tagger is 95% accurate overall. Why report its accuracy on unknown words separately?
