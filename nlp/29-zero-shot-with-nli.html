Zero-Shot Classification with NLI

A model trained to decide whether one sentence entails another can classify text into labels it has never seen. Turn each label into a hypothesis (“This text is about sports.”), ask the model how strongly the text entails each one, and pick the strongest. No labelled examples, any label set, changeable at run time. The catch is that the answer depends on how you word the labels.

This chapter measures two NLI models on three tasks with several wordings, and a small LLM on the same tasks. For DeBERTa-v3 on AG News, wording alone moved accuracy from 62.5% to 81.4%: the short label “Sci/Tech” was almost never chosen (4.5% of predictions for a quarter of the data), while “science and technology” fixed it. On SST-2 every wording landed between 87.27% and 93.35% with no training at all.

Natural-language inference

NLI gives a model a premise and a hypothesis and asks: does the premise entail the hypothesis, contradict it, or neither (neutral)? MNLI (Williams et al., 2018) has 393,000 such pairs across ten genres. A cross-encoder trained on it learns something general about meaning: whether one statement follows from another.

Yin, Hay and Roth (2019) turned that into zero-shot classification. For a text and labels {World, Sports, Business, Sci/Tech}, build four hypotheses from a template, “This text is about {label}.”, score each pair, and pick the label with the highest entailment-minus-contradiction score. This is what Hugging Face’s zero-shot-classification pipeline does.

Skew and calibration

Zero-shot classifiers are often skewed towards particular labels. With short labels and the default template, DeBERTa assigned 50.2% of AG News articles to Business and only 4.5% to Sci/Tech, although each is a quarter of the test set: “This example is Sci/Tech.” is simply an odd hypothesis to entail. Prior correction, subtracting each label’s average score over the test texts (no labels needed), is the standard fix. Here it was unreliable: across the wordings it changed accuracy by between -4.86 and +3.66 points, helping most for DeBERTa’s good/bad sentiment labels (+3.2) and hurting most on tweet emotion. Better label wording fixed the AG News skew far more than correction did.

The “probabilities” are poorly calibrated. Softmaxing the entailment scores gave an expected calibration error of 22.99% for DeBERTa on AG News with short labels and 5.0–8.79% on SST-2: a reported 90% is not 90%. A threshold on these scores (“only tag if above 0.8”) needs to be chosen on labelled data, not read off the number.

NLI classifiers against a small LLM

DeBERTa-v3-base, at 184M parameters, processed about 1,454 SST-2 texts a second against BART-large’s 708, and matched or beat it on AG News and tweet emotion; BART was better on SST-2 (93.35 against 88.76). Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, scoring the first token of each label after a prompt that names them, was not better than either NLI model at its best wording on any task (70.9, 88.3, 67.56) and ran at about 226 texts a second on SST-2: one pass per text rather than one per label, but on a model eight times the size of DeBERTa. It showed the same skew (on tweet emotion it called 55.7% of tweets angry). Note the throughput trade-off of NLI: its cost scales with the number of labels, so with four labels DeBERTa managed about 390 texts a second on AG News against 1,454 with two on SST-2.

Use case: tagging news for a new section

An editor wants articles tagged with a new set of themes (“cost of living”, “climate”, “AI policy”) by Monday, before anyone has labelled anything. A zero-shot NLI classifier does it with the themes written as hypotheses. The engineer labels 100 articles to measure it, tries three wordings per theme, keeps the best, and applies prior correction after noticing that half of all articles were going to “cost of living”. Tags are shown with a threshold rather than forced onto every article, and the corrections editors make are saved as training data for a fine-tuned model a month later.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Using bare category codes as labels: “Sci/Tech” was chosen for 4.5% of AG News articles; “science and technology” lifted accuracy by up to 18.9 points.
  • Keeping the library’s default template unexamined: “This example is {}.” was the worst template in 11 of 14 combinations here.
  • Thresholding zero-shot probabilities as if calibrated: calibration error reached 22.99% here.
  • Tuning label wording on the test set: the best of nine wordings is optimistically biased; choose on a separate sample.
  • Forgetting the cost of many labels: 50 labels means 50 forward passes per text.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How can an NLI model classify text into labels it was never trained on?

How a strong answer sounds
An NLI model is trained to decide whether a premise entails a hypothesis. To classify a text, turn each candidate label into a hypothesis with a template, “This text is about sports.”, pair it with the text, and score how strongly the text entails it; the label with the highest entailment (minus contradiction) wins. Nothing was trained on the labels, so any label set works and can be changed at run time. The weak point is that the classifier is defined by the wording: on AG News, DeBERTa-v3 scored 62.5% with “Sci/Tech” and the default template and 81.4% with descriptive labels.

Deeper: A stakeholder wants a 30-label classifier tomorrow, with no data. What do you build, and what do you warn them about?

How a strong answer sounds
For a first version tomorrow, an NLI zero-shot classifier or a small instruction-tuned LLM, with three warnings. First, cost: NLI needs one forward pass per label, so 30 labels is 30 passes per text; DeBERTa here went from about 1,454 texts a second with 2 labels to 390 with 4. Second, wording changes the answer by up to 18.9 points, and some labels are systematically under-predicted, so the labels need writing as descriptions and testing. Third, the confidences are not calibrated (ECE up to 22.99%). So I would label 200 examples straight away to choose wording and thresholds and to measure per-label recall, show predictions as suggestions with an “other” option, log corrections as training data, and plan to replace it with a fine-tuned encoder once a few hundred labels per class exist.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Measured: label wording moved DeBERTa’s AG News accuracy from 62.5% to 81.4%; descriptive labels beat short codes.
  • Measured: zero-shot scores were skewed (Sci/Tech 4.5% of predictions for 25% of articles) and badly calibrated (ECE up to 22.99%); prior correction was hit and miss.
  • Measured: a 184M-parameter NLI model matched or beat Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct on all three tasks (81.4 vs 70.9 on AG News).
  • NLI turns any label set into hypotheses; no training examples needed.
  • Measure on a small labelled sample before trusting it, and fine-tune once labels exist.
