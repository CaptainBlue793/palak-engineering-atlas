Word Embeddings: word2vec from Scratch

A bag of words treats excellent and superb as unrelated columns, so a model must learn about each from its own examples. Word embeddings give every word a dense vector of a few hundred numbers, learned so that words used in similar contexts get similar vectors. That single idea, trained on raw text with no labels, is the ancestor of every representation in modern NLP.

This chapter writes word2vec’s skip-gram with negative sampling in about thirty lines of PyTorch and trains it on 98 million words of Wikipedia in 36 seconds on one GPU. Analogy accuracy rose from 0% to 37.6% over that pass and to 50.1% after three. The vectors learned that Paris is to France as Tokyo is to Japan, and also that “bad” is to “worst” as “best” is to “sexiest”.

The distributional hypothesis

“You shall know a word by the company it keeps” (J. R. Firth, 1957). If excellent and superb appear next to the same words (a film, performance, really), they probably mean similar things. Word2vec (Mikolov et al., 2013) turns this into a prediction task that can be trained on any text: from a word, predict the words around it. A vector that is good at predicting a word’s neighbours ends up encoding what kind of word it is, because similar words have to make similar predictions.

Skip-gram and CBOW

Slide a window over the text. In skip-gram, each word in the window’s centre is used to predict each word around it, up to a distance of k (here a random 1–5 per centre, so near words count more). In CBOW (continuous bag of words), the average of the surrounding words’ vectors predicts the centre word. Each word has two vectors: an input vector, used when it is the centre, and an output vector, used when it is the target. The input vectors are the embeddings people keep.

The obvious way to predict a word is a softmax over the whole vocabulary, 100,000 scores per training pair, which is far too slow. Negative sampling replaces it with a cheap binary task: for the true (centre, context) pair, push their dot product up; for k random “noise” words, push it down.

loss = −log σ(ucontext · vcentre) − Σi=1..k log σ(−unoise i · vcentre)

Noise words are drawn in proportion to frequency raised to the power 0.75, which samples rare words more often than their frequency would. Subsampling randomly drops very frequent words before windowing: a word with frequency f is kept with probability about √(t/f) (here t = 10−4), so the is dropped most of the time and rare words never are. It speeds training and, as measured below, did more than that in this implementation.

Win  = nn.Embedding(V, d, sparse=True); Wout = nn.Embedding(V, d, sparse=True)   # input and output vectors
c  = torch.randint(window, L - window, (bs,))                  # 8,192 random centre positions
o  = random_offsets_within(1..5)                                   # one context per centre, near words likelier
ng = torch.multinomial(freq ** 0.75, bs * k, replacement=True).view(bs, k)  # k noise words each
h  = Win(S[c])                                                  # (bs, d) centre vectors
pos = (h * Wout(S[c + o])).sum(-1); neg = torch.bmm(Wout(ng), h[:, :, None]).squeeze(-1)
loss = -(F.logsigmoid(pos).sum() + F.logsigmoid(-neg).sum())   # summed, like word2vec’s per-pair updates
opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()                          # sparse SGD, learning rate 0.025 decaying to 0

Training on a corpus

The corpus is WikiText-103, about 100 million words of English Wikipedia, lower-cased, keeping the 100,000 most frequent words (98.0 million tokens). Subsampling left 53.5 million tokens, and one pass of skip-gram with up to 5 words either side and 5 negatives made 267 million training pairs. On an RTX 5070 Ti, in batches of 8,192 pairs, that took 35.7 seconds, about 7.5 million pairs a second. Quality was measured during training on two standard tests: the Google analogy set (12,234 questions such as athens : greece :: oslo : ?, restricted to the 30,000 most frequent words, counted right only if the top answer is exact) and WordSim-353 (Spearman correlation between cosine similarity and human similarity ratings for 353 word pairs).

For comparison, gensim’s optimised C implementation, with the same settings and one pass on 16 CPU threads, scored 39.5% on analogies and 0.636 on WordSim in 27.9 seconds: the from-scratch version is in the same place, as it should be. The loss, meanwhile, barely moved after the first tenth of training (4.16 → 2.48 → 2.25), a reminder that the training loss of an embedding model says little about how good its vectors are.

Data and passes mattered most: a tenth of the corpus gave a fifth of the analogy score, and a hundredth gave nothing, while three passes over the full corpus beat every other change. CBOW beat skip-gram by 8.5 points here, against the common advice that skip-gram is better; that advice comes from smaller corpora and rare words, where skip-gram’s many updates per word help, and with 100 million words CBOW’s averaged context was the better signal. More negatives helped, one negative hurt badly, and dimensions beyond 100 bought almost nothing after one pass, because 300-dimensional vectors need more training to use their extra capacity.

Turning subsampling off did not just slow training; it broke it. In this batched implementation the loss is summed over 8,192 pairs, and without subsampling the commonest words (the, the comma) appear hundreds of times in every batch, so their rows receive hundreds of gradient steps at once. Values passed 900 million before overflowing, and 91,209 of the 100,000 vectors ended as NaN, even when trained on only the first 20 million tokens. Dividing the learning rate by 64 stopped the blow-up but gave 2.0% analogy accuracy, because rare words then received steps far too small to learn anything. The original word2vec updates one pair at a time and meets this less severely. Subsampling is what reconciles the two: frequent words appear less often per batch, so one learning rate suits both common and rare words.

Nearest neighbours and analogies

A word’s nearest neighbours by cosine show what the vectors captured. france → spain, italy, portugal, netherlands, belgium: countries, ordered roughly by how often they appear in similar contexts. quickly → soon, rapidly, slowly, swiftly: note slowly, an antonym. Antonyms share contexts almost perfectly (“moved quickly”, “moved slowly”), so distributional vectors put them close together, which is a real limitation for tasks like sentiment. bank mixes its senses: banks, banking, UBS, Bank Markazi, and jodrell, from the Jodrell Bank observatory. One word, one vector, every sense averaged in. And the corpus shows through: python → meow, medievil, html, emulator, from Wikipedia’s articles on video games and software, not snakes; apple → macintosh, ipod, iphone.

Analogies are solved with vector arithmetic: the answer to a : b :: c : ? is the word nearest to b − a + c. man : king :: woman : ? gave queen, and france : paris :: japan : ? gave tokyo. walk : walked :: swim : ? gave swam, an irregular past tense learned from context alone. The failures are as informative. good : best :: bad : ? gave sexiest and oscars, because in Wikipedia “best” mostly appears in award names. london : england :: berlin : ? put czechoslovakia and italy ahead of germany, which was third. And man : doctor :: woman : ? gave first names, amy and sarah. Of the corpus’s 7,194 uses of “doctor”, 19% are in “Doctor Who” and 42% are “the doctor”, often the series’ main character, so the vector partly encodes a television character; Amy and Sarah are names of his companions. Chapter 7 measures what such vectors absorb about gender and occupations directly.

Use case: product embeddings from shopping sessions

An online grocer treats each customer session as a “sentence” and each product ID as a “word”, and trains the same skip-gram code on 40 million sessions. Products bought in the same sessions get similar vectors: oat milk sits near granola and almond butter, charcoal near burger buns. The vectors power “you may also like” and fill gaps for new products by averaging the vectors of products bought with them in their first week. The lessons from this chapter carry over directly: the commonest items (bananas, milk) must be subsampled or they dominate every neighbour list, results improve most with more sessions and passes, and neighbours are checked by hand before launch, because “bought together” is not the same as “similar”: charcoal is not a substitute for buns.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Too little text: 1 million tokens gave 0.1% analogy accuracy and 10 million gave 7.8%; embeddings need tens of millions of words.
  • Turning off subsampling in a batched implementation: 91% of vectors became NaN; a smaller learning rate avoided that but gave 2% analogy accuracy.
  • Judging embeddings by the training loss: it flattened after a tenth of the pass while analogy accuracy kept rising.
  • Expecting antonyms to be far apart: quickly’s neighbours include slowly.
  • Forgetting polysemy: one vector for bank mixes money, rivers and a radio telescope.
  • Trusting famous analogies as proof: “queen” worked; “bad : worst” came out as “sexiest”.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How does word2vec learn what words mean without any labels?

How a strong answer sounds
It turns raw text into a prediction task: from each word, predict the words around it. Every word gets a vector, and training adjusts the vectors so that a word’s vector scores high against its real neighbours and low against random words. Words that appear in similar contexts are forced to make similar predictions, so they end up with similar vectors; that is the distributional hypothesis. Trained on 98 million words of Wikipedia, a from-scratch version learned that France’s neighbours are Spain and Italy and that man is to king as woman is to queen, in 36 seconds on a GPU. The same mechanism also puts antonyms like quickly and slowly close together, because they share contexts.

Deeper: Why use negative sampling instead of a full softmax, and why raise word frequencies to the power 0.75?

How a strong answer sounds
A softmax needs a score for every vocabulary word for every training pair, 100,000 dot products, and its gradient touches every output vector. Negative sampling replaces that with a binary classification: the true context against k sampled noise words, so each update touches only k + 2 rows; that is what let 267 million pairs train in 36 seconds. The noise distribution matters: sampling by raw frequency would make almost every negative “the” or “of”, which teaches little, while uniform sampling would waste updates on words that rarely occur. The 0.75 power flattens the distribution between the two and worked best empirically. The number of negatives matters too: with one, analogy accuracy fell from 37.6% to 26.6%; with fifteen it rose to 40.5%.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Word2vec learns a vector per word by predicting nearby words; similar contexts give similar vectors.
  • Negative sampling makes each update touch only k + 2 rows: 267 million pairs trained in 36 seconds on one GPU.
  • Measured: corpus size and passes mattered most (1M tokens: 0.1%, 98M: 37.6%, three passes: 50.1%); CBOW beat skip-gram here.
  • Subsampling frequent words is essential in a batched implementation: without it training diverged.
  • Neighbours and analogies show both meaning and its limits: antonyms close together, senses mixed, the corpus’s own biases.
