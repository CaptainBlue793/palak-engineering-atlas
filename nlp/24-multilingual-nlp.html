Multilingual NLP & Cross-Lingual Transfer

Most labelled data is English; most of the world’s speakers are not. A multilingual encoder, one transformer pre-trained on text in about 100 languages with a shared vocabulary, can be fine-tuned on English labels and then used on Swahili or Thai with no Swahili or Thai labels at all. How well that works depends on the language, and the tokeniser charges some languages more than others for the same sentence.

This chapter trains XLM-R and mBERT on English inference data and tests them in 15 languages. Trained on English only, XLM-R scored 80.12% on English XNLI and 69.94% on average across the other 14 languages, from 74.59% in French down to 62.97% in Swahili and 62.93% in Urdu. The tokenisation tax was small for XLM-R (at most ×1.2) and large for GPT-2’s English tokeniser, which needed ×7.17 as many tokens for Thai.

One model, many languages

mBERT (Devlin et al., 2019) is BERT pre-trained with masked language modelling on Wikipedia in 104 languages, with one 119k-token WordPiece vocabulary. XLM-R (Conneau et al., 2020) is a RoBERTa trained on 2.5 TB of filtered CommonCrawl in 100 languages with a 250k-token SentencePiece vocabulary. Neither sees a single translated sentence pair. Transfer still happens because languages share subwords (numbers, names, borrowed words), and because a model forced to predict masked words in many languages with one set of weights finds it cheaper to reuse representations across them.

XNLI (Conneau et al., 2018) is the standard test: 5,010 natural-language-inference pairs (premise, hypothesis; entailment, neutral or contradiction) professionally translated into 14 languages. The recipe here: fine-tune on 100,000 English MNLI pairs for one epoch, then evaluate on the XNLI test set in all 15 languages without further training.

The tokenisation tax

A tokeniser learnt mostly from English splits other languages into more pieces. More tokens per sentence mean more compute, an earlier hit on the 512-token limit, a higher bill from any API priced per token, and subwords that carry less meaning each. XNLI’s premises are translations of the same sentences, so the token count per language, divided by English’s, measures the tax directly.

Measured on the first 1,000 XNLI premises in each language, XLM-R’s 250,000-piece SentencePiece vocabulary kept every language within ×0.82–×1.2 of English. mBERT’s 119,000 pieces, shared between 104 languages and built from Wikipedia sizes, charged more: ×1.63 for Greek, ×1.5 for Hindi and ×2.22 for Thai. An English-only tokeniser is far worse. GPT-2’s byte-level BPE can spell anything, so nothing is unknown, but it needed ×4.6 as many tokens for Russian, ×6.11 for Hindi and ×7.17 for Thai. English BERT’s WordPiece looked cheap on Thai (×0.23) for the worst reason: 78.3% of its Thai tokens were [UNK], and 63.0% of its Chinese ones. A sentence that is mostly [UNK] is short and meaningless. The lab turns the ratios into document lengths against a 512-token limit.

Both models transferred: with no labelled data in any of the 14 languages, XLM-R averaged 69.94% and mBERT 64.34%, against 33.3% for chance. Both lost ground relative to English (XLM-R 10.2 points, mBERT 12.7), and the losses follow resources and similarity to English. European languages lost least (XLM-R: French 74.59, Spanish 75.03, German 72.65); Swahili (62.97) and Urdu (62.93), with the least pre-training text, lost most. mBERT collapsed further on languages its vocabulary served badly: Thai 53.67 against XLM-R’s 68.04, Swahili 51.3 against 62.97. These are one seed, one epoch on 100,000 MNLI pairs; English accuracy (80.12) is below the roughly 85 that a full MNLI run reaches, and the published XLM-R base XNLI average is about 76, so read the numbers as the shape of transfer rather than the best attainable.

Translate-test

The alternative is to translate the input into English and use the English model. It needs a translation model per language and doubles the latency, but it uses the classifier on the language it was trained on.

Translating the German test set into English with a Marian model and classifying it with the same English-trained XLM-R scored 75.19%, 2.5 points above zero-shot (72.65%). For Hindi it scored 61.18%, 4.9 points below zero-shot (66.07%). Translate-test is only as good as the translation model: German–English is one of the best-resourced pairs, Hindi–English much less so, and the Hindi translations lose details the classifier needs (the lab shows examples beside the original English premises XNLI was translated from). Translating each language’s 10,020 sentences took about 29 s on the GPU, a cost paid on every input in production. A Swahili comparison was planned, but Helsinki-NLP publishes no Swahili–English Marian model.

Use case: support-ticket routing in a new market

A company routes English support tickets with a fine-tuned encoder and launches in Kenya, where many tickets are in Swahili. Swapping in XLM-R trained on the same English tickets routes Swahili tickets on day one, at an accuracy well below English, as this chapter’s XNLI numbers predict. The team compares translate-test on a few hundred hand-labelled Swahili tickets, picks whichever wins on those, and spends the next month’s labelling budget on Swahili examples rather than more English ones. They also budget for the token tax: Swahili tickets cost more tokens and hit the length limit sooner.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Using an English tokeniser on other scripts: English BERT turned 78.3% of Thai tokens into [UNK]; GPT-2 needed ×7.17 as many tokens.
  • Assuming translate-test always beats zero-shot: it gained 2.5 points for German and lost 4.9 for Hindi.
  • Assuming “supports 100 languages” means “works equally in 100 languages”: pre-training data per language varies by orders of magnitude.
  • Using an English tokeniser on other languages: it fragments them into characters and bytes, or into [UNK].
  • Testing only on translated benchmarks: XNLI is translated from English; real text in a language has its own topics, names and code-switching.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How can a model trained only on English labels classify Hindi text?

How a strong answer sounds
A multilingual encoder such as XLM-R is pre-trained with masked language modelling on text in about 100 languages with one shared vocabulary and one set of weights. Nothing tells it that “dog” and “Hund” mean the same thing, but shared subwords, names and numbers, and the pressure to reuse representations across languages, give it a partly language-neutral space. Fine-tune it on English labels and the classifier, sitting on that space, works on Hindi inputs too. It works unevenly: trained on English inference data only, XLM-R scored 80.12% in English, 66.07% in Hindi and 62.97% in Swahili. The less pre-training text a language had, and the less it shares with English, the bigger the drop.

Deeper: You must launch a classifier in three new languages with no labelled data. What do you do?

How a strong answer sounds
Start with a multilingual encoder fine-tuned on whatever English labels exist, and measure it on a few hundred labelled examples per new language, because transfer varies a lot by language (here from 74.59% in French to 62.93% in Urdu on XNLI). In parallel, try translate-test with the best available MT model for each language and compare on the same examples: it helped for German (+2.5) and hurt for Hindi (−4.9). Check the tokenisation tax for each language’s script, since it affects truncation and cost. Then spend the labelling budget on the target languages, starting with the weakest: a few hundred in-language examples usually beat any transfer trick, and translate-train, machine-translating the English training set, is a cheap extra. Monitor per language in production, because native text has its own names, topics and code-switching that translated benchmarks lack.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Measured: trained on English only, XLM-R averaged 69.94% on 14 other XNLI languages (80.12% English), mBERT 64.34%.
  • Measured: tokens per sentence relative to English stayed within ×1.2 for XLM-R but reached ×7.17 for GPT-2’s English tokeniser on Thai.
  • Measured: translate-test beat zero-shot for German (75.19 vs 72.65) and lost for Hindi (61.18 vs 66.07).
  • Multilingual encoders transfer English labels to other languages with no target labels, unevenly.
  • A few hundred target-language labels usually beat any amount of clever transfer.
