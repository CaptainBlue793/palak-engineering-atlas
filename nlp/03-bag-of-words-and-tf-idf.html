Bag of Words & TF-IDF

The oldest useful idea in NLP is to forget word order entirely: describe a document by which words it contains and how often. It sounds too crude to work, and it is still the baseline every text model is measured against, because it is fast, transparent, needs no GPU and is often within a few points of models a thousand times larger.

This chapter builds the representation step by step and measures every choice on two different tasks, sentiment on 25,000 IMDB reviews and topic on 120,000 AG News headlines. Sublinear TF-IDF beat raw counts by 2.7 points on sentiment. Bigrams added 2.2 more, and trigrams added nothing. Removing stop words cost 1.5 points on sentiment and 0.1 on topic. A vocabulary pruned to a third of its size, or no vocabulary at all with hashing, lost less than half a point.

Bag of words

Fix a vocabulary, one entry per distinct word seen in the training data. A document becomes a vector with one position per vocabulary word, holding how many times that word occurs. Order is gone: “not good, bad” and “bad, not good” are the same bag. Most entries are zero, so the vectors are stored sparsely (P5).

from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import CountVectorizer
cv = CountVectorizer(token_pattern=r"(?u)\b\w+\b")      # keep one-letter words such as "a" and "i"
X = cv.fit_transform(train_texts)                        # sparse (n_docs, n_words)
cv.transform(["A gem of a film. Not the best of the year, but the acting is excellent."])
# the: 3, a: 2, of: 2, and one each for gem, film, not, best, year, but, acting, is, excellent

Three ways to fill the vector: binary (1 if the word occurs), counts, and TF-IDF, which reweights the counts by how informative each word is.

TF-IDF weighting

Words that appear in every document, like the, say nothing about which document this is. Inverse document frequency downweights them: idf(w) = ln((1 + N) / (1 + df(w))) + 1 in scikit-learn’s version, where N is the number of documents and df(w) the number containing w. The weight of a word in a document is tf × idf, where tf is its count (or 1 + ln(count), “sublinear”, so the tenth occurrence adds less than the first), and the vector is then scaled to length 1 so long and short documents are comparable.

TF-IDF beat counts on both tasks, by 2.3 points on IMDB and 2.3 on AG News, and sublinear tf added a little more, 0.5 on the long IMDB reviews, where a word can repeat many times, and almost nothing on short headlines. On IMDB, binary presence was even slightly better than raw counts: whether “brilliant” appears matters more than how often. Raw counts were also the slowest to fit (5.2 seconds against 0.2 for sublinear TF-IDF on IMDB), because unnormalised features of very different sizes make the optimiser’s job harder. Sublinear TF-IDF, L2-normalised, is the right default.

N-gram features

An n-gram is a run of n consecutive tokens. Adding bigrams to the bag brings back a little order: “not good” becomes a feature of its own, and so does “new york”. The cost is size: there are far more distinct bigrams than words, and trigrams more again.

Bigrams were worth 2.2 points on sentiment and 1.0 on topic, for ten times the features. Trigrams added nothing on either (+0.02 and −0.08) and doubled the feature count again. Sentiment gains more because its key signals are short phrases (“not good”, “waste of”, “must see”), while topics are mostly carried by single content words. Unigrams plus bigrams is the standard choice for exactly this reason.

The same stop list had opposite value on the two tasks. On sentiment it cost 1.5 points, and keeping the ten negation words won back only a quarter of that (P3 found the same with a different pipeline); the list also deletes but, very, too and off, and with them bigrams such as “not good” and “too long”. On topic classification it cost 0.1 points, within the noise, while cutting the non-zero entries per headline from 63 to 38, a 40% saving in memory and time. Stop-word removal is a task decision: harmless for topic, harmful for sentiment.

Vocabulary pruning and the hashing trick

A vocabulary can be cut three ways: drop rare n-grams (min_df), drop near-universal ones (max_df), or keep only the most frequent (max_features). The hashing trick skips the vocabulary altogether: each n-gram is hashed straight to one of 2b columns, so nothing has to be stored or fitted and new words need no update, at the price of collisions (two n-grams sharing a column) and features you cannot map back to words.

from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import HashingVectorizer
hv = HashingVectorizer(ngram_range=(1, 2), n_features=2**18, alternate_sign=True)   # no fit, no vocabulary
X = hv.transform(texts)                                 # works on a stream; add TfidfTransformer for IDF

Measured with unigrams and bigrams: keeping only n-grams that occur in at least five documents (min_df=5) cut IMDB’s feature count from 433,170 to 157,751, a third, with no loss at all (90.98% against 90.97%); AG News behaved the same way. Keeping every n-gram (min_df=1), 1.46 million features, was slightly worse on both, because n-grams seen once are mostly noise the model can overfit. max_features=100,000 lost 0.1–0.4 points; 20,000 lost 0.8–1.4; 1,000 lost 5–7. Dropping words in more than half the documents (max_df=0.5) changed nothing, because IDF had already made them nearly weightless.

The hashing trick with 218 = 262,144 columns came within 0.3 points of the full vocabulary on IMDB and 0.4 on AG News, with nothing to fit or store; 220 columns was no better, while 214 lost 3.8–4.4 points to collisions and 210 lost 13–18. The hashed matrices here are larger in memory than the pruned ones (about 100 MB against 85) because hashing keeps every n-gram, including those seen once; combine hashing with a frequency filter if memory matters.

Use case: routing a million support tickets a day

A telecom routes tickets to 40 teams. A TF-IDF logistic regression on unigrams and bigrams is trained nightly on the last 90 days and serves predictions in under a millisecond each on CPU. Two problems appear at scale. The fitted vocabulary grows to 9 million n-grams and the model file to several gigabytes, so they cap it with min_df and max_features after measuring that accuracy barely changes. Then new product names in tickets are invisible until the nightly refit; switching to a hashing vectoriser with 220 columns lets an online model learn them as they appear, at a small, measured cost from collisions. A fine-tuned encoder is tested too; it gains two points on rare teams and is used only for tickets the linear model is unsure about.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Fitting the vectoriser on all the data: IDF and vocabulary then see the test set; fit on training data only.
  • Removing stop words for sentiment: −1.5 points on IMDB; for topic (AG News) it cost 0.1 and halved memory.
  • Adding trigrams by default: double the features for +0.02 and −0.08 points.
  • Keeping every n-gram: min_df 1 gave 1.46 million features and slightly lower accuracy than min_df 5 with 158,000.
  • Using scikit-learn’s default token pattern without knowing it drops one-character tokens such as “a”, “i” and digits (P6).
  • Expecting bag-of-words to understand order or negation: “not good” is handled only as far as a bigram can.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is TF-IDF, and why does it help?

How a strong answer sounds
TF-IDF weights each word in a document by how often it appears there (term frequency) times how rare it is across the collection (inverse document frequency, roughly log of documents over documents containing it). Words in every document, like “the”, get the lowest weight; rare, specific words get high weight. Then each document vector is scaled to length 1 so long and short documents are comparable. It helps because the most frequent words carry the least information about which document, or class, this is: on IMDB, TF-IDF beat raw counts by 2.3 points, and using 1 + log of the count instead of the raw count added another 0.5, since the tenth mention of a word says little more than the first.

Deeper: Your bag-of-n-grams model has a 5-million-feature vocabulary that is too big to ship. What are your options?

How a strong answer sounds
First check how much the long tail is worth: on IMDB, keeping only n-grams in at least five documents cut the vocabulary to a third with no accuracy loss, and n-grams seen once actually hurt slightly. Then cap the vocabulary with max_features and measure the curve: 100,000 features cost only 0.1–0.4 points in my measurements. If the vocabulary itself is the problem, for example in a streaming system where new words keep arriving, use the hashing trick: 2^18 columns came within half a point of the full vocabulary with nothing to store. Feature selection by chi-squared or mutual information is another option. In every case, keep the fitted vectoriser and model together and version them.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • A bag of words is a sparse vector of word counts; order is lost except through n-grams.
  • IDF downweights words that appear everywhere; sublinear tf and L2 normalisation make documents of different lengths comparable.
  • Sublinear TF-IDF was the best weighting on both tasks: +2.7 points over raw counts on IMDB.
  • Bigrams paid (+2.2 on sentiment, +1.0 on topic); trigrams did not; stop-word removal hurt sentiment (−1.5) and was neutral for topic.
  • min_df 5 cut the vocabulary to a third at no cost; hashing to 2^18 columns lost under half a point with no vocabulary at all.
