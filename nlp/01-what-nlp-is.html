What NLP Is

Natural language processing is getting computers to do useful things with human language: sort it, search it, pull facts out of it, translate it, answer questions about it and write it. Every one of those jobs is easy to state and hard to do, because language is ambiguous everywhere, and the meaning of a word depends on the words around it, the speaker and the situation.

This chapter maps the tasks by what they produce, shows where the ambiguity bites, and then solves one task five ways, from a rule anyone could write to a 14-billion-parameter language model. A 50-word list beat a coin toss by five points. A linear model with 74,000 weights reached 82.8%. A fine-tuned 67-million-parameter encoder reached 91.1%. A 14.8-billion-parameter LLM shown no examples reached 85.8%, mostly because it kept answering “mixed”.

Ambiguity and context

Language is ambiguous at every level, and people resolve it so effortlessly they rarely notice:

  • Words: bank (river or money), bass (fish or sound), Apple (fruit or company).
  • Structure: “I saw the man with the telescope”: who has the telescope? “The chicken is ready to eat”: who is eating?
  • Reference: “The trophy didn’t fit in the suitcase because it was too big.” Change big to small and it changes referent.
  • Meaning beyond the words: “Not bad” is praise; “Great, another Monday” is not; “Can you pass the salt?” is not a question about ability.

Each of these is a place where a method that looks only at individual words will fail, and the history of NLP is largely a history of methods that use more and more context to resolve them. The five methods below show that history in miniature.

Rules, statistics and neural models: one task, five ways

The task is binary sentiment. The main test is the 872 sentences of the SST-2 validation set, film-review sentences labelled positive or negative and not used for training by any method here. The second test is 500 full IMDB reviews, which are longer and written differently, to see what survives a change of domain. The five methods:

  1. Majority class: always predict the commoner training label. Every method must beat this.
  2. A hand-written lexicon: 25 positive and 25 negative words chosen by a person; count them; predict the side with more. The rule-based era.
  3. TF-IDF + logistic regression trained on SST-2’s 67,349 labelled phrases: the statistical era, and still the best cheap baseline (Chapters 3–4).
  4. DistilBERT fine-tuned on SST-2: a 67-million-parameter transformer encoder pretrained on books and Wikipedia, then trained on the same labelled phrases (Chapters 15–17).
  5. A zero-shot LLM: qwen3:14b, a 14.8-billion-parameter generative model, asked “Is the sentiment of this movie review positive or negative?” and shown no labelled examples at all.

The word list barely beat the majority class on SST-2 because 74.5% of the sentences contain none of its 50 words, so it fell back to guessing; on long IMDB reviews, which contain more of its words, it reached 68%. TF-IDF with logistic regression, trained in seconds, reached 82.8% and held almost exactly that on IMDB, a different kind of text. Fine-tuned DistilBERT was best on both, 91.1% and 89.2%: pretraining on a large corpus gives it context that word counts lack, such as negation, contrast and word order.

The LLM was the surprise, and the reason matters more than the score. Of its 124 mistakes on SST-2, 105 were not wrong answers at all: it replied “mixed” 96 times, “neutral” 6 times and with other words a few more. When it did answer positive or negative, it was right 97.5% of the time. Many of those sentences really are mixed: “pumpkin takes an admirable look at the hypocrisy of political correctness, but it does so with such an uneven tone…” is labelled negative. Rewording the prompt to name the two allowed answers and to pick the overall verdict when mixed raised accuracy to 89.3% on SST-2 and 85.5% on IMDB, and it still said “mixed” 66 times. Two lessons follow. An LLM does the task you describe, not the one you meant, so the specification is part of the model. And a few words of prompt moved its accuracy by 3.5 to 4.5 points, more than the gap between many published methods.

Where encoders and LLMs fit

The last two methods are the two families of modern NLP model. Encoders such as BERT read a whole text at once and turn it into vectors, one per token; a small layer on top turns those into a label, a tag or a span. They are small, fast and excellent at the first four rows of the task table, but they need labelled examples to fine-tune, and they do not write text. Generative LLMs such as GPT, Llama or Qwen predict the next token, so they can do any task you can phrase as text in and text out, often with no labelled data at all. The cost is size and speed. Measured on the same GPU, DistilBERT classified 9,215 SST-2 sentences a second and the 14.8-billion-parameter LLM 5.6, about 1,600 times slower; on IMDB’s long reviews, 1,064 against 1.1. The TF-IDF model, on a CPU, managed 61,563.

This course covers the whole span: counting methods and linear models (Chapters 3–5), word vectors and recurrent networks (6–14), transformer encoders and what is built on them (15–24), and the practice of evaluating, debugging and shipping all of them, including when to use an LLM instead (36–39). How LLMs themselves are built and trained is the subject of the LLMs course.

Baselines first

Before any model, build two baselines: the majority class, which tells you what “no skill” scores, and a TF-IDF logistic regression, which tells you what a few seconds of training buys. Both take minutes. Every later model has to justify its extra cost against them, and the margin is often smaller than expected: here the TF-IDF baseline trains in seconds and classifies tens of thousands of sentences per second. The baseline also catches problems early. A word list scored barely above the majority class on SST-2 because 74.5% of the sentences contain none of its 50 words, so it falls back to the majority label. A test like that takes a minute to run and saves a week of building on the wrong idea.

🧾
Use case: triaging app-store reviews

A product team wants every new app-store review sorted into bug report, feature request, praise or complaint, about 3,000 a day. The engineer starts with the output type (one label per text), builds the majority-class and TF-IDF baselines on 2,000 reviews labelled in an afternoon, and gets 81% accuracy against 38% for the majority class. A zero-shot LLM prompt, tried next, reaches 86% with no labelling at all but costs about a second per review and fails on reviews in Hindi and Portuguese; a fine-tuned multilingual encoder (Chapter 24) reaches 89% and runs on a CPU. They ship the encoder, keep the TF-IDF model as a fallback and a sanity check, and use the LLM to label the next batch of training data (Chapter 27).

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Starting with the biggest model: without a baseline you cannot tell whether it is any good, or worth its cost.
  • Testing only on data like the training data: every method’s score moved when the text changed from SST-2 sentences to IMDB reviews: DistilBERT 91.1% → 89.2%, the LLM 85.8% → 81.0%, the word list 56% → 68%.
  • Writing rules for open-ended language: 74.5% of the SST-2 sentences contained none of 50 hand-picked sentiment words.
  • Ignoring cost: the LLM was about 1,600 times slower per sentence than DistilBERT on the same GPU: for 3,000 reviews a day, nine minutes against a third of a second.
  • Treating every problem as classification: extracting an order number is a span task, and matching a question to an FAQ is a pair task; the output type decides the method.
  • Assuming the hard cases are rare: negation, sarcasm and mixed opinions defeated all four methods on 8 of 872 sentences.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What makes natural language hard for computers?

How a strong answer sounds
Ambiguity at every level, resolved by context that is often not in the words. A word can mean several things (bank), a sentence can have several structures (I saw the man with the telescope), a pronoun can point to several things, and meaning often goes beyond the literal words: “not bad” is praise, “great, another Monday” is sarcasm. Language is also open-ended, with new words, names and spellings arriving constantly, so no fixed list or rule set covers it. A 50-word sentiment lexicon I tested found none of its words in 74.5% of film-review sentences. Methods have improved by using more context: from single words, to word pairs, to whole sentences with transformers.

Deeper: A product manager asks you to build a sentiment classifier for support tickets. Where do you start?

How a strong answer sounds
Define the output and the metric first: which labels, and what a mistake costs, so whether precision or recall matters more. Then label a few hundred to a few thousand tickets, with two annotators on a sample to measure agreement. Build the majority-class and TF-IDF logistic-regression baselines, which take minutes and show what no skill and cheap skill score. Then try a zero-shot LLM prompt and a fine-tuned encoder, and compare accuracy, cost per ticket and latency, ideally also on tickets from a different month or product to check robustness. On film reviews, a fine-tuned DistilBERT reached 91% and ran 1,600 times faster than a zero-shot 14.8-billion-parameter LLM, which reached 86–89% depending on the prompt, mostly because it answered “mixed” to mixed sentences. The choice depends on volume, latency and how much labelled data you can get.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Organise NLP tasks by output: a label, a tag per token, a span, a pair score, a sequence or a structure.
  • Ambiguity at every level, resolved by context, is what makes language hard; methods have progressed by using more context.
  • On one sentiment task: a word list 56%, TF-IDF 82.8%, fine-tuned DistilBERT 91.1%, a zero-shot LLM 85.8–89.3% depending on the prompt, at 1/1,600 of DistilBERT’s speed.
  • Encoders are small, fast and need labels; generative LLMs need no labels but cost far more per example.
  • Build a majority-class and a TF-IDF baseline first; every later model must justify its cost against them.

Finding every person, place and organisation name in a news article is which kind of task?

Named-entity recognition tags each token, usually with B/I/O labels, and is scored on the resulting spans.

“The trophy didn’t fit in the suitcase because it was too small.” What makes this hard for a word-counting model?

Reference resolution depends on meaning and context that word counts cannot capture.

Why build a majority-class baseline?

On SST-2 it scored about 51%; a method that scores 56% has learned very little.

What is the main practical advantage of a fine-tuned encoder over a zero-shot generative LLM for a high-volume classification task?

Measured here: DistilBERT classified about 9,200 sentences a second on one GPU; the 14.8-billion-parameter LLM about 5.6.

A word-list sentiment rule scored 56% on short sentences but higher on long reviews. Why?

Coverage is the first problem of rules: most short sentences contained none of the 50 words.
