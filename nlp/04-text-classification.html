Text Classification with Linear Models

Assigning a label to a text is the most common NLP job in industry: spam or not, which team, which intent, how angry. Two linear models have done most of it for decades. Naive Bayes counts how often each word appears with each label; logistic regression learns a weight for each word directly. Both train in seconds, and knowing when each wins is still useful knowledge.

This chapter compares them, and two relatives, on three datasets with different shapes, then measures what happens with little data, with imbalanced classes and with probabilities you want to trust, and finally reads the mistakes of the best model. Logistic regression beat naive Bayes at every training size, including 50 examples, against the textbook expectation. Naive Bayes gave 92.7% of its predictions more than 99% confidence and was right on only 87.8% of those. And of the best model’s 15 most confident mistakes, at least 7 were reviews whose own text contradicts their label.

Naive Bayes

Bayes’ rule gives the probability of class c for document d as P(c | d) ∝ P(c) · P(d | c). Naive Bayes makes one bold assumption, that words are independent given the class, so P(d | c) is a product of per-word probabilities, and in log space (P4) a sum:

log P(c | d) = log P(c) + Σw in d count(w) · log P(w | c) + const

Training is counting: P(w | c) is how often w appears in documents of class c, divided by all words in class c, with Laplace smoothing (add α, usually 1, to every count) so an unseen word does not zero the product. The assumption is false (“new” and “york” are not independent), yet the classifier often ranks classes well, because errors in the probabilities tend to push in the same direction for every class. Variants differ in what they count: multinomial NB uses word counts; Bernoulli NB models presence and absence of every vocabulary word; complement NB estimates each class’s word distribution from all the other classes, which is more stable when classes are imbalanced.

from sklearn.naive_bayes import MultinomialNB
cv = CountVectorizer(ngram_range=(1, 2), min_df=2); X = cv.fit_transform(train_texts)
nb = MultinomialNB(alpha=1.0).fit(X, y)                 # one pass of counting: seconds
nb.feature_log_prob_[1] - nb.feature_log_prob_[0]           # per-word log-likelihood ratio: the evidence each word gives

The discriminative models won on the two large datasets: NB-SVM was best on IMDB (91.35%), the linear SVM on AG News (92.92%), and logistic regression was within half a point of both. Naive Bayes was 5–6 points behind on IMDB but only 1.8–2.4 behind on AG News, where topic words are strong, independent evidence and the naive assumption costs less. On tweet_eval emotion, with 3,257 short, imbalanced training tweets, the picture flipped: complement naive Bayes had the best macro F1 of all six (61.9%) and NB-SVM the best accuracy (68.2%), both ahead of plain logistic regression (57.5% macro F1). Complement NB earns its name there: by estimating each class from the other classes’ data it is far less dominated by the large classes, and on the two-class IMDB task it gives exactly the same predictions as multinomial NB, as the maths says it should. Everything here trains in seconds; the slowest, logistic regression on 120,000 AG News headlines, took 24.7 seconds against 2.2 for the SVM.

NB-SVM (Wang and Manning, 2012) is worth knowing: it scales each binary feature by its naive Bayes log-count ratio before fitting a linear model, so the linear model starts from naive Bayes’s view of which words matter. It is one line more than logistic regression and was the most accurate model on two of the three datasets.

Learning curves: when naive Bayes wins

Naive Bayes is generative: it models how each class produces words, then inverts with Bayes’ rule. Logistic regression is discriminative: it models the label given the words directly. A classic result (Ng and Jordan, 2001) is that the generative model reaches its best accuracy with fewer examples, while the discriminative one has a better best. The learning curve tests that on IMDB:

The classic pattern did not appear. Logistic regression on TF-IDF was ahead at every size measured, from 50 training reviews (57.3% against 56.1%) to 25,000 (90.9% against 85.4%), and naive Bayes flattened early: it gained only 2.5 points between 1,000 and 25,000 reviews, while logistic regression gained 6.4. Two things in this setup work against the textbook result. The features differ: logistic regression gets sublinear, normalised TF-IDF, which already does some of naive Bayes’s smoothing work. And with unigrams and bigrams, even 50 reviews give thousands of features, a regime in which regularised logistic regression is stable. The lesson is not that the theory is wrong, but that “naive Bayes for small data” is a hypothesis to test on your data, which takes a minute.

Class imbalance

To see imbalance clearly, the IMDB training set was cut to 9 negatives for every positive (12,500 negative, 1,388 positive reviews), and the model was tested twice: on the balanced test set and on a test set with the same 9:1 ratio. Trained naively, the model learned that “negative” is almost always right and found only 29.8% of the positive reviews. Two standard fixes, measured:

Accuracy on the 9:1 test barely separates them (90–95%), because 90% is free. Class weights doubled recall and gave the best F1; a threshold of 0.1 found 87% of positives but at 50% precision, half of its flags wrong. Neither is “correct”: the threshold is where you choose your trade-off (P7), and class weights move the whole model. Report recall and precision for the rare class, not accuracy.

Calibration

Naive Bayes multiplies one probability per word, and words are not independent: a review that says “great” also tends to say “excellent” and “wonderful”, and naive Bayes counts that one piece of evidence three times. Its probabilities are therefore pushed to the extremes. On IMDB it gave 92.7% of test reviews more than 99% confidence, and it was right on 87.8% of those; in its 90–99% band it was right only 57.7% of the time, barely better than a coin. Its log-loss was 2.315, ten times logistic regression’s 0.238. Logistic regression was again mildly underconfident, as in P6: in its 70–80% band it was right 84.2% of the time. Use naive Bayes for its ranking, never for its probabilities; if you need probabilities, calibrate (Chapter 30) or use logistic regression.

Error analysis

A score says how often a model is wrong; only reading its mistakes says why. The method is simple and almost always skipped: take a random sample of errors, not just the most confident, read each one, and tag it with a reason. Then count the tags. The counts tell you what to fix next, and whether a bigger model would help at all.

The best IMDB logistic regression made 2,257 errors on 25,000 test reviews. Two samples were read. The 15 most confident errors are dominated by labels: at least 7 read as mislabelled, such as a review labelled negative that says “This movie was pure genius … I give it 9.5/10”, and one labelled positive that opens “It is really unfortunate that a movie so well produced turns out to be such a disappointment”. IMDB’s labels come from the star rating the reviewer gave, which sometimes disagrees with what they wrote. Another was sarcasm the model could not see: “Master P’s acting skills make you actually believe he is Italian!”, correctly labelled negative.

Forty random errors, tagged by reading the opening of each review, look different:

Most errors are reviews that a bag of words genuinely cannot read: a verdict that comes after a long run of praise, or positive words aimed at a different film. Better features help only some of these; models that read in order and in context (Chapters 9 and 17) help more. The label-noise errors are a different problem, and no model will fix them; they also set a ceiling on the score anyone can reach.

Use case: an email spam filter for a small business

A firm wants to filter spam from a shared inbox with 600 labelled emails, 15% spam. Naive Bayes on word counts is trained first and is already decent, as learning curves on small data predict. Accuracy is misleading at this balance, so they report spam recall and, more importantly, the rate of real emails sent to spam, which costs customers; the threshold is raised until that is under 1 in 500. Six months later they have 20,000 labelled emails and logistic regression on TF-IDF overtakes NB. Reading 40 of its mistakes shows half are newsletters the staff actually subscribed to, a labelling question, not a modelling one.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Assuming naive Bayes wins on small data: here logistic regression led at every size from 50 to 25,000 examples; test it.
  • Training on imbalanced data with a 0.5 threshold: at 9:1 the model found 29.8% of positives while scoring 93% accuracy.
  • Trusting naive Bayes probabilities: 92.7% of its predictions claimed over 99% confidence; in its 90–99% band it was right 57.7% of the time.
  • Reading only the most confident errors: they are dominated by label noise; sample errors at random as well.
  • Forgetting smoothing: without it a single unseen word sets a class’s probability to zero.
  • Comparing models on one random split without an interval (P7).

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How does naive Bayes classify a text, and why does it work despite its “naive” assumption?

How a strong answer sounds
It applies Bayes’ rule: the probability of a class given the text is proportional to the class’s prior times the probability of the text under that class, and it estimates the latter as a product of per-word probabilities, one for each word, as if words were independent given the class. Training is just counting words per class, with add-one smoothing so unseen words do not zero everything, and prediction is a sum of log-probabilities. The independence assumption is false, but each word still pushes the score in the right direction, so the ranking of classes is often good. What breaks is the probabilities: correlated words are counted as independent evidence, so it is badly overconfident. On IMDB it put 93% of predictions above 99% confidence while scoring 85% accuracy.

Deeper: You have 300 labelled examples, 10% of them positive. How would you build and evaluate a classifier?

How a strong answer sounds
With 30 positives, every decision is about the rare class. I would use stratified cross-validation rather than one split, and report positive-class precision, recall and the precision-recall curve rather than accuracy, which is 90% for doing nothing. I would compare naive Bayes, complement naive Bayes and regularised logistic regression with class weights; on small imbalanced text data complement NB is a strong baseline (it had the best macro F1 on a 3,000-tweet emotion set). Then choose the threshold from the cost of each error, read every false negative, and check label quality, because with 30 positives two wrong labels are 7% of the class. If performance is not good enough, getting more positives, by labelling or by an LLM pass over unlabelled data, usually beats any modelling change.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Naive Bayes is counting plus Bayes’ rule in log space, with smoothing; logistic regression learns weights directly.
  • Measured on three datasets: logistic regression, linear SVM and NB-SVM led on large data; complement NB had the best macro F1 on small, imbalanced tweets.
  • Logistic regression beat naive Bayes at every training size here, from 50 to 25,000 reviews: test the small-data rule, don’t assume it.
  • For imbalance, class weights or a lower threshold; for probabilities, never naive Bayes (93% of its predictions claimed over 99% confidence).
  • Read a random sample of errors and count the reasons before choosing what to fix.

Check your understanding

What is the “naive” assumption in naive Bayes?

It lets P(document | class) be a product of per-word probabilities, a sum in log space.

Why does naive Bayes use Laplace (add-α) smoothing?

Without it, one unseen word multiplies the product by zero (P4).

According to the generative–discriminative trade-off, which is usually true?

That is the theory (Ng and Jordan, 2001). In this chapter’s IMDB measurements logistic regression still led at every size, so treat it as something to test.
