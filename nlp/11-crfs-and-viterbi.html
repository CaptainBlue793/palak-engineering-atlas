CRFs & Structured Prediction

The tagger of Chapter 10 chooses each token’s tag on its own. It knows the context through the BiLSTM, but not the tags it gave the neighbours, so nothing stops it from writing I-PER straight after O, or from labelling half a name as a person and the other half as an organisation. Structured prediction scores the whole tag sequence at once, and a single algorithm, Viterbi, finds the best sequence among exponentially many.

This chapter builds a hidden Markov model tagger by counting, writes Viterbi from scratch, adds a conditional random field to the BiLSTM, and measures what each change buys. Viterbi beat greedy decoding by 0.9 points on an HMM POS tagger and changed 1,487 of 3,453 sentences. The CRF layer raised NER span F1 from 84.07 to 86.26. It also cut invalid tag sequences from about 140 per run to 5, 3, 2, at four times the training time.

Why independent tags fail

A softmax tagger maximises each token’s tag probability separately. The most probable sequence and the sequence of most probable tags are not the same thing. If “Bank” is 51% B-ORG and “of” 55% I-LOC, the independent choice is B-ORG I-LOC, which no annotator would write. In BIO the damage is limited, because the decoder repairs an orphaned I- tag into a new entity; but each repair is usually a wrong boundary, and wrong boundaries cost twice in strict span F1 (P7).

Measured with Chapter 10’s BiLSTM tagger, the softmax version produced 168 · 136 · 134 orphaned I- tags in three runs on the CoNLL-2003 test set. A typical one: the person “Nadim Ladki” tagged O, I-ORG, a non-start followed by a continuation of the wrong type. In “Takuya Takagi scored … a Hiroshige Yanagimoto cross”, both names came out as a mixture of PER and ORG pieces. Each token decision was locally plausible; the sequence was not.

HMMs vs CRFs

A hidden Markov model is a generative story: each tag depends on the previous tag (a transition probability), and each word is generated by its tag (an emission probability). Both are counted from training data, exactly like the n-gram models of Chapter 5, and the best tag sequence for a sentence maximises P(tags) · P(words | tags). Its weakness is that each word may depend only on its own tag, so features like “is capitalised” or “ends in -ing” or “the next word is Inc.” cannot be used.

A conditional random field (Lafferty et al., 2001) models P(tags | words) directly, as a score for the whole sequence: the sum of per-token emission scores, which can come from any features or from a BiLSTM, plus a learned transition score for each adjacent pair of tags, normalised over all possible sequences. Training maximises the log-probability of the gold sequence; the normaliser, a sum over every possible tagging, is computed exactly with the forward algorithm, a logsumexp version of Viterbi.

The Viterbi algorithm

With K tags and n tokens there are Kn sequences: 930 for a 30-word sentence with nine NER tags. Viterbi finds the best in O(n · K2) with dynamic programming. Keep, for every tag at position t, the score of the best sequence ending in that tag, δt(k) = maxj [δt−1(j) + T(j, k)] + emissiont(k), and a backpointer to the j that achieved it. At the end, take the best final tag and follow the backpointers. Step through it in the lab.

def viterbi(emit, T, start):                         # emit: (n, K) log-scores, T: (K, K)
    d = start + emit[0]; back = []
    for t in range(1, len(emit)):
        s = d[:, None] + T                                # (K prev, K next)
        back.append(s.argmax(0)); d = s.max(0) + emit[t]
    path = [int(d.argmax())]
    for b in reversed(back): path.append(int(b[path[-1]]))
    return path[::-1]

Measured: HMM, greedy vs Viterbi

An HMM POS tagger, built by counting tag transitions and word emissions in the 14,041 training sentences, scored 88.03% with Viterbi decoding and 87.16% decoding greedily, left to right. That is a modest 0.87 points, but Viterbi changed at least one tag in 1,487 of the 3,453 test sentences: in most of them it fixed one or two tags where a later word overturned an early local choice, as with “most” in “China controlled most of the match”, which greedy decoding tagged RBS and Viterbi correctly JJS. Viterbi took 0.37 seconds for the whole test set, three times greedy’s 0.12, still trivial. The HMM itself is weak: 88.03% is just below the most-frequent-tag baseline of Chapter 10 (88.41%), because its emissions cannot use spelling or capitalisation, and on unseen words it got 51.01%. That limitation is what CRFs remove.

It cost about four times the training time here, because the forward algorithm is a Python loop over positions; optimised CRF implementations close most of that gap. The learned transition matrix (in the lab) rediscovered the BIO grammar: O → I-PER scored -1.11, B-PER → I-PER 0.96, B-PER → I-LOC -1.62.

Use case: parsing addresses into fields

A delivery company splits free-text addresses into house number, street, town and postcode. A per-token classifier gets most tokens right but produces sequences like STREET POSTCODE STREET, or a postcode split across two fields, which break the downstream geocoder. Adding a CRF layer, with hard constraints that forbid impossible transitions (a postcode cannot be followed by a street within one address), removes every structurally invalid output, and the transition matrix it learns is short enough to read and check with the operations team. The gain in token accuracy is small; the gain in addresses parsed with no invalid structure is what mattered.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Decoding greedily when tags depend on each other: on an HMM POS tagger it cost 0.9 points and changed 1,487 test sentences.
  • Expecting a CRF to fix weak features: the HMM, a sequence model with weak emissions, scored 88.03%, below the most-frequent-tag baseline; the CRF helped the BiLSTM because its emissions were already good.
  • Forgetting the start and end transitions, so sequences may begin with I-PER.
  • Computing the CRF normaliser naively: enumerating Kn sequences is impossible; use the forward algorithm in log space.
  • Comparing a CRF and a softmax tagger on one seed: differences of half a point are within seed noise here.
  • Assuming HMMs are obsolete: they are still a fast, interpretable baseline when labelled data is small.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What does the Viterbi algorithm do, and why is it efficient?

How a strong answer sounds
It finds the highest-scoring tag sequence for a sentence without trying every sequence. For each position and each tag it keeps the score of the best sequence ending there, computed from the previous position’s scores plus the transition and the current emission, and a pointer to where that best came from. At the end it takes the best final tag and follows the pointers back. That is n × K² work instead of Kⁿ: for 9 NER tags and 30 words, about 2,400 steps instead of 4·10²⁸. On a CoNLL HMM tagger it decoded 3,453 sentences in 0.37 seconds and beat greedy decoding by 0.9 points.

Deeper: When would you add a CRF layer on top of a neural tagger, and when not?

How a strong answer sounds
Add one when output structure matters and the encoder is not already strong enough to get it right. On CoNLL-2003 a CRF on a BiLSTM added 2.2 F1 and cut invalid sequences from about 140 per run to a handful, so for a BiLSTM it is worth it. With a fine-tuned BERT the gain is usually a few tenths of a point, because BERT’s contextual vectors already make consistent decisions, and the CRF adds training time and complexity. I would add it if invalid outputs break something downstream, such as an address parser, or use constrained decoding (forbid invalid transitions at inference only) as a cheaper middle ground, and measure either against the plain head over several seeds.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Independent per-token decisions can produce invalid or inconsistent tag sequences.
  • HMMs count transitions and emissions; CRFs score whole sequences with arbitrary features and learned transitions.
  • Viterbi finds the best of Kn sequences in O(n·K²); the forward algorithm sums over them for training.
  • Measured: Viterbi beat greedy decoding by 0.9 points on an HMM POS tagger; the HMM itself sat below a most-frequent-tag baseline.
  • A CRF on the BiLSTM raised NER F1 from 84.07 to 86.26 and cut invalid sequences from about 140 to 5, 3, 2 per run, and its transitions relearned the BIO rules.
