Counting & Probability for Language

Every language model, from a 1990s trigram model to a modern LLM, is a machine for one number: the probability of the next word given the words so far. Before any neural network, that number came from counting, and the facts counting reveals still shape every tokeniser, vocabulary and loss function in the course.

This prerequisite counts the words in the first 20,000 documents of C4, 7.2 million of them. A hundred word types cover 44% of all the text, while 46% of the vocabulary appears exactly once. The vocabulary never stops growing. And multiplying the probabilities of a sentence’s words gives exactly zero in 32-bit floats after 16 words, which is why everything in NLP is done in logs.

  • Count tokens and types and read a frequency table
  • Recognise Zipf’s law and what it means for vocabularies
  • Estimate conditional probabilities such as P(next word | word)
  • Use log-probabilities to avoid underflow
  • Measure entropy and perplexity in bits

Tokens and types

A token is one occurrence of a word in running text; a type is a distinct word. “The cat saw the dog” has five tokens and four types. Here words are lower-cased runs of letters, with an optional apostrophe part, so don’t written with a straight apostrophe is one word:

import re, collections
WORD = re.compile(r"[a-z]+(?:'[a-z]+)?")
toks = [w for d in docs for w in WORD.findall(d.lower())]
cnt = collections.Counter(toks)
len(toks), len(cnt)        # (7212270, 140665): 7.2 M tokens, 140,665 types
cnt.most_common(5)          # [('the', 371587), ('and', 216709), ('to', 204201), ('of', 178311), ('a', 164509)]

The table has a lesson about tokenisation already: the 22nd most common “word” is s, 33,364 times. It comes from it’s and Palak’s written with a curly apostrophe (’), which the pattern does not include, so the word is split in two. The tokeniser’s rules decide what counts as a word, and every count downstream inherits them.

Zipf’s law: a few words are most of the text

Sort types by frequency and plot frequency against rank on log–log axes, and you get very nearly a straight line. That is Zipf’s law: the frequency of the word at rank r is roughly proportional to 1/rs, with s close to 1. Fitted on ranks 10 to 10,000 here, s = 1.12. In practice it means two things at once:

A small vocabulary covers most of the text, and the long tail never ends. A word-level model with a 10,000-word vocabulary would mark one token in every twelve as unknown. Words seen exactly once, called hapax legomena, are almost half of all types, and they are where names, typos, technical terms and new words live. Their share also gives a classic estimate: by the Good–Turing argument, the probability that the next token is a word you have never seen is about the share of tokens that are words seen once, here 0.9%.

The vocabulary never stops growing

Read the corpus word by word and count distinct types as you go. After a million tokens there were already about 45,000 types, and every further million added more. The growth fits Heaps’ law, V ≈ K·Nβ, with β = 0.57 and K = 16.5 here: double the text and the vocabulary grows by about half. Fitted on the curve, it predicts 142,600 types at 7.2 million tokens; the count was 140,665. This is why word-level vocabularies are always too small for new text, and why modern models split rare words into subword pieces (Chapter 2): with subwords, nothing is ever out of vocabulary.

Conditional probability: the next word

Counting pairs of adjacent words gives the first language model. The probability of the next word given the previous one is estimated by dividing counts:

big = collections.Counter(zip(toks, toks[1:]))
def p_next(w, nxt): return big[(w, nxt)] / cnt[w]   # P(nxt | w) = count(w nxt) / count(w)
p_next("united", "states")             # 0.6417
p_next("the", "most")                  # 0.0107: after "the", nothing is likely

How much a word constrains the next one varies enormously. After united (1,429 times), states followed 64% of the time and kingdom 7%. After new (13,726 times), the top choice york was only 9%, followed by year, zealand and jersey. After the, the most likely next word, most, had just over 1%. The estimate also has an obvious flaw: any pair never seen in the corpus gets probability exactly zero, however plausible it is. Smoothing, which Chapter 5 covers, is the fix.

Log-probabilities and underflow

The probability of a whole sequence is a product of per-word probabilities, and each is small: the average word here had probability around 10−3. Multiply sixteen of them and you are below 10−45, the smallest positive 32-bit float. Measured on the corpus’s first sentence (“the woman who died after falling from a bridge over the …”), the running product of unigram probabilities became exactly 0.0 in float32 at word 16, and in float64 at word 96. A zero is not a small number: every comparison between two zero-probability documents is a tie, and the log of it is minus infinity.

The fix is to add logs instead of multiplying probabilities. The log-probability of 800 words was −6,111 in natural log units: an ordinary number. This is why classifiers output log-probabilities, why F.cross_entropy takes logits rather than probabilities, and why torch.logsumexp exists: it adds probabilities that are stored as logs without ever leaving log space.

import math, torch
sum(math.log(p[w]) for w in words)              # fine for any length; math.prod(...) is 0.0 by word 96
torch.logsumexp(torch.tensor([-1000., -1001.]), 0)  # -999.69 = log(e^-1000 + e^-1001), without underflow

Entropy and surprise

The surprise of a word is −log2 P(word) bits: a word with probability 1/8 carries 3 bits. Entropy is the average surprise, H = −Σ P(w) log2 P(w). For the unigram distribution here, H = 10.93 bits per word. Its exponent, 2H = 1,948, is the perplexity: a model that knows only word frequencies is, on average, as uncertain as if it were choosing uniformly among about 1,950 words. Every language model in this course is judged by how far it pushes that number down by using context, and its training loss, cross-entropy, is the same quantity measured on the model’s predictions.

🧾
Use case: autocomplete for a help-centre search box

A team builds query suggestions from a year of search logs. They count word pairs and triples and rank completions by P(next | previous words), exactly as above. Three things go wrong in the first week, each a fact from this chapter. Long completions score as exactly zero once they multiply probabilities in float32, so they move to summed log-probabilities. Any query containing a word that never appeared in the logs, such as a new product’s name, gets no suggestions, because unseen pairs have probability zero; they back off to shorter contexts and add smoothing. And their 20,000-word vocabulary keeps missing words, because, as Heaps’ law predicts, new words arrive as long as people keep typing.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Multiplying probabilities: the product was exactly zero in float32 after 16 words and in float64 after 96; add log-probabilities.
  • Treating unseen as impossible: any pair not in the counts has P = 0 until you smooth.
  • Expecting a fixed vocabulary to cover new text: types kept growing as N0.57; 46% of them were seen only once.
  • Forgetting that tokenisation defines the counts: s was the 22nd most common word because of curly apostrophes.
  • Mixing log bases: natural logs give nats, log2 gives bits; perplexity is eH or 2H to match.
  • Passing probabilities to F.cross_entropy, which expects logits and applies log-softmax itself.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Why do NLP systems work with log-probabilities instead of probabilities?

How a strong answer sounds
Because the probability of a sequence is a product of many small numbers, and floating point runs out. On C4, multiplying word probabilities gave exactly zero in float32 after 16 words and in float64 after 96. Taking logs turns the product into a sum, which stays an ordinary number for any length (−6,111 for 800 words). It is also more convenient: sums are easy to differentiate, and cross-entropy loss is just the average negative log-probability of the right answer. When you do need to add probabilities, logsumexp does it without leaving log space.

Deeper: Your word-level model keeps hitting unknown words in production, even after you doubled the vocabulary. Why, and what do you do?

How a strong answer sounds
Word frequencies follow Zipf’s law, and vocabulary keeps growing with the amount of text: on 7.2 million words of C4 it grew as about N0.57, and 46% of the 140,665 types appeared once. Doubling the vocabulary only covers a few more rare words. New names, typos and product terms keep arriving, and the Good–Turing estimate says about 1% of upcoming tokens will be new. The fix is to stop using whole words: a subword tokeniser such as BPE or WordPiece splits any unseen word into known pieces, so nothing is out of vocabulary. If you must keep words, map rare ones to an UNK token during training so the model learns what to do with it.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Word frequencies follow Zipf’s law (slope 1.12 here): 100 types are 44% of the text, and 46% of types appear once.
  • Vocabulary grows without limit, as N0.57 here: word-level vocabularies are always too small, which is why subword tokenisers exist.
  • P(next | word) is a ratio of counts, and unseen pairs get zero until you smooth.
  • Products of probabilities underflow (float32 at 16 words, float64 at 96): add log-probabilities.
  • Entropy is average surprise in bits; unigram entropy here was 10.93 bits, a perplexity of about 1,950.
