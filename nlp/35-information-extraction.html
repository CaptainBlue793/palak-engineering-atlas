Information Extraction: Relations & Linking

Named-entity recognition finds the names in a text. Information extraction asks what they have to do with each other and which real-world thing each one refers to: that “Apple” in this sentence is the company, and that the company acquired another. The output is a set of (subject, relation, object) triples that a database or knowledge graph can store and query.

This chapter trains a relation classifier, tests it with noisy labels, links ambiguous names, and extracts triples from news. Classifying from the two entity markers beat classifying from [CLS] by 2.1 points of macro-F1 (81.22 against 79.12); randomising 40% of the training labels cost only 5.5. Linking 24 ambiguous mentions by context got 83.3% right against 33.3% for always choosing the most familiar sense. And running the pipeline over 300 news sentences kept just 10 triples, several of them wrong and four of them from one name split in two.

  • Frame relation extraction as classification over a marked entity pair
  • Compare [CLS] pooling with entity-marker pooling
  • Measure how label noise from distant supervision hurts
  • Link ambiguous mentions to a knowledge base by context
  • Assemble NER, relation extraction and linking into triples, and read what comes out

Relation extraction with entity markers

Given a sentence and two entity mentions, predict the relation between them, or “Other”. SemEval-2010 Task 8 has nine relation types, each in two directions (Cause-Effect(e1,e2) is not Cause-Effect(e2,e1)), plus Other. The standard recipe (Baldini Soares et al., 2019) wraps the entities in marker tokens, <e1>…</e1> and <e2>…</e2>, adds them to the vocabulary, fine-tunes BERT, and classifies either from [CLS] or from the concatenated hidden states at the two opening markers. Reading the relation from the vectors at the two opening markers, which sit exactly where the entities start, was 2.1 points better than reading it from [CLS], with both seeds of each setup within half a point; the published entity-start result with BERT-large is about 89 on the official (direction-merged) score, so these base-model numbers on the stricter directed score are in range. The relations differ greatly in difficulty. Cause-effect and destination relations were easiest (Entity-Destination(e1,e2) 92.3, Cause-Effect(e1,e2) 93.0, Cause-Effect(e2,e1) 93.1); Member-Collection(e1,e2) 65.7, Instrument-Agency(e1,e2) 71.8, Instrument-Agency(e2,e1) 81.5 were hardest, and Entity-Destination(e2,e1) scored 0.0 because it has essentially no examples in the data, which a macro average weights as heavily as any other class. Other, the catch-all, is 16.7% of the test set; accuracy including it (83.47%) looks higher than macro-F1 without it (81.22) and hides the weak relations.

Distant supervision and label noise

Hand-labelling relations is slow, so large systems use distant supervision (Mintz et al., 2009): if a knowledge base says (Paris, capital-of, France), label every sentence mentioning both as capital-of. Many such sentences say nothing about capitals, so the labels are noisy. Random label noise is a simpler stand-in that shows how sensitive the model is.

Replacing 20% of the training labels with random relations lowered macro-F1 from 81.02 to 78.88, and 40% to 75.56: the model learnt mostly from the 60% that were right. Fine-tuned transformers are fairly tolerant of random noise because random wrong labels do not form a consistent pattern to learn. Distant supervision’s noise is worse than this test suggests, because it is systematic: the same entity pairs are mislabelled in the same way every time they co-occur (every sentence mentioning Paris and France tagged capital-of), and systematic noise is learnable. Methods for distant supervision therefore aggregate over all the sentences for an entity pair (multi-instance learning) rather than trusting each one.

Entity linking

“Jordan scored 63 points” and “tourists in Jordan” mention different entities. Entity linking maps each mention to a knowledge-base entry. Systems such as BLINK (Wu et al., 2020) use a bi-encoder: embed the mention with its context, embed each candidate entity’s description, pick the nearest. Here the knowledge base is 24 hand-written entries and the encoder is a general sentence model, a deliberately small test of the idea.

Comparing each mention’s sentence with the descriptions of its candidate entities picked the right one for 83.3% of the 24 test sentences; always picking the first-listed, most familiar sense got 33.3%. The four failures are instructive: “Paris posted another selfie from her Beverly Hills mansion.” → Paris, the capital city of France, on the Seine; “Tourists in Jordan visit Petra and the Dead Sea.” → the Jordan River, flowing into the Dead Sea; “Pilgrims are baptised in the Jordan.” → Jordan, a country in the Middle East; “Lawmakers in Washington passed the budget.” → Washington State in the Pacific Northwest. In each, the deciding clue (a selfie and a mansion; Petra; baptism; lawmakers) is not something a general sentence encoder associates strongly with the entity’s short description, and the description that shares surface words wins (“Dead Sea” appears in the river’s description, so the tourist sentence went to the river). Real linkers use richer entity descriptions, entity popularity as a prior, and encoders trained on linking data. This test is deliberately tiny, 24 hand-written sentences, so it shows the mechanism, not a benchmark score.

Triples from news

Running NER, pairing entities and classifying each pair with the SemEval model over 300 news sentences, then keeping pairs predicted as a relation with probability above 0.8, left only 10 triples (Entity-Destination(e1,e2) × 2, Entity-Origin(e1,e2) × 7, Instrument-Agency(e2,e1) × 1). Reading them shows two failures that a single model’s score never reveals. First, the schema does not fit the text: SemEval’s nine relations describe common nouns (a pipe and its burst, a cup of coffee), not people and organisations, so the facts that matter in news (detained at, national of, sold to) have no box, and the model forces pairs into the nearest one. Some triples are defensible: “Getty” Entity-Destination “Texaco” from “the sale of Getty to Texaco”, and Rashid’s Entity-Origin London and Istanbul (he is from London and flew from Istanbul). Others are plainly wrong: “UK” Entity-Origin “London”, “London” Entity-Origin “Istanbul”. Second, errors compound: our simple span decoding split “Yahya” into two entities (“Ya” and “hya Rashid”) because the NER model tagged a word piece as the start of a new name, and both fragments went on to produce triples. An extraction pipeline needs a relation schema built for the target domain, entity-level decoding that respects word boundaries, and human review of a sample of its final output.

Use case: a supplier-risk knowledge graph

A procurement team wants to know when a supplier is acquired, sued or sanctioned. A pipeline reads business news nightly: NER finds companies, a relation model trained on a few thousand labelled sentences tags acquired-by, sued-by and sanctioned-by, and a linker matches each company name to the internal supplier list, where “Apex” could be one of six suppliers. Every new edge touching a top-100 supplier goes to an analyst for a yes or no before anyone acts; those answers retrain the relation model monthly and set the confidence threshold so that analysts see about twenty items a day.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Classifying relations from [CLS] when entity positions are known: entity-start pooling was 2.1 points better here.
  • Using a relation schema from another domain: SemEval’s common-noun relations turned 300 news sentences into 10 triples, few of them useful.
  • Decoding entities from word pieces without respecting word boundaries: “Yahya” became two entities, and both became triples.
  • Ignoring direction: “A acquired B” and “B acquired A” are different facts; score directed relations.
  • Reporting accuracy with a large Other class: predicting Other often looks good; use macro-F1 without it.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How does a BERT model extract the relation between two entities in a sentence?

How a strong answer sounds
Mark the two entities in the sentence with special tokens, <e1> … </e1> and <e2> … </e2>, add the markers to the vocabulary, and fine-tune BERT as a classifier over the relation labels, including direction (Cause-Effect(e1,e2) differs from Cause-Effect(e2,e1)) and an Other class. The classifier reads either the [CLS] vector or, better, the concatenated vectors at the two opening markers, which encode each entity in context. On SemEval-2010 Task 8 that gave 81.22 macro-F1 against 79.12 from [CLS]. The model only classifies a given pair; finding the entities (NER) and choosing which pairs to classify are separate steps.

Deeper: Design a system that builds a knowledge graph of company acquisitions from news.

How a strong answer sounds
Define the schema first: acquired-by, with acquirer, target, date and amount, and decide what counts (announced, completed, rumoured). Pipeline: news ingestion; NER for organisations with word-level decoding; an acquisition classifier over organisation pairs in the same sentence, trained on a few thousand labelled sentences, possibly bootstrapped with distant supervision from a known-deals database, aggregated over all sentences that mention a pair; entity linking of both organisations to a company database with aliases and tickers, using context and popularity priors; then normalisation (dates, amounts) and deduplication of the same deal across articles. Evaluate each stage, but above all measure precision of the final triples on a human-checked sample, because errors compound across stages. Route low-confidence edges to an analyst and feed their decisions back as training data.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Measured: entity-start pooling 81.22 macro-F1 against [CLS] 79.12 on SemEval-2010 Task 8; the weakest relations scored far below the average.
  • Measured: 20% and 40% random labels cost 2.1 and 5.5 points; distant supervision’s systematic noise is harder.
  • Measured: context-based linking 83.3% against 33.3% for the most familiar sense; the news pipeline kept 10 triples from 300 sentences, few of them useful.
  • Relation extraction is classification over a marked pair of entities, with direction.
  • Pipelines compound errors; measure precision on a human-checked sample of the final triples.
