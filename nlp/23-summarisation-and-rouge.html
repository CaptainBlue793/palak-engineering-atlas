Summarisation & ROUGE

A summary should be short, cover what matters and say only true things. Extractive summarisers copy sentences from the source; abstractive ones write new text, which reads better and can say things the source never said. ROUGE, the standard score, counts n-grams shared with a human reference and checks none of those three properties directly.

This chapter summarises 500 news articles seven ways, writes ROUGE from scratch, and measures what ROUGE misses. The first three sentences of each article scored a ROUGE-2 of 16.85; BART fine-tuned on this dataset 20.84, and DistilBART 21.3. Only about 10% of the abstractive summaries’ bigrams were new; the human reference highlights had 50%. And the faithfulness check flagged more unsupported names in the references (7.06%) than in any system’s output.

Extractive baselines

The simplest summary of a news article is its first three sentences, lead-3: journalists put the most important facts first. TextRank (Mihalcea and Tarau, 2004) builds a graph of sentences linked by word overlap, here TF-IDF cosine, and picks the sentences with the highest PageRank, the most “central”. An oracle picks, with knowledge of the reference, the three sentences that maximise ROUGE-2: no system can do this, but it shows how high extraction could go.

Abstractive summarisation

An encoder–decoder fine-tuned on article–summary pairs writes the summary token by token. BART-large fine-tuned on CNN/DailyMail and its distilled version DistilBART are the standard models; Flan-T5 is an instruction-tuned T5 asked to “summarise this news article in three sentences” with no summarisation fine-tuning of its own on this dataset. Decoding uses beam search (4 beams), a minimum length and a ban on repeated trigrams, without which generated summaries loop.

ROUGE from scratch

ROUGE (Lin, 2004) compares a summary with a reference. ROUGE-N is the F-score of shared n-grams (precision: shared / n-grams in the summary; recall: shared / n-grams in the reference). ROUGE-L uses the longest common subsequence, words appearing in the same order but not necessarily adjacent, so it rewards sentence-level word order. Edit the summary in the lab and watch the three scores.

def rouge_n(h, r, n):
    a, b = ngrams(h, n), ngrams(r, n); ov = sum((a & b).values())       # clipped overlap
    p, rc = ov / max(1, sum(a.values())), ov / max(1, sum(b.values()))
    return 0 if ov == 0 else 2 * p * rc / (p + rc)
# ROUGE-L: same F-score with lcs(h, r) as the overlap

Our implementation and Google’s rouge_score package (without stemming) gave identical scores on every system below, to two decimals. That agreement is the point of writing it yourself: when you then read a paper’s ROUGE, you know the score depends on tokenisation, stemming and whether ROUGE-L is computed per sentence or over the whole summary, and that numbers computed differently cannot be compared.

Lead-3 beat TextRank by 5.1 ROUGE-2 points: in news, position is a better signal of importance than centrality. The two fine-tuned BART models beat lead-3 by about four points, with DistilBART, the smaller distilled model, marginally ahead of BART-large (21.3 against 20.84, a difference that one 500-article sample does not establish) and 1.7× faster to run. Flan-T5-base, which was only asked to summarise, landed at lead-3’s level (17.86) while writing the shortest abstractive summaries (55.6 words, close to the references’ 52.3); ROUGE rewards length, and lead-3 at 80.9 words is longer than every abstractive system. The oracle, which picks the three article sentences that best match the reference, reached 33.89: the ceiling for extraction is far above what any system got.

The abstractive models are less abstractive than they look. Only 12.6% of DistilBART’s bigrams and 9.9% of BART-large’s do not appear in the article; the human highlights have 50.3%. Trained to maximise likelihood of reference-like text from an article it can copy from, the model learns that copying is safe.

Faithfulness and hallucination

The check is crude on purpose: extract named entities and numbers from each summary with spaCy and flag any that do not appear verbatim in the article. It flagged 2.32% of DistilBART’s entities, 1.59% of BART-large’s and 2.35% of Flan-T5’s, almost no numbers (0.38%, 0.1%, 0.54%), and nothing for the extractive systems, as it should. Reading the flags is essential, because most of them are false alarms: possessives (spaCy keeps the ’s in “Arsene Wenger’s”, so the exact string is not in the article), entity boundaries that spaCy drew oddly (“Atletico 4-0” tagged as a person), and product names mistaken for people. A few are real: in an article about Lyvette Crespo, BART-large named her husband “Daniel Crespi” and DistilBART wrote “Crepo”, wrong names delivered with the same fluency as the right ones.

The reference highlights were flagged most of all, 7.06% of their entities and 7.46% of their numbers. CNN and Daily Mail editors wrote them with knowledge beyond the article text (titles, full names, context) and in different wording, so “not in the article” does not mean “false”. It does mean the training data teaches models to add things the source does not say, and that ROUGE against such references cannot measure faithfulness. Dedicated faithfulness metrics (entailment-based checks, question-answering-based checks such as QAGS) and human review are the tools; this check is a cheap first filter.

Length and lead bias

Matching each reference sentence to the article sentence it overlaps most shows where the highlights come from: 40% from the article’s first three sentences, 15% from the very first, and 25% from sentence 11 or later. Lead-k for k = 1 to 5 scored ROUGE-2 9.94, 15.15, 16.85, 16.96, 16.63: the score rises to three sentences and then flattens as extra sentences add words that dilute precision. That lead bias is a property of the dataset, which is why a model that does well on CNN/DailyMail can learn little more than “copy the top” and why results on other genres (meetings, scientific papers, dialogue) can be very different.

Use case: summarising incident reports for managers

An operations team wants three-line summaries of long incident reports. A fine-tuned BART produces fluent summaries with a ROUGE-2 well above lead-3, and the team nearly ships it, until an engineer notices a summary saying the outage lasted “four hours” when the report said forty minutes. A faithfulness check like this chapter’s, flagging any number, name or date in a summary that is not in the report, catches it and about one summary in ten. They ship with that check: flagged summaries show the extractive lead sentences instead, and the real metric they track is how often managers had to open the full report to correct something.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Skipping the lead-3 baseline: it scored 16.85 ROUGE-2 here, above TextRank (11.76) and level with a prompted Flan-T5 (17.86).
  • Treating a high ROUGE as a faithful summary: “Daniel Crespi” costs one token of overlap and is the wrong man.
  • Reading every flagged entity as a hallucination: most flags here were possessives and entity-boundary errors; the references themselves were flagged at 7.06%.
  • Comparing ROUGE across implementations without stating tokenisation, stemming and whether ROUGE-L is sentence-level or summary-level.
  • Decoding without a repetition ban: abstractive models repeat phrases; no_repeat_ngram_size=3 is standard.
  • Comparing summaries of different lengths: ROUGE F-scores move with length; match lengths or report it.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is ROUGE, and why is a good ROUGE score not enough?

How a strong answer sounds
ROUGE compares a generated summary with a human reference by counting overlap: ROUGE-1 and ROUGE-2 are F-scores over shared unigrams and bigrams, ROUGE-L over the longest common subsequence. It is cheap, reproducible and correlates with quality at the system level. But it measures overlap with one particular reference, not whether the summary is true or useful. A summary that reverses a fact by changing one word keeps almost all its ROUGE; a correct paraphrase in different words loses it. It also rewards length and copying: here lead-3, simply the first three sentences, scored 16.85 ROUGE-2, and fine-tuned models that copy 87% of their bigrams from the article did best. And it inherits reference quality: the references here contained more entities absent from the article (7.06%) than the systems did. So I report ROUGE with its settings, alongside a faithfulness check and human review of a sample.

Deeper: How would you reduce and detect hallucination in a production summariser?

How a strong answer sounds
Reduce it at the source: fine-tune on references that are supported by their documents (filtering training pairs whose summaries contain entities or numbers absent from the source), constrain decoding (copy mechanisms, or penalising tokens not in the source for names and numbers), and keep the output short and close to the text where the use allows. Detect it after generation: an entity-and-number check like this chapter’s as a cheap filter, an NLI model that checks each summary sentence is entailed by the source, or a question-answering check that asks questions of the summary and compares the answers with the source. Route flagged summaries to an extractive fallback or a human. Measure all of this on a labelled sample from the real domain, because the cheap checks have false positives (most flags here were possessives and span errors) and miss errors that keep the right names in the wrong relation.

  • Measured: 12.6% and 9.9% novel bigrams for the BART models against 50.3% for the references: “abstractive” models mostly copy.
  • Measured: 40% of reference sentences come from the article’s first three sentences, which is why lead-3 is so strong on news.
