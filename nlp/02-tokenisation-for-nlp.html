Tokenisation, Stemming & Lemmas

Before a model can count, embed or attend, text has to be cut into units: words, pieces of words or characters. That first cut decides what the model can see. Split “don’t” in two and negation becomes a separate token; lower-case “US” and it becomes “us”; keep only whole words and every new name, typo and product is unknown.

This chapter compares seven real tokenisers on awkward text, then trains the three subword algorithms used by modern models (BPE, WordPiece and Unigram) at four vocabulary sizes and measures what each does. On the same sentence they produced between 6 and 20 tokens. A 16,000-word vocabulary built from film reviews left 3.2% of review words and 13.6% of news words unknown; a 16,000-piece BPE vocabulary left none. And for a sentiment classifier, stemming and lemmatising made accuracy slightly worse, while lemmatising took 12 minutes.

Word and rule-based tokenisers

The simplest tokeniser splits on spaces, which leaves punctuation stuck to words: “good!” and “good” become different tokens. A regex such as \w+ splits on anything that is not a letter, digit or underscore, which separates punctuation but also breaks “don’t” into “don” and “t” and drops the apostrophe entirely. Rule-based tokenisers such as NLTK’s Treebank tokeniser and spaCy’s encode decades of English conventions: “don’t” becomes “do” + “n’t”, “U.S.” stays whole, “e-mail” stays one token or splits by a rule. Every choice is a decision about meaning, and none is right for every task.

On “Don’t panic: the U.S. e-mail costs £5.99 :-)”, the seven tokenisers in the lab produced between 6 and 20 tokens. scikit-learn’s default pattern kept 6, dropping “U”, “S”, “e” and “5” because it ignores one-character tokens, and the emoticon entirely. NLTK and spaCy split “Don’t” into “Do” + “n’t”, keeping the negation as a token, and kept “U.S.” whole; only spaCy and GPT-2 kept “:-)” as one token. BERT’s WordPiece lower-cased everything and cut “U.S.” into four pieces. GPT-2’s byte-level BPE marks a leading space as part of a token (shown ␣), and shows “£” as “Â£”, because it works on the two UTF-8 bytes of £ and displays each byte as a character. Pick the string you care about in the lab and compare.

Subword tokenisers in practice

Modern models use subword tokenisers: frequent words stay whole, rare words are split into frequent pieces, and anything can be spelled out from small pieces, so nothing is ever unknown. Three algorithms dominate:

AlgorithmHow the vocabulary is builtUsed by
BPE (byte-pair encoding)Start from characters; repeatedly merge the most frequent adjacent pair into a new symbol, until the vocabulary has V entries. Encoding replays the merges in order.GPT-2 onwards (on bytes), RoBERTa, Llama
WordPieceLike BPE, but merge the pair that most increases the likelihood of the training data: count(ab) / (count(a) · count(b)), which favours pairs that rarely occur apart. Pieces after the first are marked ##.BERT, DistilBERT
Unigram (SentencePiece)Start from a large candidate vocabulary and repeatedly remove the pieces whose loss least reduces the likelihood under a unigram model; segment by the most probable split.T5, XLM-R, ALBERT

Step through BPE yourself in the lab below: type any words and watch the merges form. The first merges join common letter pairs; later ones build whole frequent words; rare words stay in pieces.

Out-of-vocabulary words

A word-level model has a fixed vocabulary, and every word outside it becomes [UNK], which carries no information. P4 showed why the vocabulary can never be big enough: it grows with the text, and almost half of all word types appear once. Subword tokenisers remove the problem, but the cost moves: a rare word becomes several pieces, so sequences get longer and the model must learn to assemble meaning from parts.

The training reviews contain 74,951 distinct words. Keep the 16,000 most frequent and 3.2% of the words in new reviews are unknown; move to news headlines, a different domain, and 13.6% are, more than four times as many, because names, places and companies the reviews never mention are exactly what news is about. Even 32,000 words left 8.1% of news words unknown. The subword tokenisers, trained on the same reviews, left none, at the cost of splitting more words in the new domain: 16.4% of news words became two or more BPE pieces, against 4.2% of review words. How they split shows the mechanism: at 16,000 pieces BPE kept unwatchable whole but split covid, a word the 2011 reviews could not contain, into co + vid, the misspelt witherspooon into wither + spo + o + on, and blockchain into block + chain.

Vocabulary size trade-offs

The vocabulary size is the main dial. Smaller means more splitting; larger means a bigger embedding table and more pieces seen too rarely to learn well. Measured as pieces per word on review text, BPE went from 1.526 at 1,000 pieces to 1.197 at 4,000, 1.056 at 16,000 and 1.025 at 32,000: most of the gain comes early, and past 16,000 almost every review word is a single piece. WordPiece behaved almost identically. On news text every size split more: 1.116 pieces per word even at 32,000.

Unigram was the exception, and an instructive one. With Hugging Face’s Unigram trainer it plateaued at about 1.25 pieces per word and kept splitting a fifth of review words even at 32,000 pieces, because its likelihood objective found it cheaper to peel off frequent final letters: unwatchabl + e, re + watche + d. That is a property of this trainer on this corpus, not necessarily of every Unigram tokeniser, and it is a reminder to inspect a trained tokeniser’s output rather than trust its vocabulary size. Real models choose 30,000–50,000 pieces for one language and 100,000–250,000 for many, because each language needs its own pieces (Chapter 24).

Stemming vs lemmatisation

Both map related word forms to one unit, so a count-based model pools their evidence. A stemmer chops suffixes by rule: the Porter stemmer turns studies and studying into studi. It is fast and crude: it also merges words that are unrelated, and its output is often not a word. A lemmatiser maps each word to its dictionary form using a vocabulary and the word’s part of speech: studies → study, better → good (as an adjective), was → be. It needs a tagger, so it is slower.

Run over the review vocabulary, the Porter stemmer reduced 73,438 distinct words to 49,952 stems, a third fewer. Its largest groups show both sides. travel, travelled, traveller, travelling share a stem, as intended. But gener collects general, generous, generate and generation; organ collects organ, organic, organism and organization; univers collects university, universe and universal; and was becomes wa. Lemmatisers make different mistakes: spaCy’s small English model turned “better” in “the studies were better” into well rather than good.

from nltk.stem import PorterStemmer; import spacy
PorterStemmer().stem("university"), PorterStemmer().stem("universe")   # ('univers', 'univers'): university and universe merge
nlp = spacy.load("en_core_web_sm", disable=["parser", "ner"])
[(t.text, t.lemma_) for t in nlp("The studies were better")]   # [('The','the'), ('studies','study'), ('were','be'), ('better','well')]: a lemmatiser error

Words won, narrowly. Stemming and lemmatising both cost about 0.8 points with unigrams and almost nothing with bigrams. They throw away distinctions the classifier can use, such as tense and number, and they took 70 seconds and 12 minutes respectively, against nothing for plain words. With 25,000 training reviews the model has enough examples of each form not to need pooling; stemming helps most when data is scarce, and in search, where a query for “running” should find “runs”.

BPE pieces with only 1,000 entries lost 4 points as unigrams, because a piece like “un” or “able” means little on its own, but recovered almost all of it with bigrams (90.2%), since a pair of pieces is often a whole word. At 32,000 pieces BPE was within 0.3 points of words. Character n-grams matched words with no tokeniser at all (88.9%), which is why they are a good choice for noisy text full of misspellings. All these differences except BPE-1k unigrams are under a point, on a test set whose standard error is about 0.2 points (P7): the unit matters less than the bigrams do.

Use case: a search box for an electronics shop

A shop’s product search matches query words against product titles. Customers type “usb-c charger”, “USB C charger” and “usbc chargers”, and each finds different results, because the tokeniser keeps “usb-c” whole, splits “USB C” in two and treats “usbc” and “chargers” as unrelated to “usb” and “charger”. The team normalises case and hyphens, adds light stemming for plurals, and indexes character n-grams as a second field so “usbc” still matches. Product codes such as “RTX5070Ti” are kept whole by a rule, because splitting them into pieces made every graphics card match every query. Each change is checked against a list of 200 real queries with known correct products.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Using a different tokeniser from the one a model was trained with: the IDs mean different pieces and nothing raises an error.
  • Splitting on \w+ by default: it breaks “don’t” and drops “!” and emoticons, which carry sentiment.
  • Building a word vocabulary on one domain and using it on another: unknown words rose from 3.2% on reviews to 13.6% on news with the same 16,000 words.
  • Stemming or lemmatising by default: both cost about 0.8 points on IMDB unigrams, lemmatising took 12 minutes, and Porter merges general with generous.
  • Trusting a vocabulary size without reading the output: a 32,000-piece Unigram tokeniser still split “unwatchable” into “unwatchabl” + “e”.
  • Lower-casing for a cased model, or for tasks where case matters: “US” and “us”, “Apple” and “apple”.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Why do modern models use subword tokenisers instead of words or characters?

How a strong answer sounds
Words give a vocabulary that is never big enough: on film reviews, the 16,000 most frequent words left 3% of new review words unknown and 14% of news words, and every unknown word loses all its information. Characters never fail, but sequences become five or six times longer and each character means little on its own. Subwords sit between: frequent words stay whole and rare ones are spelled out from frequent pieces, so nothing is unknown and sequences stay short. A 16,000-piece BPE vocabulary trained on the same reviews left 0% unknown and averaged 1.06 pieces per review word. The vocabulary size trades sequence length against embedding-table size, and most of the gain comes by about 16,000 pieces for one language.

Deeper: You are building a classifier for short, noisy social-media text. Would you stem, lemmatise, use subwords or characters?

How a strong answer sounds
For a pretrained transformer I would use its own tokeniser and nothing else. For a count-based model on noisy social text I would not stem or lemmatise first: on IMDB both cost almost a point and lemmatising was slow, and lemmatisers are worse on slang and misspellings. I would compare word n-grams with character 2–5-grams, which matched words on IMDB with no tokeniser and are robust to “sooooo”, hashtags and typos, and keep emoticons, “!” and “?” as tokens because they carry sentiment that scikit-learn’s default pattern drops. Then measure each option on a validation set from the same platform, since the right unit is an empirical question.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • The tokeniser decides what a model can see; seven real tokenisers produced seven different splits of the same sentence.
  • Subword tokenisers (BPE, WordPiece, Unigram) leave nothing unknown: a 16k BPE vocabulary left 0% unknown words where 16k whole words left 3.2% in domain and 13.6% out of domain.
  • Most of the benefit of a larger vocabulary comes early: BPE averaged 1.53 pieces per review word at 1k, 1.06 at 16k and 1.03 at 32k.
  • Stemming and lemmatising did not help a sentiment classifier with enough data (−0.8 points with unigrams) and lemmatising took 12 minutes; character n-grams matched words with no tokeniser.
  • Always use the tokeniser a model was trained with; for your own count-based models, measure the unit like any other choice.
