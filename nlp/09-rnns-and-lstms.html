Recurrent Networks & LSTMs from Scratch

Bags and CNNs ignore most of word order. A recurrent network reads a sentence one token at a time and carries a hidden state forward, a running summary of everything so far, so in principle it can use any earlier word to interpret the current one. In practice the plain version forgets within a few steps, and the gated versions, the LSTM and the GRU, were the fix that powered NLP from about 2014 until transformers.

This chapter writes the cells by hand, checks them against PyTorch, and then measures the failure and the fix directly. With a forget-gate bias of 1, an LSTM learnt to remember a symbol across 20 steps perfectly; at PyTorch’s default initialisation it never left chance. On 400-token reviews the vanilla RNN trailed the LSTM by 6.1 points, and an LSTM language model reached a perplexity of 131.1 against 242.2 for Chapter 5’s best n-gram model.

The recurrent cell

A recurrent neural network keeps a hidden vector h and updates it once per token: ht = tanh(W xt + U ht−1 + b). The same W and U are used at every step, so the network can read sequences of any length. For classification, use the last h (or pool all of them); for language modelling, predict the next token from each ht.

An LSTM (long short-term memory, Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997) adds a second state, the cell c, and three gates computed from xt and ht−1, each a sigmoid between 0 and 1: the forget gate f decides how much of the old cell to keep, the input gate i how much new content g to write, the output gate o how much of the cell to expose. The key line is additive: ct = f ⊙ ct−1 + i ⊙ g. A GRU (Cho et al., 2014) merges cell and hidden state and uses two gates; it is a little smaller and usually performs about the same.

class LSTMCell(nn.Module):
    def __init__(s, d, h): super().__init__(); s.W = nn.Linear(d, 4 * h); s.U = nn.Linear(h, 4 * h, bias=False)
    def forward(s, x, hc):
        h, c = hc; i, f, g, o = (s.W(x) + s.U(h)).chunk(4, -1)            # PyTorch's gate order
        c = torch.sigmoid(f) * c + torch.sigmoid(i) * torch.tanh(g)      # keep some old, write some new
        h = torch.sigmoid(o) * torch.tanh(c); return h, c

Backpropagation through time

Training an RNN means unrolling it over the sequence into a deep network with shared weights and back-propagating through every step: backpropagation through time (BPTT). For long texts, such as a language model reading a whole corpus, the unrolled graph is cut every k steps (truncated BPTT): the hidden state is carried forward across the cut, but gradients are not, so the model can use information from further back than k but cannot learn to.

Vanishing gradients

The gradient reaching step t from the end is a product of T − t Jacobians, ∂hk/∂hk−1 = diag(tanh′) · U. If their typical size is below 1, the product shrinks exponentially; above 1, it explodes. In a vanilla RNN, tanh′ is at most 1 and usually much less, so early tokens get almost no gradient and the network never learns to use them. In an LSTM, the path through the cell is ct = f ⊙ ct−1 + …, whose derivative is just f: if the network learns f ≈ 1, gradient flows back almost unchanged. Gradient clipping handles the exploding case; gating handles the vanishing one. Try both in the lab.

Measured at initialisation, the gradient reaching an input 50 steps back was 10-13.1 of the last step’s for the vanilla RNN, 10-10.7 for the GRU and 10-9.9 for an LSTM with PyTorch’s default initialisation: per step they shrink by factors of about 0.56, 0.62 and 0.64. Gating alone does not keep the gradient alive, because at initialisation the forget gate sits near σ(0) = 0.5. Setting the forget-gate bias to 1 (Gers et al., 2000; Jozefowicz et al., 2015), so the gate starts near 0.73, changed the factor to 0.86 and the gradient 50 steps back to 10-3.6, about a million times larger.

Measured: remembering the first token

The task: read L random symbols out of 10 and output the first one. Only the last output is trained, so the whole signal has to travel back L steps. After 6,000 training steps:

This is not the textbook picture, and it is worth reading carefully. At L = 20 the LSTM with forget bias 1 was perfect by the first check at 1000 steps and the GRU by 2000 steps, while the LSTM at default initialisation stayed at chance for all 6,000 steps, below the vanilla RNN, which hovered near 47.4%. So the gates make long memory possible; the initialisation decides whether training finds it. Beyond 20 steps nothing learnt the task in this budget, the forget-biased LSTM included: with the only error signal at the end and a gradient already down to 10-3.6 at 50 steps, the first symbol’s contribution is too faint for the optimiser to pick out of the noise. Classic LSTM demonstrations on tasks like this trained far longer, or used curricula that grow L gradually. One more measurement points the same way: the forget-biased LSTM trained on L = 50, which failed, ended with a gradient 50 steps back of 10-10.1, back at the default LSTM’s level. Training on a task it could not solve taught it to forget.

Bidirectional RNNs and pooling

A left-to-right RNN’s state at word t knows only words 1 to t. A bidirectional RNN runs a second RNN right to left and concatenates the two states, so every position sees the whole sentence; it cannot be used to generate text, but for classification and tagging (Chapter 10) it is the standard choice. For classification there is also the question of what to feed the classifier: the final state(s), or the mean or maximum over all positions.

On SST-2’s short sentences every gated model landed between 84.52 and 85.09, closer together than the two seeds of a single model (the GRU’s runs were 84.75 and 85.44); the vanilla RNN was 1.0 points behind the LSTM. All of them beat Chapter 8’s best SST-2 model, a CNN on GloVe at 82.87, by about two points: on short, compositional sentences word order is worth something.

IMDB reviews are long, and the differences open up. The vanilla RNN fell to 80.31, 6.1 points behind the LSTM, and max pooling over a BiLSTM’s states was best at 88.5: the classifier no longer depends on one final state having kept everything from 400 tokens earlier. Mean pooling (86.86) did no better than the last states (86.98). Two caveats. First, the LSTM row is noisy: an earlier run with the same seeds, interrupted and restarted, gave 87.44 instead of 86.41, most likely because cuDNN’s recurrent kernels are not deterministic by default, so differences under about a point are not real. Second, none of these beat a bag of words on IMDB: TF-IDF + logistic regression reached 90.94 in Chapter 8, in seconds.

An LSTM language model

A two-layer LSTM with 650 units (28.4M parameters, input and output embeddings tied) trained for 12 epochs on WikiText-2 reached a test perplexity of 131.1 with truncated BPTT over 35 tokens, against 242.2 for Chapter 5’s Kneser–Ney trigram on exactly the same tokens and vocabulary. Cutting the BPTT window to 5 tokens raised it to 163.7: the state still flows across the cuts, but the model can no longer learn to use more than a few words of context. It also took 2.4× as long (1127 s against 477 s), because seven times as many, much smaller, sequential steps keep the GPU idle. The 35-token model was still improving at the end (validation 145.6 → 143.6 → 142.1 over the last three epochs), so longer training would go lower; AWD-LSTM (Merity et al., 2018), a heavily regularised three-layer LSTM with 33M parameters, reports about 66 on WikiText-2.

Use case: on-device keyword spotting and next-word prediction

Before transformers fit on phones, keyboards and voice assistants used small LSTMs: a one-layer LSTM with a few hundred units, quantised to 8 bits, predicts the next word or detects a wake word in a few milliseconds and a few megabytes. Its constant memory per step is the attraction: it processes an unbounded stream without a growing cache. The engineering lessons are this chapter’s: clip gradients when training, use truncated BPTT with a window matched to how far back the signal lives, and measure against the n-gram model it replaces, because a well-smoothed n-gram is a stubborn baseline when the training text is small.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Leaving the forget-gate bias at zero: at PyTorch’s default initialisation the LSTM’s gradient 50 steps back was 10-9.9 of the last step’s; with the bias at 1 it was 10-3.6.
  • Expecting an LSTM to beat a bag of words on long documents: the best recurrent model here reached 88.5 on IMDB; TF-IDF reached 90.94 (Ch 8).
  • Taking the “last state” of a padded batch from the last position: it is a pad; pack the sequences or index each row’s true length.
  • For a bidirectional RNN, using the backward direction’s state at the end: the backward RNN’s summary of the whole sentence is at position 0.
  • Training without gradient clipping: a single exploding batch can wipe out the model.
  • Reading a one-point difference between runs as a result: the same LSTM, same seeds, gave 87.44 and 86.41 on IMDB; cuDNN’s RNN kernels are not deterministic by default.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is the vanishing gradient problem, and how does an LSTM address it?

How a strong answer sounds
Training back-propagates through every time step, and the gradient reaching a token k steps back is a product of k Jacobians; if they are smaller than 1 it shrinks exponentially, so early tokens get no learning signal. An LSTM adds a cell state updated additively, c = f·c + i·g, whose derivative along the cell path is the forget gate f; if f stays near 1, the gradient passes back almost unchanged. The caveat I would add from measuring it: at default initialisation the forget gate sits near 0.5, and an LSTM’s gradient 50 steps back was 10-9.9 of the last step’s, little better than a plain RNN’s 10-13.1. Setting the forget-gate bias to 1 raised it to 10-3.6, and on a remember-the-first-symbol task at 20 steps it was the difference between 100% and chance. Exploding gradients are the other half of the problem, and are handled by clipping.

Deeper: Why did transformers replace LSTMs for most NLP, and where do RNNs still make sense?

How a strong answer sounds
Two reasons, and accuracy is the smaller one. An RNN computes step t from step t − 1, so a sequence of n tokens takes n sequential steps however big the GPU; a transformer processes every position at once, which made pre-training on billions of tokens practical. And attention connects any two positions directly, instead of through n multiplications that shrink the gradient. Here the LSTM language model took 477 s for 12 epochs of a 2M-token corpus, and cutting the BPTT window made it 2.4× slower, which is the sequential bottleneck in miniature. RNNs still make sense where memory per step must stay constant: streaming audio or keyboard models on devices, very long streams where a transformer’s cache keeps growing, and as the inspiration for linear-recurrent models such as state-space models, which train in parallel and run like an RNN.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • An RNN carries a hidden state token by token with shared weights; training unrolls it (BPTT), usually truncated.
  • Measured: at default initialisation the gradient 50 steps back was 10-13.1 (RNN) and 10-9.9 (LSTM); a forget-gate bias of 1 raised the LSTM’s to 10-3.6 and turned a 20-step memory task from chance into 100.0%.
  • Measured sentiment: gated models all within 1.5 points on SST-2 (83.55–85.09); on IMDB the vanilla RNN fell to 80.31 and BiLSTM max pooling led at 88.5, still below TF-IDF’s 90.94.
  • Measured language model: LSTM test perplexity 131.1 against Kneser–Ney’s 242.2; truncating BPTT to 5 tokens cost 32.6 points of perplexity.
  • Sequential computation makes RNNs slow to train on GPUs; that, more than accuracy, is what transformers fixed.
