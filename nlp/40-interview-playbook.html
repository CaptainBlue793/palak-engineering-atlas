The NLP Interview Playbook

NLP interviews mix four kinds of question: explain a concept, do some arithmetic on a model or a metric, debug a failing pipeline, and design a system. Each has a recognisable shape, and each rewards the habits this course has drilled: say what the tokeniser does to the input, count before guessing, and know what was actually measured.

This chapter collects the formulas to produce on a whiteboard, gives you calculators to check yourself, and lists the concept, debugging and design prompts that come up most, with answers built from the numbers measured in the earlier chapters. Use it with the timed mock interview room.

  • Answer concept questions with a mechanism, a number and a limit
  • Compute model size, attention cost, F1, perplexity and token cost quickly
  • Work through a debugging scenario in a disciplined order
  • Structure a design round from requirements to evaluation, serving and monitoring
  • Talk about trade-offs with numbers rather than preferences

# softmax cross-entropy and perplexity (P4, Ch 5)

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(Q·Kᵀ / √d_k) · V      # scores: n × n per head → cost ∝ n²·d

Debugging scenarios

The interviewer wants an order of operations, not a guess. A reliable order: reproduce (same texts, same code path), inspect the inputs (decode the token IDs the model actually receives, check lengths against max_length, casing, encoding), check the batch path (attention mask, padding side, batch 1 vs batch 64), check the data (label map, leakage, duplicates, label noise, drift), and only then the model.

Design rounds

Most NLP design prompts (search, classification and routing, moderation, extraction, summarisation, a RAG assistant) fit one structure. Spend the first minutes on requirements, because they decide everything else: what is the output, which error matters more, what volume and latency, which languages, how many labels exist.

Talking about trade-offs

Trade-off questions (“BERT or TF-IDF?”, “beam search or greedy?”, “LLM or fine-tuned model?”) are asking whether you can put a price on each side. The pattern that works: name the axis, give the number on each side, then say which side the product needs. A few measured in this course:

  • Accuracy against latency and cost. On SST-2 a fine-tuned DistilBERT scored 91.06; qwen3:14b zero-shot 85.78, 89.33 with a prompt that named the answers, at about 1,600 times DistilBERT’s time per review (Ch 1). TF-IDF + logistic regression trained in under a second and scored 82.11 (Ch 8).
  • Quality against decoding time. Beam search added under a point of BLEU (22.36 → 23.17) and took 14 s per 1,000 sentences one at a time; adding attention added 12.7 (Ch 12–13). Improve the model before the search.
  • Structure against training time. A CRF layer lifted NER span F1 from 84.07 to 86.26 and removed almost all invalid tag sequences, for about four times the training time (Ch 11).
  • Labels against pre-training. Pre-training substitutes for labels when labels are few: +2.8 points at 2,000 SST-2 examples, nothing at 67,349 for a small model (Ch 16).
  • Metric against meaning. BLEU could not tell a reversed meaning from a synonym (Ch 14); accuracy hid a 9:1 class imbalance until recall was measured (Ch 4). Choose the metric before the model.

When the interviewer pushes back (“but the LLM is more accurate”), agree with the number and return to the requirement: at a million texts a day, a 1,600× cost difference decides the design unless the accuracy gap costs more.

Use case: a worked design answer in outline

“Route customer emails to 15 teams, 200,000 a day, in English and Spanish, with no labels.” Requirements: macro-F1 matters because small teams must not be starved; under a second per email; two languages. Data: run an LLM over 20,000 historical emails to propose labels, have agents correct 2,000 of them, hold out 1,000 human-labelled emails per language as the test set, scrub PII before anything reaches annotators (Ch 36, Ch 38). Baseline: TF-IDF + logistic regression on the corrected labels, measured (Ch 4). Model: a multilingual encoder such as XLM-R fine-tuned on both languages; check Spanish separately, since transfer is uneven (Ch 24). Evaluation: macro-F1 and per-team recall on the held-out set, CheckList tests for negation and forwarded threads (Ch 30). Serving: ONNX int8 on CPU, dynamic padding, truncation measured on real email lengths (Ch 34, Ch 37). Monitoring: confidence, unseen-word rate and per-team volume each week, 200 emails relabelled weekly, retrain when they show a drop (Ch 38). Every choice comes with a number to check.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Starting with the model: state the metric and the error that matters first.
  • No baseline: TF-IDF or BM25 takes minutes and is sometimes the answer (Ch 8, Ch 26).
  • Quoting benchmark numbers as if they transfer: say what you would measure on the real data.
  • Ignoring the tokeniser: truncation, casing and language all start there.
  • Forgetting cost: convert throughput into pounds per million texts before choosing an LLM (Ch 36).

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Walk me through how BERT classifies a sentence, from raw text to a label.

How a strong answer sounds
Raw text first goes through BERT’s WordPiece tokeniser: lower-casing for the uncased model, splitting into pieces from a 30,522 vocabulary (“unbelievable” → “un ##bel ##ie ##vable”), adding [CLS] at the start and [SEP] at the end, truncating to the model’s 512-token limit, and padding the batch with an attention mask of 1 for real tokens. Each piece becomes a 768-dimensional vector: its token embedding plus a position embedding (and a segment embedding). Twelve encoder layers then mix the vectors: in each, multi-head self-attention lets every token read every other token, and a feed-forward network transforms each one, with residual connections and layer norm. The final vector at [CLS] goes through a linear layer to one score per class, and a softmax turns the scores into probabilities. Fine-tuning trains all 110M weights plus that head with cross-entropy for a few epochs at a learning rate around 2·10−5. A fine-tuned DistilBERT, a 6-layer distilled version, reached 91% on SST-2 in this course. The things I would check in practice: the tokeniser matches the model, the attention mask is passed, and inputs longer than 512 tokens are handled deliberately rather than silently truncated.

Deeper: Design a system that flags abusive comments on a news site, end to end.

How a strong answer sounds
Requirements first: what counts as abusive (a written policy), which error costs more (wrongly removed comments silence users, missed abuse harms them), volume and latency (comments appear within a second), languages, and whether a human review queue exists. Data: sample real comments, label them against the policy with several annotators and measure agreement, because offensiveness is subjective (P7 measured kappa from 0.80 down to 0.21 across categories); build a test set that over-samples identity terms, quoted abuse and negation. Baseline: TF-IDF + logistic regression, measured. Model: a fine-tuned encoder. Thresholds matter as much as the model: on tweet_eval offensive the TF-IDF baseline’s precision and recall at 0.5 were 68.4 and 49.6, and its best F1 came at 0.35 (P7), so the threshold is a product decision, ideally three bands: publish, hold for review, remove. Evaluation: precision and recall at those thresholds, false-positive rates per identity group, and CheckList tests for negation (“not an idiot at all”) and quotation. Serving: a quantised encoder on CPU with dynamic padding. Monitoring: appeals and moderator overrides become labelled data, weekly drift checks on confidence and vocabulary, and a re-run of the identity and behavioural tests before every model release.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Concept answers: mechanism, a measured number, then the limit.
  • Arithmetic: parameters ≈ V·d + 12·L·d²; attention cost grows with n²; perplexity = exp(cross-entropy).
  • Debugging: inputs and token IDs first, then the batch path, then the data, then the model.
  • Design: requirements and the metric first, a baseline always, and numbers at every step.
  • Trade-offs are argued with cost, latency and accuracy measured on the real data.

Check your understanding

Roughly how many parameters does a 12-layer encoder with d = 768 and a 30,522-token vocabulary have?

30,522 × 768 ≈ 23M for embeddings, plus 12 × 12 × 768² ≈ 85M for the layers: BERT-base.

A language model assigns every word a probability of 1/1,000. What is its perplexity?

Perplexity is exp of the mean negative log-probability: exp(ln 1000) = 1000, the effective number of choices.

In a debugging question, what should you check before touching the model?

Tokeniser mismatch, truncation and casing all show up there (Ch 39).

Doubling the input length of a full-attention encoder multiplies the attention cost by about…

The score matrix is n × n; the feed-forward layers only double (Ch 34).
