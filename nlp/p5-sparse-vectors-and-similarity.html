Sparse Vectors & Similarity

Search engines, deduplication, recommendation and the first stage of every retrieval-augmented LLM answer the same question: which documents are most like this one? The classic answer turns each document into a vector with one dimension per vocabulary word, then compares vectors with a dot product. Almost every entry of those vectors is zero, and that one fact decides how they are stored and computed.

This prerequisite builds that matrix for 25,000 IMDB reviews. It is 99.7% zeros: 39 MB stored sparsely, 4.3 GB dense. Cosine similarity finds reviews of the same film. The same search on unnormalised counts sends more than half of all queries to the single longest review in the collection.

  • Turn documents into count and TF-IDF vectors
  • Compute dot products and cosine similarity, and know when they differ
  • Store a sparse matrix in CSR form and estimate its memory
  • Find nearest neighbours with one matrix product
  • Choose between sparse and dense computation on CPU and GPU

Vectors from text

Fix a vocabulary of V words. A document becomes a vector of length V whose entry for each word is the number of times the word appears: a bag of words, because order is thrown away. A better weighting, TF-IDF, multiplies each count by how rare the word is across documents, so the counts for almost nothing and Witherspoon for a lot; Chapter 3 covers it properly. Here the vocabulary is every word that appears in at least two of the 25,000 reviews: 44,796 words.

from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer
v = TfidfVectorizer(min_df=2, sublinear_tf=True)        # tf = 1 + log(count); rows scaled to length 1
X = v.fit_transform(texts)                               # scipy.sparse CSR, shape (25000, 44796)
X.nnz / X.shape[0]                                       # 136.0 non-zero entries per review, out of 44,796

Dot product and cosine

The dot product of two count vectors adds up, over the words they share, the product of their counts. It grows with overlap, which is what we want, and also with length, which is not: repeat a document ten times and its dot product with everything grows tenfold, though its content has not changed. Cosine similarity divides by both lengths, cos(a, b) = a·b / (‖a‖‖b‖), so it measures only the angle between the vectors: 1 for the same direction, 0 for no shared words. If every row is first scaled to length 1, as scikit-learn’s TF-IDF does by default, the dot product is the cosine, and a whole similarity search is one matrix product.

Sparse storage: CSR

The TF-IDF matrix for 25,000 reviews has 25,000 × 44,796 = 1.12 billion entries, of which 3,401,071 are non-zero: 0.30%. Stored densely as 32-bit floats it needs 4,272 MB. Stored as compressed sparse row (CSR), it needs 39 MB, because CSR keeps only the non-zeros in three arrays:

# the 3 x 5 matrix  [[0, 2, 0, 0, 1],
#                    [0, 0, 0, 0, 0],
#                    [3, 0, 0, 4, 0]]
data    = [2, 1, 3, 4]        # the non-zero values, row by row
indices = [1, 4, 0, 3]        # the column of each value
indptr  = [0, 2, 2, 4]        # row i is data[indptr[i]:indptr[i+1]]; row 1 is empty

Reading a row is a slice, so CSR is fast for “give me document i” and for multiplying the matrix by something on the right. Building a matrix entry by entry is slow in CSR; build it in COO (coordinate) form or let the vectoriser do it, then convert. The cost per document is about 12 bytes per non-zero (an 8-byte value and a 4-byte column index), here about 1.6 KB per review, and it does not depend on the vocabulary size.

Nearest neighbours, with and without normalisation

Take one review, “Reese Witherspooon’s first movie. Loved it. The plot and the acting was top notch…”, and rank all others by cosine. Its heaviest TF-IDF terms are not sentiment words but rare ones: reese (0.405), witherspoon (0.237), flows, viewings, notch, moon. The three nearest reviews (cosine 0.321, 0.317, 0.299) are all about The Man in the Moon, her first film and the subject of the query: one names its characters, one its co-star Jason London, one its title. Cosine on TF-IDF finds the same topic. Whether it finds the same opinion is a separate question: using each of the first 2,000 reviews as a query, the nearest neighbour had the same positive or negative label 81.0% of the time, against 50% by chance.

Nearest neighbour of each of 2,000 reviewsSame label as the queryDistinct neighboursMedian length of the neighbour
Cosine on TF-IDF (rows normalised)81.0%1,792177 words (collection median 165)
Dot product on raw counts49.2% (chance)62,335 words

Without normalisation the search collapses. The 2,000 queries found only 6 distinct “nearest” reviews, and 52.6% of them chose the same one: the longest review in the collection, 2,335 words, which shares some words with everything. Label agreement fell to chance. Nothing errors, every query returns a confident answer, and every answer is close to useless. This is the same failure that later appears with dense embeddings, whose dot products also favour long vectors unless the model or the index normalises them.

Sparse or dense, CPU or GPU

Scoring 1,000 queries against all 25,000 reviews is a (1,000 × 44,796) by (44,796 × 25,000) product, 25 million scores. Timed four ways:

1,000 queries × 25,000 reviewsMedian timeMemory for the documents
Sparse CSR on CPU (scipy)663 ms39 MB
Sparse CSR on GPU (torch)61.7 msabout 40 MB
Dense float32 on GPU81.5 ms, plus 1.0 s to build and copy it once4,272 MB
Dense float64 on GPU3,281 ms8,544 MB

Sparse on the GPU was fastest and a hundred times smaller than dense. Dense float32 was close in speed, because a GPU does dense arithmetic so quickly that multiplying zeros barely matters at this size; the cost is memory, and at 10 million documents the dense matrix would be 1.8 TB. The last row is a trap this experiment fell into in its first version: scikit-learn and scipy produce float64 by default, torch.tensor(X.toarray()) silently keeps float64, and consumer GPUs have few 64-bit units. The same product was 40 times slower. Cast to float32 before moving text features to a GPU.

🧾
Use case: duplicate bug reports

A team gets 2,000 bug reports a week and wants each new one shown with its five most similar past reports, so duplicates are closed early. TF-IDF over title and body, rows normalised, sparse product against the archive, top 5: that is the whole first version, and it runs in milliseconds. Their first attempt used raw counts and suggested the same three enormous crash-log reports for almost every new bug, the collapse measured above. The fix was one argument. A year later the archive is 100,000 reports, still about 160 MB in CSR, and they add a dense sentence-embedding model (Chapter 20) to catch duplicates that use different words, keeping TF-IDF for exact error messages and IDs, where it is still better.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Ranking by unnormalised dot products: 2,000 queries collapsed onto 6 reviews, half onto the longest one, and label agreement fell from 81% to chance.
  • Calling .toarray() on a large sparse matrix: 39 MB becomes 4.3 GB here, and terabytes at real scale.
  • Moving float64 features to a GPU: 40× slower than float32 on a consumer card.
  • Building a CSR matrix one entry at a time: build COO or use the vectoriser, then convert.
  • Assuming cosine on TF-IDF measures meaning or sentiment: it measures shared rare words, and found the same film, not the same opinion.
  • Comparing vectors from two different vectorisers: column 1,000 means a different word in each.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Why use cosine similarity rather than the dot product to compare documents?

How a strong answer sounds
The dot product grows with how long the documents are, not only with how much they share, so long documents look similar to everything. Cosine divides by both lengths and measures only the angle. I measured it on 25,000 IMDB reviews: with raw dot products, 2,000 queries found only 6 distinct nearest neighbours, more than half the longest review, and the neighbour’s label matched only 49% of the time. With cosine on TF-IDF, 1,792 distinct neighbours and 81% agreement. If rows are normalised to length 1 first, dot product and cosine are the same thing, and that is what most libraries do.

Deeper: You need near-duplicate detection over 10 million support tickets with TF-IDF. How do you store and compute it?

How a strong answer sounds
Keep it sparse. At around 136 non-zeros per document and 12 bytes each, that is about 1.6 KB per ticket, 16 GB for 10 million, or 11 GB with float32 values; dense float32 with a 45,000-word vocabulary would be 1.8 TB. Normalise rows so the dot product is cosine. Compute similarities in blocks of queries, sparse times sparse-transposed, keep only the top k per row, and never materialise the full similarity matrix. On a GPU, use sparse kernels in float32. For pure duplicates at that scale, MinHash with locality-sensitive hashing avoids comparing all pairs; for paraphrases, add dense embeddings with an approximate nearest-neighbour index.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Bag-of-words and TF-IDF vectors have one dimension per vocabulary word and are almost all zeros: 0.30% non-zero here.
  • CSR stores only the non-zeros: 39 MB against 4,272 MB dense, about 12 bytes per non-zero.
  • Cosine is the dot product of length-normalised vectors; without normalisation, 2,000 queries collapsed onto 6 documents and accuracy fell to chance.
  • Cosine on TF-IDF finds shared rare words, so it finds the same topic, which matches sentiment only some of the time (81% here).
  • Sparse on GPU was fastest (62 ms); dense float32 was close but 100× larger; float64 on a consumer GPU was 40× slower.
