Momentum, RMSProp & Adam

Plain SGD has two problems Chapter 8 measured: in a ravine, the steep direction caps the learning rate and the flat direction crawls; and near a saddle point, where the gradient is almost zero, it barely moves at all. The optimisers in every training script today fix these with two ideas. Momentum keeps a running average of past gradients, so consistent directions build speed and oscillating ones cancel. Adaptive methods divide each parameter’s step by a running estimate of its own gradient size, so every direction moves at a similar rate. Adam combines both.

This chapter writes six optimisers in a few lines each and races them. On the Rosenbrock function, a curved ravine, 2,000 steps of SGD stopped at a loss of 0.20 while momentum reached 10⁻⁸ and Adam 2 × 10⁻⁵. Started next to a saddle point, SGD took 90 steps to escape and AdaGrad 2. On a Fashion-MNIST MLP, with each optimiser’s learning rate tuned, Adam reached 88.5% and plain SGD 86.7%. On a CIFAR-10 CNN with augmentation and a one-cycle schedule, tuned SGD with Nesterov momentum and tuned AdamW finished within half a point (89.5% and 89.9%). Our hand-written Adam matched torch.optim.Adam to within 2 × 10⁻⁷ after 200 steps.

Momentum and Nesterov

Momentum keeps a velocity: m ← βm + g, and steps w ← w − lr·m. With β = 0.9 the velocity is roughly a sum of the last ten gradients. In a ravine, the components that flip sign each step (across the ravine) cancel in the sum, and the component along the floor, which points the same way every step, adds up to about ten times its size. That is exactly the cure for Chapter 8’s zig-zag. Nesterov momentum evaluates the correction at the point the velocity is about to carry the weights to, which damps overshooting a little.

class Opt:                                               # one class, six update rules
    def step(self):
        self.t += 1
        for w, m, v in zip(self.params, self.m, self.v):   # m, v: one buffer per parameter, same shape
            g = w.grad
            if self.kind == "SGD":      w -= lr * g
            if self.kind == "momentum": m.mul_(b1).add_(g); w -= lr * m
            if self.kind == "Nesterov": m.mul_(b1).add_(g); w -= lr * (g + b1 * m)
            if self.kind == "AdaGrad":  v.add_(g * g); w -= lr * g / (v.sqrt() + 1e-10)
            if self.kind == "RMSProp":  v.mul_(b2).add_((1 - b2) * g * g); w -= lr * g / (v.sqrt() + eps)
            if self.kind == "Adam":
                m.mul_(b1).add_((1 - b1) * g); v.mul_(b2).add_((1 - b2) * g * g)
                m_hat = m / (1 - b1 ** self.t); v_hat = v / (1 - b2 ** self.t)   # bias correction
                w -= lr * m_hat / (v_hat.sqrt() + eps)

AdaGrad and RMSProp

Adaptive methods keep a second buffer v of squared gradients per parameter and divide each step by √v. A parameter whose gradients are large takes small steps; one whose gradients are tiny takes large ones. The update’s size becomes roughly lr in every direction, whatever the curvature or the gradient’s scale, which is what fixes a ravine from the other side and what pushes a parameter off a near-flat saddle. AdaGrad sums every squared gradient ever seen, so its steps only shrink, eventually to nothing: good for sparse features, bad for long training. RMSProp uses an exponential moving average instead (β₂ = 0.9 to 0.99), so the scale tracks recent gradients and steps do not die out.

Adam and bias correction

Adam (Kingma and Ba, 2015) keeps both: an average of gradients m (momentum, β₁ = 0.9) and an average of squared gradients v (RMSProp, β₂ = 0.999), and steps by m̂ / √v̂. Both buffers start at zero, so in the first steps they are biased towards zero: after one step, m = 0.1g and v = 0.001g². Dividing by 1 − βᵗ undoes that, so step 1 is m̂ / √v̂ = g / |g|, a full-sized step in the sign of the gradient. Without correction the first update would be 0.1g / √(0.001g²) ≈ 3.2 times too large, and during the first few hundred steps v would be underestimated more than m, giving early steps far larger than intended. Checked against the library: after 200 steps on the same batches, our Adam and torch.optim.Adam gave weights within 2 × 10⁻⁷ of each other, which is float32 rounding.

Saddle points and ravines

In high dimensions most points where the gradient vanishes are not minima but saddle points: downhill in some directions, uphill in others. Plain gradient descent started almost exactly on a saddle has a tiny gradient in the escape direction and takes many steps to notice it. On f = x² − y² + y⁴/4, started at y = 10⁻⁴:

Momentum won the ravine: the velocity built up along the curved floor and rode it to (1, 1). AdaGrad won the saddle: dividing the tiny y-gradient by its own size turned it into a full-sized step, and it was off the saddle in two. It then lost the ravine because its accumulated v kept growing and its steps shrank towards nothing. Adam did well on both without being the best at either, which is its appeal: a robust default. On the real network every method with momentum or adaptive scaling beat plain SGD by 1.2 to 1.8 points over five epochs, and the four best were within half a point of one another, close to seed noise (P8).

When SGD still wins

A widely repeated result is that adaptive methods generalise worse than well-tuned SGD with momentum on image classification (Wilson et al., 2017). On a CIFAR-10 CNN with augmentation, batch norm and a one-cycle schedule, with five learning rates tried for each, this did not show up: the best SGD with Nesterov momentum (lr 0.4) reached 89.47% and the best AdamW (lr 0.01) 89.89%, a gap within single-seed noise. Two details matter. The comparison used AdamW, whose weight decay is applied directly to the weights rather than through the gradient, and much of the historical gap came from Adam with L2 regularisation, which Chapter 13 measures. And both curves were fairly flat near their best rate: a poorly tuned run of either loses more than the choice between them. SGD with momentum still has real advantages, half the optimiser memory (one buffer, not two) and a long record on vision models, but “Adam generalises worse” is not a safe default assumption.

Use case: the optimiser state that did not fit

A team fine-tunes a 7-billion-parameter model on one 80 GB GPU. The bf16 weights take 14 GB and the gradients another 14 GB, but AdamW’s two fp32 buffers per parameter take 56 GB more, and the run does not fit. Options all trade against this chapter’s results: SGD with momentum halves the state but needs far more learning-rate tuning; 8-bit Adam quantises the two buffers to a quarter of the memory; parameter-efficient fine-tuning trains only a small adapter so the optimiser state is tiny (LLMs, LoRA). They choose LoRA, and the optimiser state drops to under 1 GB.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Comparing optimisers at one shared learning rate: their best rates here spanned 3e-4 to 0.4; tune each before comparing.
  • Skipping bias correction in a hand-written Adam: early steps come out too large.
  • AdaGrad for long training: its steps shrink forever (loss 0.137 on the ravine after 2,000 steps).
  • Assuming SGD generalises better than Adam: tuned AdamW matched it here; Adam with L2 is the case that loses (Chapter 13).
  • Forgetting the optimiser’s memory: Adam stores two extra buffers per parameter.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What does Adam do, and why does it need bias correction?

How a strong answer sounds
Adam keeps two exponential moving averages per parameter: of the gradient, which acts as momentum, and of the squared gradient, which estimates the gradient’s scale. It steps by the first divided by the square root of the second, so every parameter moves at roughly the learning rate in the direction its recent gradients agree on. Both averages start at zero, so early on they underestimate the true values, by a factor of 1 − βᵗ; dividing by that factor corrects it. Without correction the very first steps are the wrong size, mostly too large, because the second average, with β₂ = 0.999, is biased far more than the first. My from-scratch version matched PyTorch’s Adam to 2 × 10⁻⁷ after 200 steps.

Deeper: How would you compare SGD with momentum and AdamW fairly for a new model?

How a strong answer sounds
Tune each one’s learning rate separately on a log grid, wide enough that the best value is inside the grid, with the same schedule shape, weight decay set appropriately for each (they are not on the same scale), the same number of steps, and several seeds. Compare validation, not training, accuracy, and check the curves are flat near the optimum. In a CIFAR-10 CNN comparison I first found both optima at the edge of my grid and had to extend it; after that SGD with Nesterov reached 89.5% and AdamW 89.9%, inside seed noise. I would then choose on secondary grounds: memory, tuning effort and what the architecture is usually trained with.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Momentum averages gradients: consistent directions speed up, oscillations cancel (ravine loss 10⁻⁸ against SGD’s 0.20).
  • Adaptive methods divide by each parameter’s gradient scale; AdaGrad escaped a saddle in 2 steps, SGD in 90.
  • Adam = momentum + RMSProp + bias correction; it is a robust default (88.5% on the MLP, best of six).
  • Compare optimisers only after tuning each one’s learning rate; their best rates differed a thousandfold.
  • Tuned SGD with momentum and AdamW finished within half a point on a CIFAR-10 CNN.
