Deep Reinforcement Learning: DQN & Policy Gradients

In supervised learning every example comes with the right answer. In reinforcement learning an agent acts, the world changes, and the only feedback is a reward that may arrive many steps after the actions that earned it. The agent’s own behaviour decides what data it sees, so the training distribution moves as it learns. Deep RL uses neural networks to represent what the agent learns, either the value of each action (Q-learning) or the policy itself (policy gradients), and inherits every instability of both.

This chapter writes the classic cart-pole task out from its equations and trains both families on it, 3 seeds each, on a CPU. A Deep Q-Network balanced the pole for an average of 185 to 428 steps out of 500 depending only on the seed. Without its target network its Q-value estimates grew to 2.7 × 10⁸ and it never learned at all. Policy gradients with a simple mean baseline reached 419 to 452. Measuring the gradient estimates directly showed why baselines matter: a learned value baseline nearly doubled their signal-to-noise ratio. And a well-known fix did nothing here: DQN, which is famous for overestimating values, underestimated them by about 20%.

  • Implement DQN with experience replay and a target network, and measure what each one does
  • Derive and implement REINFORCE, and reduce its variance with baselines
  • Build an actor-critic with generalised advantage estimation

Agents, rewards and returns

At each step t the agent sees a state st, picks an action at, and receives a reward rt and the next state. The goal is to maximise the return, the discounted sum of future rewards Gt = rt + γ rt+1 + γ² rt+2 + …, where the discount γ (0.99 here) makes rewards far in the future count for less. Cart-pole (Barto, Sutton and Anderson, 1983): a pole hinged on a cart; the state is the cart’s position and velocity and the pole’s angle and angular velocity; the two actions push the cart left or right; the reward is 1 for every step the pole stays within 12° of upright and the cart on the track, up to 500 steps. A random policy lasts about 20 steps.

# the dynamics, 0.02 s per step (the same constants as the standard CartPole-v1)
tmp = (force + 0.05 * th_dot**2 * sin(th)) / 1.1
th_acc = (9.8 * sin(th) - cos(th) * tmp) / (0.5 * (4/3 - 0.1 * cos(th)**2 / 1.1))
x_acc = tmp - 0.05 * th_acc * cos(th) / 1.1

An agent can learn either of two things. A value function Q(s, a) estimates the return of taking action a in state s and acting well afterwards; the policy is then “take the action with the highest Q”. Or a policy π(a | s) gives action probabilities directly and is improved by gradient ascent on the expected return.

DQN: Q-learning with a network

Q-learning rests on the Bellman equation: the value of an action is its reward plus the discounted value of the best action in the next state. A Deep Q-Network (Mnih et al., 2015) trains a network so that Q(s, a) ≈ r + γ maxa′ Q(s′, a′), treating the right-hand side as a regression target. Two additions made it work. Experience replay stores past transitions and trains on random samples of them, rather than on the last few steps, which are highly correlated. A target network, a copy of the Q-network updated only every 500 steps, computes the targets, so the network is not chasing a target that moves every time it updates. Measured, 60,000 environment steps each:

The target network was the difference between learning and not learning at all. Without it, every update changed the very values used to build the next targets, the estimates fed on themselves, and by the end of training the Q-values were in the hundreds of millions for a task whose true discounted return can never exceed 100. Replay mattered less than its reputation on this small problem: training on the last 128 consecutive transitions gave returns in the same wide range. Double DQN (van Hasselt et al., 2016) selects the next action with the online network and evaluates it with the target network, to remove the upward bias of taking a max over noisy estimates. Here there was little upward bias to remove: the variants that learned predicted 71 to 76 for start states whose actual discounted return was mostly 80 to 99 (one run, 67). The textbook failure, overestimation, depends on the task, the network and the training length; measure it before fixing it.

Policy gradients and REINFORCE

A policy network outputs action probabilities. The policy-gradient theorem says the gradient of the expected return is the expectation of Gt ∇ log π(at | st): increase the log-probability of each action in proportion to the return that followed it. REINFORCE (Williams, 1992) estimates that from sampled episodes, and it is just a weighted cross-entropy loss with the sampled actions as labels:

dist = torch.distributions.Categorical(logits=policy(states))
loss = -(dist.log_prob(actions) * advantages).mean()   # advantages = returns-to-go, minus a baseline
loss.backward(); opt.step()

The estimate is unbiased but very noisy, because a return depends on everything that happened in the episode. Subtracting a baseline b(s) from the return leaves the expected gradient unchanged and can reduce its variance a great deal. Three choices, measured:

A baseline made the gradient cleaner, as theory says: a learned value baseline nearly doubled the signal-to-noise ratio of plain REINFORCE, and even the batch’s mean return gave a 40% improvement. In final return the simplest baseline won on this task, likely because its gradients were clean enough and it had no second network to train. The actor-critic did worst here, and its gradient was the noisiest, which needs explaining rather than hiding: generalised advantage estimation (Schulman et al., 2016) replaces most of each return with the critic’s own predictions, so its quality depends on the critic, and the critic measured here had seen only 30,000 steps. With a good critic, bootstrapping trades a little bias for much lower variance, which is why actor-critic methods such as PPO dominate on harder tasks; with a poor one it adds noise.

Why RL is unstable

Every number in this chapter came with a wide spread across seeds. Three DQN runs with identical settings ended at 185, 283 and 428; Double DQN at 261, 275 and 497. Supervised learning on a fixed dataset has nothing like this. The reasons compound. The agent generates its own data, so an early lucky or unlucky streak changes everything it sees afterwards. The targets are the network’s own predictions (in Q-learning) or depend on its own behaviour (in policy gradients), so errors feed back. And rewards are sparse in time, so credit for an action is assigned through long, noisy chains. Henderson et al. (2018) showed that published RL comparisons often reverse with a different set of seeds.

The practical consequences: run at least 3 to 5 seeds and report all of them or their spread, never the best; compare learning curves, not only final numbers; keep a target network or a similar stabiliser; clip gradients; and evaluate the greedy policy separately from the exploring one.

🏭
Use case: tuning a data-centre cooling controller

An engineering team trains an RL agent to set chiller and fan levels, with a reward that pays for lower energy use and penalises temperatures outside a safe band. Training on a simulator, their first DQN looks excellent on one seed and diverges on the next two; adding a target network and gradient clipping makes all five seeds learn, with returns spread over a 15% range. They report the median seed to management, not the best. Before deployment they run the learned policy in shadow mode next to the existing rule-based controller for a month, because the simulator’s mistakes are exactly what an RL agent learns to exploit; the policy is allowed to act only within limits the rule-based controller already respects.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Dropping the target network: Q-values reached 2.7 × 10⁸ and the agent never learned.
  • Reporting the best seed: identical DQN runs ended between 185 and 428.
  • REINFORCE without a baseline: the noisiest estimator but one; a learned baseline nearly doubled signal-to-noise.
  • Assuming a known failure is present: DQN here underestimated values, so Double DQN’s fix was not needed.
  • Trusting an actor-critic with a weak critic: its advantages are only as good as the value estimates.
  • Evaluating with exploration switched on: test the greedy policy separately.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What do experience replay and the target network do in DQN?

How a strong answer sounds
DQN regresses Q(s, a) towards r plus γ times the best Q in the next state. Experience replay stores past transitions and trains on random samples, which breaks the correlation between consecutive steps and reuses data. The target network is a slowly updated copy used to compute the targets, so the target does not move every time the network updates. In my measurement on cart-pole, removing the target network was catastrophic: the Q-values fed on themselves and reached 10⁸, and the agent never balanced the pole. Removing replay mattered much less on that small problem, which is a reminder that the importance of each piece depends on the task.

Deeper: Why do policy gradients need a baseline, and why does subtracting one not bias the gradient?

How a strong answer sounds
The REINFORCE gradient is the expectation of the return times the gradient of the log-probability of the action taken. Returns are noisy, so the estimate has high variance. Subtracting any baseline that depends only on the state leaves the expectation unchanged, because the expected gradient of a log-probability is zero: the probabilities sum to one. It can cut the variance a lot: I measured the signal-to-noise ratio of the gradient at 0.78 without a baseline, 1.11 with the mean return and 1.44 with a learned value function. An actor-critic goes further and bootstraps from the critic, which lowers variance if the critic is good; with a critic trained for only 30,000 steps it was actually noisier.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • RL learns from reward on data the agent generates itself; the return discounts future rewards by γ.
  • DQN’s target network was essential: without it Q-values diverged to 10⁸.
  • Policy gradients are weighted log-likelihood; baselines keep them unbiased and cut their variance.
  • Actor-critics trade variance for bias, and depend on the critic’s quality.
  • Results spread widely across seeds; report every seed and the curves, and measure before applying a fix.
