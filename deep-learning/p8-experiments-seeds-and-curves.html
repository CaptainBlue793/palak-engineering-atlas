Experiments: Seeds, Logging & Learning Curves

Deep learning is an experimental science: you change one thing, train, and look at a number. The trouble is that the number moves even when you change nothing. A different random seed gives different initial weights and a different order of batches, and the final accuracy shifts by a few tenths of a point, sometimes more. Most “improvements” claimed in notebooks and pull requests are differences smaller than that shift.

This prerequisite measures the shift and gives you the habits that keep you honest. Ten seeds of a 2 × 256 MLP on Fashion-MNIST ranged from 87.29% to 87.94%; ten seeds of a twice-as-wide 2 × 512 model ranged from 87.00% to 88.63%. Their means differed by 0.04 points, which is nothing, yet a comparison of one seed each named the wider model the winner 52% of the time and the narrower one 47%. Then it reads learning curves: the same plot of training and validation loss tells you whether a model is fine, overfitting, underfitting, or being trained with the wrong learning rate.

  • Seed every source of randomness, and know when that still does not give identical runs
  • Measure seed variance and compare two configurations with means and standard errors
  • Log the right things at the right frequency, and smooth a noisy loss
  • Diagnose a run from its training and validation curves
  • Keep the test set for the end

Seeds and determinism

A training run draws random numbers in at least four places: the initial weights, the order of examples in each epoch, dropout masks, and data augmentation. Seeding the generators makes those draws repeatable:

def seed_everything(s):
    random.seed(s); np.random.seed(s); torch.manual_seed(s)   # CPU and all GPUs
g = torch.Generator().manual_seed(s)                            # a dedicated generator for the shuffle order
loader = DataLoader(ds, batch_size=128, shuffle=True, generator=g)
# optional, slower: refuse nondeterministic GPU kernels
torch.use_deterministic_algorithms(True); torch.backends.cudnn.benchmark = False

Seeding is necessary but not always sufficient. Some GPU kernels add numbers in whatever order threads finish, and floating-point addition is not associative, so the last bits differ from run to run. Measured: training the MLP twice with seed 0 gave weights that were bit-for-bit identical. Training a small CNN twice with seed 0 for one pass over 20,000 images did not: the largest weight difference was 0.013, from nondeterministic convolution backward kernels. Those tiny differences grow as training goes on, so two “identical” CNN runs end at different accuracies. Chapter 38 measures the deterministic mode and its cost; here, the practical point is that seed variance is real even when you seed everything, and the only honest response is to measure it.

Seed variance and comparing two runs

Train the same configuration with different seeds and the test accuracy spreads out. Measured on Fashion-MNIST, 5 epochs, Adam:

Difference B − A0.04 points, standard error 0.16, t = 0.27: no evidence of a difference

If you had trained one seed of each and compared, you would have been answering a coin toss. Over all 100 pairings of an A seed with a B seed, B came out ahead 52 times and A 47 times (one tie). Pick B’s luckiest seed, 88.63%, against A’s seed 0, 87.66%, and you could report “doubling the width gains a point”. The rule this course follows: run several seeds (five is a reasonable minimum, ten when the effect is small), report the mean and the spread, and do not call a difference real unless it is clearly larger than its standard error, roughly √(sd²ₐ/n + sd²ᵦ/n). When both configurations can share seeds, compare them seed by seed (a paired comparison), which removes the part of the noise they have in common.

Logging metrics

Log enough to diagnose a run you are not watching, and no more than you will read. A minimal set: training loss every N steps, validation loss and the task metric every epoch, the learning rate (schedules change it), and the gradient norm, which is the earliest warning of divergence. Two cautions from P7 apply. Calling loss.item() forces the CPU to wait for the GPU, so accumulate on the GPU and read every 50 or 100 steps. And a training loss logged per step is noisy, because each mini-batch is a different sample: over steps 200 to 400 its standard deviation was 0.172 at batch 32 and 0.053 at batch 256, a ratio of 3.2, close to the √8 = 2.8 you expect when averaging 8 times as many examples. Plot an exponential moving average, ema = 0.95 · ema + 0.05 · loss, alongside the raw values; never judge a run on one step.

running = torch.zeros((), device="cuda")
for step, (xb, yb) in enumerate(loader):
    loss = F.cross_entropy(model(xb), yb); opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward()
    gnorm = torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), float("inf"))   # total norm, no clipping
    opt.step(); running += loss.detach()
    if step % 100 == 0:                                                       # one sync per 100 steps
        log(step=step, loss=(running / 100).item(), grad_norm=gnorm.item(), lr=opt.param_groups[0]["lr"])
        running.zero_()

Reading training and validation curves

Plot training loss and validation loss against epochs on the same axes. The two lines and the gap between them diagnose most runs:

The test set is not a third curve to watch. Every decision made by looking at a number, the epoch, the learning rate, the architecture, fits the result a little to the data that produced the number. Make those decisions on validation and look at test once. If you have tuned against the test set, it has become a second validation set and you no longer have an unbiased estimate.

Use case: the pull request that improved accuracy by 0.6 points

An engineer on a fraud-detection team opens a pull request that swaps the activation function and reports validation AUC up from 0.912 to 0.918. The reviewer asks for five seeds of each. The baseline’s five runs range from 0.909 to 0.919 and the new version’s from 0.910 to 0.920; the means differ by 0.001. The change is dropped, and the team adds a rule to its experiment template: every comparison table shows mean ± standard deviation over at least five seeds, and runs are paired by seed so the shared randomness cancels.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • One seed per configuration: measured, it named the winner 52% vs 47% for two models whose means differed by 0.04 points.
  • Reporting the best seed instead of the mean and spread.
  • Assuming a seeded run is reproducible on the GPU: the CNN’s weights differed by up to 0.013 after one epoch.
  • Judging a run on one step’s loss: per-step noise was 0.17 at batch 32. Smooth it.
  • Choosing the epoch or the hyperparameters on the test set.
  • Logging with .item() every step in a CPU-bound loop: each one is a synchronisation.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Your change improves validation accuracy from 87.6% to 88.1%. Is it an improvement?

How a strong answer sounds
I cannot tell from two numbers. The first thing I would ask is how much the metric moves between seeds with no change at all. On Fashion-MNIST I measured the spread of ten seeds of the same MLP at 87.3% to 87.9%, a standard deviation of about 0.3, so half a point is within reach of luck. I would run five to ten seeds of each version, ideally paired so seed k of the baseline and seed k of the change share initialisation and data order, and compare the means against their standard error. If the difference is several standard errors, it is real; if not, it is not shown. And I would only check the test set once, after deciding.

Deeper: Training loss keeps falling but validation loss started rising at epoch 9. What is happening and what do you do?

How a strong answer sounds
The model has started to fit things specific to the training set: it is overfitting. Validation accuracy can stay flat for a while even as validation loss rises, because the model is becoming over-confident on the examples it gets wrong. The immediate fix is early stopping: keep the checkpoint with the best validation loss. To push the best point further, add data or augmentation, regularise with weight decay or dropout, or use a smaller model. In a measured run on Fashion-MNIST, validation loss bottomed at 0.318 at epoch 9 and rose to 0.427 by epoch 20, while accuracy stayed near 89%.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Seed Python, NumPy, PyTorch and the loader’s generator; even so, some GPU kernels are nondeterministic (the CNN here).
  • Seed variance is the yardstick: 10 seeds spanned 0.65 points (A) and 1.6 points (B) on Fashion-MNIST.
  • Compare means over several seeds against their standard error; one seed each was a coin toss.
  • Log loss, validation metrics, learning rate and gradient norm; smooth per-step loss, which is noisier at small batch sizes.
  • Training and validation curves diagnose overfitting, underfitting and bad learning rates; choose on validation, report test once.
