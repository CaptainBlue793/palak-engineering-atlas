Mixed Precision Training

A modern GPU does 16-bit matrix arithmetic several times faster than 32-bit, and 16-bit tensors take half the memory and half the bandwidth. Mixed precision training takes that speed without paying for it in accuracy: the expensive operations run in 16 bits, the numerically delicate ones stay in 32, and the weights the optimiser updates stay in 32. Two 16-bit formats compete. bf16 has fp32’s range and little precision; fp16 has more precision and a range so narrow that gradients fall out of the bottom of it, which is what loss scaling exists to fix.

This chapter measures the whole recipe on an RTX 5070 Ti. bf16 autocast made a CIFAR-10 CNN 1.6 times faster and a matmul-heavy MLP 2.8 times faster, at the same accuracy. Gradients turned out to be far smaller than the textbook picture: in the CNN, the median gradient flowing back through the activations was 2 × 10⁻⁸, and 64% of those values were too small for fp16 to represent at all. The CNN shrugged that off; a small transformer language model trained in fp16 without a loss scaler did not, ending 0.8 bits per byte worse than with one. And one result nobody warns you about: the same CNN in fp32 ran over four times slower when its tensors were stored channels-last.

What autocast does

Inside a torch.autocast block, each operation is run in the precision PyTorch has listed for it. Matrix multiplies and convolutions, which are most of the arithmetic and gain the most from tensor cores, run in the 16-bit format. Operations that are sensitive to rounding or range, such as softmax, log, exponentials, normalisation layers, losses and large reductions, are run in fp32. Everything outside the block, including the weights, their gradients and the optimiser state, stays fp32: the weights are cast to 16 bits on the way into each matmul, and the gradients are cast back to fp32 on the way out.

# bf16: fp32's range, so no scaler is needed
with torch.autocast("cuda", dtype=torch.bfloat16):
    loss = loss_fn(model(x), y)
loss.backward(); opt.step(); opt.zero_grad()

# fp16: scale the loss up so small gradients survive, unscale before the step
scaler = torch.amp.GradScaler("cuda")
with torch.autocast("cuda", dtype=torch.float16):
    loss = loss_fn(model(x), y)
scaler.scale(loss).backward()
scaler.unscale_(opt); torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0)   # clip the real gradients, not the scaled ones
scaler.step(opt); scaler.update(); opt.zero_grad()

Why the weights stay fp32: an update is usually a tiny fraction of the weight it changes, and 16-bit formats cannot represent the sum. A bf16 weight of 1.0 given 1,000 updates of −0.0001 was still exactly 1.0 afterwards, because 0.9999 rounds back to 1.0 in bf16 every time; the fp32 copy reached 0.89998 (the absorption effect from P3). Keeping an fp32 master copy costs memory but lets small updates accumulate.

Both 16-bit formats ran an epoch about 1.6 times faster than fp32 and used less than half the memory, at the same accuracy: these are single runs, so differences of a few tenths of a point are not a ranking. TF32 did nothing for this network’s speed. A convolutional network at this size spends much of its step on BatchNorm, ReLU and pooling, which are limited by memory bandwidth rather than arithmetic (Chapter 22 profiles it), so faster multiplies help less. Its lower peak is not from smaller tensors, since TF32 stores fp32, but from the convolution algorithms cuDNN chose and the scratch space they need.

Where the arithmetic dominates, the gains are larger. One training step of a 4,096 → 8,192 → 8,192 → 8,192 → 10 MLP on a batch of 4,096:

Pure bf16, with the weights themselves in bf16, lost 0.7 points in 8 epochs: absorption swallowed the smallest updates. It is used for very large models where the fp32 master copy is too expensive, usually with tricks such as stochastic rounding or Kahan summation in the optimiser; for everything else, keep fp32 master weights.

How small gradients really are

fp16’s smallest normal number is 2⁻¹⁴ ≈ 6.1 × 10⁻⁵; below that it has subnormals with fewer significant bits, down to 2⁻²⁴ ≈ 6 × 10⁻⁸, and anything smaller becomes 0. P3 found that a small MLP’s weight gradients mostly fit. The gradients that flow backwards through the network’s activations, which the backward pass computes in 16 bits under autocast, are much smaller, because the loss is an average over the batch and every element’s share is divided by its size. Most of the CNN’s activation gradients were too small for fp16, and yet the CNN trained in fp16 without a scaler to within half a point of fp32. A weight’s gradient is a sum over every position and example in the batch, and that sum is dominated by the largest terms; losing the smallest ones changed only 0.004% of the weight-gradient values to zero. The transformer was not so lucky. In fp16 it would lose 1.5% of its activation gradients outright, and almost all the rest would be subnormals with reduced precision, and it trained badly:

Byte-level LM, 2,000 stepsValidation bits per byteWeight gradients exactly 0
fp321.9540.58%
bf16 autocast1.9570.60%
fp16 autocast, no scaler2.7471.53%
fp16 autocast + GradScaler1.951(scale 65,536, no skipped steps)

(The 0.6% of zeros in fp32 are mostly embedding rows for byte values that did not occur in the batch: Chapter 20’s sparse gradients.) Loss scaling multiplies the loss by a large factor S before backward(), so every gradient in the chain is S times larger and lands inside fp16’s range; the scaler divides the weight gradients by S before the optimiser uses them. GradScaler starts at S = 65,536, halves it and skips the step whenever a gradient overflows to inf or NaN, and doubles it after 2,000 clean steps. In this run it never needed to change: 65,536 moved the gradients into range and nothing overflowed. It cost nothing measurable, which is the point: in fp16, always use it. bf16 needs none, because its range is fp32’s; its 8-bit precision rounds each gradient, but rounding is an error networks tolerate, and a zero is not.

The layout trap

The first run of this chapter’s experiment gave absurd numbers: fp32 at 23.4 seconds an epoch, fp16 at 14.8, and bf16 at 2.8. The cause was the memory layout. The data had been loaded in a way that left it stored channels-last (NHWC), and the convolution kernels cuDNN picked for that layout on this GPU were fast in bf16 and very slow in fp32 and fp16:

Seconds per epochNCHW (default)Channels-last (NHWC)
fp325.2923.44 (4.4× slower)
fp16 autocast + GradScaler3.2214.81 (4.6× slower)
bf16 autocast3.292.80 (15% faster)

Channels-last is the layout tensor cores prefer and is usually recommended together with mixed precision, and for bf16 it was indeed the fastest configuration measured. But the right layout depends on the precision, the GPU and the library version, and a layout change can happen by accident: a permute without .contiguous() is enough. Accuracy was unaffected either way; only the clock noticed. Chapter 22 finds this kind of problem with the profiler.

🛰️
Use case: moving a satellite-image segmentation model to mixed precision

A team trains a U-Net on 512 × 512 satellite tiles on older V100 GPUs, which have fp16 tensor cores but no bf16. They wrap the forward pass in fp16 autocast and add GradScaler, but their training loop clips gradients to a norm of 1 and they at first clip the scaled gradients, which are 65,536 times larger, so every step is clipped to almost nothing and the loss stalls. Calling scaler.unscale_(opt) before clipping fixes it. They then check the speed-up properly: 1.9 times faster with NCHW, but slower than fp32 when someone’s data-loading change left the tiles channels-last. A profile shows the convolution kernels as the culprit, and one .contiguous() restores the speed.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • fp16 without GradScaler: fine for the CNN here, 0.8 bits per byte worse for the transformer. You cannot tell in advance which you have.
  • Clipping scaled gradients: call scaler.unscale_(opt) first.
  • Casting the model to 16 bits (model.half()) instead of autocast: small updates are absorbed; pure bf16 lost 0.7 points.
  • Expecting TF32 to speed up every network: 1.5× on matmuls, nothing on this CNN.
  • Trusting a timing without checking the layout: channels-last made fp32 4.4× slower and bf16 15% faster.
  • Computing the loss on 16-bit logits: cast them to fp32 (.float()) or let autocast run the loss in fp32.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is mixed precision training, and why keep an fp32 copy of the weights?

How a strong answer sounds
Running the expensive operations, matmuls and convolutions, in a 16-bit format on tensor cores, while keeping numerically sensitive operations such as softmax, normalisation and the loss in fp32, and keeping the weights, gradients and optimiser state in fp32. The fp32 master weights matter because updates are tiny relative to the weights: a bf16 weight of 1.0 given a thousand updates of 1e-4 never moved, since each result rounded back to 1.0, while fp32 accumulated them to 0.9. Measured, bf16 autocast was 1.6 times faster on a CNN and 2.8 times on a matmul-heavy MLP, at the same accuracy, with less than half the memory.

Deeper: Why does fp16 need loss scaling and bf16 does not?

How a strong answer sounds
Range. fp16 has 5 exponent bits, so anything below about 6e-8 becomes zero, and gradients of activations are often smaller than that because the loss averages over the batch: in a CNN I measured, the median activation gradient was 2e-8 and 64% of them would be zero in fp16. Loss scaling multiplies the loss by a large factor, 65,536 by default, so every gradient is shifted into range, then divides it out before the optimiser step, and GradScaler lowers the factor when something overflows. bf16 has fp32’s 8 exponent bits, so nothing underflows, only rounds. In practice the effect varies: the CNN trained fine without scaling, but a small transformer ended 0.8 bits per byte worse, so with fp16 I always use the scaler.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Autocast runs matmuls and convolutions in 16 bits and sensitive operations in fp32; weights and optimiser state stay fp32.
  • bf16 autocast: 1.6× faster on a CNN, 2.8× on a matmul-heavy MLP, less than half the memory, same accuracy.
  • Activation gradients are tiny: median 2 × 10⁻⁸ in the CNN, 64% below fp16’s range.
  • fp16 needs GradScaler: without it a transformer ended 0.8 bits per byte worse. bf16 needs no scaler.
  • Memory layout can change a timing by 4×; time NCHW and channels-last for your precision and GPU.
