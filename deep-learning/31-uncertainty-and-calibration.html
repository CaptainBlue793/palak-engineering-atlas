Uncertainty & Calibration in Neural Networks

A classifier’s softmax output looks like a probability, and every system that sets a threshold, ranks cases for review or combines the model with other evidence treats it as one. Whether it deserves that is an empirical question with two parts. Is the network calibrated: when it says 80%, is it right 80% of the time? And does it know what it does not know: is it less confident on inputs unlike its training data? Neural networks are known for failing both, and the fixes, from rescaling the logits to training several networks, have measurable costs.

This chapter measures them on a CIFAR-10 CNN. Wider versions were more over-confident: calibration error rose from 0.6% to 2.3% as the network grew 60-fold. One fitted temperature halved the error in distribution. An ensemble of five networks gained 1.4 points of accuracy and cut the log loss by 17%. Then the inputs changed. Under moderate blur, accuracy fell from 90% to 21% while the network stayed confident: a calibration error of 54%, which no method brought below 44%. Shown handwritten digits, a kind of image it had never seen, it was on average 82% sure they were airplanes, cars or frogs. The best single score for spotting such inputs was the energy of the logits, not the softmax probability.

  • Measure calibration with ECE, log loss and a reliability diagram, and know what each misses
  • Explain why larger networks tend to be over-confident, and fix it with temperature scaling
  • Build a deep ensemble and an MC-dropout model, and compare what they buy
  • Test uncertainty under distribution shift, not only on the test set
  • Score inputs as in- or out-of-distribution, and use confidence for selective prediction

Over-confidence

Three numbers describe how well probabilities match outcomes. Expected calibration error (ECE) bins predictions by confidence and averages the gap between confidence and accuracy in each bin. Log loss (negative log-likelihood) punishes confident mistakes heavily and rewards confident correct answers. The mean confidence compared with the accuracy shows the direction of the error. The same CNN at four widths, each trained for 30 epochs with augmentation and weight decay:

WidthParametersAccuracyMean confidenceECELog loss
¼36k82.5%82.1%0.61%0.503

Each wider network was more accurate and better by log loss, and also more over-confident: the smallest was slightly under-confident, the largest 2.3 points more confident than it was right. This is the pattern Guo et al. (2017) reported for modern networks: high capacity lets the training loss keep falling by making correct predictions ever more confident, and that confidence carries over to the test set where some of those predictions are wrong. With augmentation and weight decay the effect here is modest; without them it is much larger. The fix that needs no retraining is temperature scaling: divide the logits by one number T fitted on held-out data. For the width-1 network, T = 1.15 cut ECE from 1.61% to 0.75%, with the same accuracy, since dividing every logit by the same T never changes which class is largest.

Ensembles and MC dropout

A single network gives one answer per input, with no sense of how much a different, equally good network would disagree. Two practical ways to get that sense: train several networks from different random seeds and average their probabilities (a deep ensemble, Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017), or keep dropout switched on at test time and average many stochastic passes of one network (MC dropout, Gal and Ghahramani, 2016).

ModelAccuracyLog lossECEMean confidenceTest-time cost
Single network90.43%0.2901.61%91.9%1×
Single, temperature scaled90.43%0.2860.75%90.4%1×
Ensemble of 291.20%0.2600.91%90.8%2×
Ensemble of 591.82%0.2401.59%90.3%5×
MC dropout, 20 passes89.73%0.3112.64%87.2%20×

The ensemble was the best model by accuracy and log loss, and the gains came quickly: two members gave most of the log-loss improvement. Its ECE did not keep falling, because averaging made it under-confident: with five members it was right 91.8% of the time while averaging 90.3% confidence. Ensembles and temperature scaling combine well, since a temperature fitted to the ensemble removes that residual bias. MC dropout was the weakest here: the dropout network was itself less accurate, and its averaged predictions were under-confident. It is cheap to add to a model that already uses dropout; it is not a substitute for an ensemble.

Calibration under distribution shift

Calibration measured on the test set says nothing about inputs that differ from the training data, and deployed models always meet some. The test images were blurred, noised and reduced in contrast at three severities:

Test imagesSingle: accuracy / ECETemperature scaled: ECEEnsemble of 5: accuracy / ECEMC dropout: ECE
Clean90.4% / 1.6%0.8%91.8% / 1.6%2.6%
Gaussian noise, mild65.4% / 13.8%10.8%70.5% / 4.7%9.1%
Blur, mild68.8% / 9.7%6.4%72.0% / 2.7%2.1%
Blur, moderate20.6% / 54.2%50.2%22.0% / 45.9%44.7%
Low contrast, moderate63.1% / 2.0%4.8%66.7% / 5.8%4.4%

Under moderate blur the network was right one time in five and still confident, a calibration error of 54%, and every method left it above 44%. A temperature fitted on clean data helped a little with blur and noise and made low contrast worse. The ensemble was the most accurate model on seven of the nine corrupted test sets and had the best log loss on six, and much the best calibration under mild noise, which matches the large-scale study by Ovadia et al. (2019). The lesson is uncomfortable but useful: no post-hoc fix makes a network’s confidence trustworthy on inputs far from its training data, so test on shifted data before relying on the probabilities, and monitor for shift in production.

Detecting unfamiliar inputs

An input from a different distribution entirely, a handwritten digit shown to a CIFAR-10 classifier, has no correct class among the ten. The network answers anyway. On MNIST digits the single network’s average confidence was 82%, close to its 92% on real CIFAR-10 images. Out-of-distribution (OOD) detection scores each input and flags the low scorers; AUROC measures how well a score separates CIFAR-10 test images (in) from the unfamiliar set (out), where 50% is chance:

Score (AUROC)Handwritten digitsClothingUniform noise
Max softmax probability, single75.9%89.1%86.6%
Energy (log-sum-exp of the logits), single80.4%94.0%96.3%
Max softmax, ensemble77.2%91.2%86.6%
Entropy of the ensemble average78.1%93.5%92.8%
Disagreement between members (mutual information)80.2%91.2%88.2%
Mutual information, MC dropout81.3%85.5%70.8%

The energy score (Liu et al., 2020), computed from one network’s logits at no extra cost, was the best or near-best on all three sets. It works because the softmax throws away the overall size of the logits, and unfamiliar inputs tend to produce smaller logits across the board even when one of them still wins. Digits were the hardest case for every method: around 80%, meaning one in five pairs of a digit and a real image was ranked the wrong way round. In practice OOD detection is tuned on whatever unfamiliar data you expect to meet, and paired with simple input checks; no score catches everything.

Within the distribution, confidence is still useful for selective prediction: answer only when confident and send the rest to a person. Answering the most confident 80% of test images, the single network was right on 97.6% of them and the ensemble on 98.5%; at 50% coverage, 99.7% and 99.8%.

Use case: a skin-lesion triage tool

A clinic deploys a classifier that marks photos of skin lesions as low or high risk, and doctors review only the uncertain ones. On the validation set, temperature scaling makes the probabilities match outcomes and the review queue is small. Months later, a new phone model with stronger image processing arrives and the error rate on “confident” photos triples. The team adds three things: a five-member ensemble, which keeps its log loss far better on shifted photos; an energy-score check that routes photos unlike anything in training straight to a doctor; and a monthly calibration report on newly labelled cases, so drift is seen in weeks rather than discovered in complaints.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Treating softmax outputs as probabilities without checking: the width-2 network was 2.3 points over-confident.
  • Checking calibration only on the test set: under blur the ECE was 54%.
  • Fitting the temperature on training data: fit it on held-out data the model never trained on.
  • Assuming an ensemble is calibrated: averaging made it under-confident; refit a temperature on the ensemble.
  • Using max softmax probability for OOD detection by default: energy was better on every set here.
  • Expecting low confidence on nonsense inputs: the network was 82% confident on handwritten digits.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What does it mean for a classifier to be calibrated, and how do you fix one that is not?

How a strong answer sounds
Among all the predictions it makes with 80% confidence, about 80% should be right, and likewise at every level. I check with a reliability diagram and ECE, and with log loss, which punishes confident mistakes. Large networks tend to be over-confident: widening a CIFAR-10 CNN 60-fold raised ECE from 0.6% to 2.3%. The standard fix is temperature scaling, dividing the logits by one number fitted on held-out data, which halved ECE in my measurement without changing a single prediction. If I can afford it, an ensemble also improves accuracy and log loss, but I refit a temperature on it because averaging made it under-confident.

Deeper: Your model will see inputs unlike its training data. How do you handle its uncertainty?

How a strong answer sounds
First, measure on shifted data, because test-set calibration does not transfer: under blur a CNN fell to 21% accuracy with an ECE of 54%, and temperature scaling barely helped. Ensembles are the most robust option I know; they were the most accurate on seven of nine corrupted test sets. Second, detect inputs that are not from the training distribution at all: the softmax was 82% confident on handwritten digits, so I would use the energy score, the log-sum-exp of the logits, which separated unfamiliar images best. Third, design the system around it: selective prediction, answering only above a confidence threshold, gave 98.5% accuracy on the 80% most confident cases, and monitoring calibration on fresh labels in production.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Wider networks were more accurate and more over-confident (ECE 0.6% → 2.3%).
  • Temperature scaling halved ECE in distribution and never changes a prediction.
  • A 5-member ensemble: +1.4 points accuracy, −17% log loss, usually best under shift, but under-confident until rescaled.
  • Under moderate blur ECE reached 54%; no method fixed it. Test on shifted data.
  • Networks are confident on nonsense; the energy score detected unfamiliar inputs better than softmax confidence.
