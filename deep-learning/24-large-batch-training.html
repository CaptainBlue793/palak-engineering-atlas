Batch Size, Scaling Rules & Large Batches

A larger batch does more work per step and uses the GPU better, and with many GPUs it is the only way to use them all at once. The question is what it does to learning. A gradient averaged over more examples is less noisy, so each step can be larger, but there are fewer steps per epoch, and past some batch size a cleaner gradient stops helping. Practitioners scale the learning rate with the batch by a rule, and the rules hold only so far.

This chapter measures that on a CIFAR-10 CNN with batches from 32 to 8,192. With 15 epochs of training, the linear scaling rule worked at batch 512 and failed badly beyond it: at 2,048 it gave 68.9% where leaving the learning rate alone gave 80.6%, and at 8,192 it did not train at all. With the learning rate tuned for each batch and no limit on epochs, the steps needed to reach 85% test accuracy halved every time the batch doubled, up to a few hundred; beyond that each doubling saved less, and batch 8,192 never reached 85% in 40 epochs. A popular estimate of where that turn comes, the gradient noise scale, put it at 16 to 39, an order of magnitude too small. Layer-wise optimisers did help at 8,192: LARS, LAMB and AdamW reached about 80% where SGD peaked at 74%, still ten points short of batch 256.

  • Measure how gradient noise falls with batch size and how it changes during training
  • Apply and test the linear and square-root learning-rate scaling rules
  • Read a steps-to-target versus batch-size curve and find the critical batch size
  • Know what LARS and LAMB change and when they are worth using
  • Choose a batch size for a fixed compute or time budget

Batch size and gradient noise

Chapter 8 showed that the error of a mini-batch gradient falls as 1/√B. What matters for a step is direction: does the mini-batch gradient point the way the full-data gradient does? Measured as the cosine between them on the CNN, at four points in training (BatchNorm in evaluation mode, so a gradient does not depend on its batch-mates):

Two things stand out. At initialisation even 1,024 examples gave a direction only 0.66 aligned with the truth, because the full gradient was tiny (norm 0.035) and the per-example gradients pointed every which way. After one epoch the full gradient was 250 times larger and a batch of 256 was already 0.97 aligned. And as training continued the gradients became noisier again: by epoch 20 a batch of 16 was only 0.54 aligned. Early in training the signal is strong and small batches suffice; late in training, near a minimum, the useful signal shrinks relative to the noise, which is one argument for growing the batch as training proceeds.

McCandlish et al. (2018) turn this into a number, the simple noise scale Bnoise = tr(Σ)/|G|², the batch size at which noise and signal are about equal, and argue that it predicts the critical batch size beyond which larger batches stop paying. Measured here: 1,340 at initialisation, then 16 after one epoch, 18 after five and 39 after twenty. Keep those numbers in mind for the next section.

The linear scaling rule

If B examples give a gradient with noise ∝ 1/√B, multiplying the batch by k means k times fewer steps, and to cover the same distance each step should be k times longer: the linear scaling rule (Goyal et al., 2017), used with a warm-up. The square-root rule scales by √k, keeping the update’s noise constant instead. Tested from a tuned base of batch 256 at learning rate 0.1, 15 epochs each, one-cycle schedule, 5% warm-up:

BatchNo scaling (lr 0.1)Square-root ruleLinear ruleLinear, no warm-up

At a fixed number of epochs both rules held up to batch 512, and warm-up mattered there (83.9% without, 88.1% with). Beyond that they failed, because at 8,192 fifteen epochs are only 105 optimiser steps: no learning rate can make 105 steps do the work of 2,940, and the large rates the rules prescribe made those few steps unstable. Goyal et al. trained ImageNet at batch 8,192 with the linear rule because 90 epochs of 1.28 million images still gave about 14,000 steps. The rule is a statement about step size, not a promise that fewer steps are enough.

Critical batch size: steps versus samples

A fair comparison gives every batch size its own tuned learning rate and enough epochs, and asks how many optimiser steps, and how many training examples, it takes to reach a target, here 85% test accuracy (Shallue et al., 2019):

The curve has the shape Shallue et al. found across many models. From 64 to 512, doubling the batch nearly halved the steps while the examples processed rose only slowly (0.65M to 0.86M): close to perfect scaling, where a step on twice the data is worth two steps. From about 1,024 the savings fell to 29% per doubling, and the examples needed rose steeply: 2.1 million at 4,096 against 0.75 million at 256. That turn is the critical batch size, here somewhere between 512 and 1,024. Beyond it, larger batches still finish in fewer steps, which is what matters when steps are spread across many GPUs, but they waste compute. The best learning rate did not follow either scaling rule: it grew from 0.006 to 0.4 and then fell back to 0.1, because at large batches the binding constraint became stability rather than noise.

The gradient noise scale, 16 to 39 after the first epoch, predicted the turn an order of magnitude too early. The estimate assumes plain SGD at its optimal learning rate on a quadratic model of the loss; this network has momentum, BatchNorm and augmentation, and the noise scale changed fourfold during training. It is a useful order-of-magnitude tool for very large models, where it was calibrated, and here a direct measurement was worth more.

LARS and LAMB

At very large batches, the update that suits one layer can be far too big for another, because layers differ in the ratio of gradient norm to weight norm. LARS (You et al., 2017) gives each layer a learning rate proportional to ‖w‖/‖g‖, a “trust ratio”, on top of SGD with momentum; LAMB (You et al., 2020) does the same on top of Adam. At batch 8,192, 30 epochs (210 steps), each with a learning-rate sweep:

Optimiser at batch 8,192Learning rates triedBest test accuracy
SGD + Nesterov momentum0.1 – 3.274.17% (lr 0.2)

All three adaptive optimisers beat tuned SGD by about six points, and all three peaked at the largest learning rate tried, so their true best is likely somewhat higher: the sweeps were not wide enough, a mistake worth naming because it is so common. Plain AdamW did as well as the trust-ratio methods here; per-parameter adaptivity was already enough at this scale. And none came within ten points of batch 256 trained for the same 30 epochs, which had 28 times as many steps. Large-batch optimisers make large batches workable; they do not make 210 steps equal to 5,880.

Steps versus samples to a target

The right batch size depends on what is scarce. If compute is scarce (one GPU, a fixed bill), choose a batch near or below the critical batch size, where each example does the most work: here 128 to 512. If time is scarce and hardware plentiful, go larger, accept that each doubling buys less, and pay for it in compute: batch 4,096 used three times the examples of 256 to reach the same accuracy, in a sixth of the steps. Either way, re-tune the learning rate and warm-up at the new batch size, and give the schedule enough steps.

🛰️
Use case: moving a training job from 1 GPU to 32

A team trains an image model at batch 256 on one GPU in two days and gets access to 32 GPUs. Scaling to batch 8,192 with the linear rule and the same 90 epochs, the run diverges in the first epoch; with a 5-epoch warm-up it trains but finishes 3 points worse, and the hoped-for 32-times speed-up is 18 times. They measure steps to their target accuracy at batches 256, 1,024, 2,048 and 4,096 for one epoch budget each and find the curve bending at about 2,048. They settle on batch 2,048 across 32 GPUs with gradient accumulation off, LAMB, and a longer schedule, which reaches the single-GPU accuracy in 6 hours instead of two days, and they stop expecting batch size alone to buy the rest.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Scaling the batch at a fixed epoch budget: at 8,192, 15 epochs were 105 steps and the linear rule gave 10%.
  • Dropping warm-up with a scaled learning rate: at batch 512, 83.9% without against 88.1% with.
  • Trusting a scaling rule instead of re-tuning: the best rate rose from 0.006 to 0.4 and fell back to 0.1.
  • Taking the noise-scale estimate as the answer: it said 16–39; the measured turn was near 1,000.
  • Sweeps that stop at the best value: LARS, LAMB and AdamW all peaked at the edge.
  • Expecting large-batch runs to reproduce: rerunning batch 8,192 at lr 0.2 gave 43.7% where an earlier run gave 54.0%; few, large steps amplify every small difference (Chapter 38).

Interview drill

Explain it simply: You increase the batch size by 8×. What do you do to the learning rate?

How a strong answer sounds
Start from the linear scaling rule, multiplying it by 8 with a warm-up, or the square-root rule, multiplying by about 2.8, and then re-tune, because the rules are only a starting point. They come from noise: a gradient averaged over 8 times the data is less noisy and can take a longer step. They break down when there are too few steps or the step becomes unstable: on a CIFAR CNN at a fixed 15 epochs the linear rule worked at batch 512, gave 68.9% against 80.6% at 2,048, and failed to train at 8,192. I would also check that the run still has enough optimiser steps, and lengthen the schedule if not.

Deeper: What is the critical batch size and how would you find it?

How a strong answer sounds
It is the batch size beyond which doubling the batch no longer halves the number of steps to a target, so larger batches start wasting compute. I find it directly: tune the learning rate at several batch sizes, measure steps to a fixed accuracy, and look for where steps stop halving and examples processed start climbing. On a CIFAR CNN steps halved from 64 to 512 with almost constant examples, and from about 1,024 each doubling saved only 29% while examples tripled by 4,096. The gradient noise scale is a cheap estimate, but it said 16 to 39 there, an order of magnitude too small, so I would trust the measurement.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Larger batches point the gradient more truly, but the signal weakens late in training (batch 16: 0.71 → 0.54 alignment).
  • Scaling rules set a step size; at a fixed epoch budget they failed beyond batch 512 because steps ran out.
  • With tuned rates, steps halved per doubling up to ~512, then savings fell to 29%: the critical batch size.
  • The noise-scale estimate (16–39) missed the measured turn (~1,000) by an order of magnitude.
  • At 8,192, LARS, LAMB and AdamW reached ~80% vs SGD’s 74%, still ten points below batch 256.
