Activation Functions

Between every pair of linear layers sits one small function applied to each number on its own. Without it, a hundred layers collapse into one linear map (Chapter 5). With it, the network can bend. Which function you choose decides two things at once: what shapes the network can build easily, and, far more important in practice, how gradients survive the trip back through many layers. A derivative that is at most 0.25 sounds harmless until it is multiplied by itself ten times.

This chapter measures six activations on Fashion-MNIST. At initialisation, in a 10-layer network, the gradient reaching the first layer was 40 million times smaller than the last layer’s with sigmoid and 24 with ReLU. Trained for five epochs, a 10-layer sigmoid network reached 10.0% while ReLU reached 86.33% and GELU 86.82%; at two layers all six were within 0.84 points. A 4-layer ReLU network trained with Adam at 0.03 ended with 90.62% of one layer’s units dead, never active on any of 10,000 images, and 55.76% accuracy. Leaky ReLU did not save it (50.07%); the default learning rate did (86.4%).

Why non-linearity

Two linear layers in a row compute W₂(W₁x + b₁) + b₂ = (W₂W₁)x + (W₂b₁ + b₂): one linear layer with a different weight matrix. Any depth of purely linear layers is a single linear model, and Chapter 2 showed what one of those cannot learn. An activation φ applied between the layers breaks the collapse, and almost any non-linear φ will do for expressiveness: the universal approximation results of Chapter 5 hold for sigmoids, tanh and ReLU alike. What separates them is trainability, and that is a question about derivatives.

sigmoid(z) = 1 / (1 + e^-z)          # (0, 1);  derivative σ(1 − σ) ≤ 0.25
tanh(z)                             # (−1, 1); derivative 1 − tanh² ≤ 1, zero-centred
relu(z)    = max(0, z)              # [0, ∞);  derivative 1 for z > 0, 0 for z < 0
leaky(z)   = max(0.01z, z)          # derivative 1 or 0.01: never exactly 0
gelu(z)    = z · Φ(z)                # smooth ReLU; Φ is the Gaussian CDF (BERT, GPT)
silu(z)    = z · sigmoid(z)          # also "swish"; smooth, slightly negative dip (EfficientNet, LLaMA's SwiGLU)

Saturation in sigmoid and tanh

Backprop multiplies the gradient by φ′(z) at every layer (Chapter 4). Sigmoid’s derivative is at most 0.25, at z = 0, and falls towards zero on both sides as the function flattens, or saturates. Through ten sigmoid layers the gradient is multiplied by at most 0.25¹⁰ ≈ 10⁻⁶ from the activations alone. tanh is better (its derivative reaches 1 at zero and its outputs are centred on zero) but saturates just the same for large |z|. Measured at initialisation in a 10-layer, 256-wide network with PyTorch’s default weights, the gradient norm of the first layer’s weights relative to the last layer’s:

Two things stand out. Sigmoid is in a class of its own: its first layer received a gradient 40 million times smaller than its last, and the 10-layer sigmoid network never left chance (10.0%), even with Adam, which rescales small gradients. The rest of the ordering is less tidy than the textbook story. tanh passed gradients back almost unchanged at initialisation (ratio 0.81), yet its 10-layer network trained erratically with Adam (72.66% after one epoch, 55.52% after five) while plain SGD took it to 84.22%. The ReLU family shrank gradients 24 times and still trained to about 86%. With plain SGD, though, the 10-layer ReLU network did not train at all (10.0%): PyTorch’s default initialisation shrinks a ReLU network’s signal at every layer (the activations’ standard deviation was 0.02043 by layer 10), which Adam can compensate for and SGD cannot. That is an initialisation problem, and Chapter 9 fixes it. At two layers the choice barely matters: all six landed within 0.84 points (87.46% to 88.3%).

ReLU and dead units

ReLU’s derivative is exactly 1 wherever the unit is active, so an active path passes gradients back undiminished; that is the main reason deep networks became trainable after 2010. It is also nearly free to compute, and it produces sparse activations (about half the units are zero for a given input). Its weakness is the other half of its derivative: 0 for negative input. A unit whose pre-activation is negative for every input receives no gradient from any example, so its weights never change and it stays off forever. It is dead. A large learning-rate step that pushes a unit’s bias strongly negative is the usual cause.

Measured on a 4-hidden-layer ReLU network after 3 epochs of Adam, counting units that output zero for all of 10,000 training images: at initialisation, 0.0% in the first three layers (5.47% in the fourth). After training at the default rate of 1e-3: 0.0%, 5.47%, 8.98%, 12.11% by layer, at 86.4% accuracy. At 1e-2, 26% to 66% of units were dead and accuracy fell to 83.17%; at 3e-2, up to 90.62% and 55.76%. Leaky ReLU, whose units can never be cut off completely, did not rescue the high rates: as large a share of its units ended up never producing a positive output (up to 89.84%), and its accuracy was no better (80.95% and 50.07%). A slope of 0.01 gives a dormant unit a gradient a hundred times weaker than an active one, too weak to pull it back against a learning rate that keeps knocking it out. The fix that worked was the learning rate itself.

GELU, SiLU and smooth variants

GELU, z·Φ(z), and SiLU, z·σ(z), look like ReLU with the corner rounded off: close to z for large positive z, close to 0 for large negative z, smooth in between, with a small negative dip around z ≈ −0.75 (GELU) or −1.28 (SiLU). Their derivatives are never exactly zero over a region, so units cannot die in the ReLU sense, and the smoothness seems to help optimisation in very deep transformer stacks. GELU is the activation of BERT and the GPT family; SiLU, usually inside a gated unit called SwiGLU, is used by LLaMA and many recent models (LLMs, The Transformer Block). In this experiment they shrank gradients more than ReLU at initialisation (the first layer got about 0.3% of the last layer’s gradient, against 4% for ReLU), because their slope near zero is about 0.5, yet ten layers deep with Adam they matched the best ReLU variant (86.82% and 86.75%) and they were the best two at two layers (88.28% and 88.3%).

Use case: a click model whose capacity quietly halved

An ads team retrains its click-through model with a learning rate twice as high to finish overnight. Offline AUC drops slightly, which they put down to noise. A dashboard that logs the fraction of ReLU units with zero activation over a day’s traffic shows the real story: 47% of the units in the second hidden layer are dead after the first epoch, so the model has half the capacity it was designed with. Switching to Leaky ReLU on its own changes little; lowering the peak rate and adding warm-up (Chapter 12) restores the AUC, and the dead-unit fraction becomes a standing training metric.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Sigmoid or tanh as the hidden activation of a deep network: the gradient shrinks at every layer.
  • Ignoring dead units: measure the fraction of units that never fire; high learning rates kill them.
  • An activation on the final layer before cross-entropy: emit logits; the loss applies softmax or sigmoid itself.
  • Assuming the activation matters most in a shallow network: at two layers all six were within a point.
  • Swapping the activation without revisiting initialisation: Kaiming assumes ReLU’s halving of variance (Chapter 9).

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Why did ReLU replace sigmoid as the default hidden activation?

How a strong answer sounds
Because of gradients through depth. Backprop multiplies by the activation’s derivative at every layer; sigmoid’s derivative is at most 0.25 and near zero when it saturates, so in a deep sigmoid network the early layers get gradients that are orders of magnitude too small and barely learn. ReLU’s derivative is exactly 1 for active units, so gradients pass through unchanged, and it is cheaper to compute. In a 10-layer network at initialisation I measured the first layer’s gradient 40 million times smaller than the last with sigmoid, and the sigmoid network never got off chance; ReLU trained to 86.33%. The price is dead units: a ReLU that is negative for every input never gets a gradient again.

Deeper: Your ReLU network’s accuracy is lower than expected and you suspect dead units. How do you check and fix it?

How a strong answer sounds
Run a few thousand training examples through the network and count, for each hidden layer, the units whose activation is zero for every one of them. A few per cent is normal; tens of per cent is a problem. The usual cause is too large a learning rate, often early in training, pushing biases strongly negative. Fixes: lower the learning rate or add warm-up, use Leaky ReLU or GELU so units always get some gradient, use a proper initialisation, and add normalisation layers, which keep pre-activations centred. In a measured run, Adam at 0.03 left up to 90.62% of a layer’s ReLUs dead and accuracy at 55.76%; Leaky ReLU did not fix that (50.07%), but the default rate of 1e-3 did (86.4%).

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Without a non-linearity any number of layers is one linear map; with almost any, the network can approximate anything.
  • The choice is about trainability: backprop multiplies by φ′ at every layer, and sigmoid’s is at most 0.25.
  • Measured at 10 layers: sigmoid stayed at chance (10.0%), ReLU, GELU and SiLU reached about 86.5%; at 2 layers all six were within 0.84 points.
  • ReLU passes gradients unchanged when active but can die; Adam at 0.03 left up to 90.62% of a layer dead, and Leaky ReLU did not rescue it: lower the learning rate.
  • GELU and SiLU are smooth ReLUs used in transformers; sigmoid and tanh remain for gates and bounded outputs.

Check your understanding

What is the largest possible derivative of the sigmoid?

σ′ = σ(1 − σ), largest when σ = 0.5. Ten such factors multiply to at most about 10⁻⁶.

What makes a ReLU unit “dead”?

No gradient means no update, so it cannot recover on its own.

Why does Leaky ReLU avoid dead units?

A 0.01 slope keeps a gradient flowing. Measured here, though, it did not rescue a learning rate that was far too high.

Which activation should the final layer of a classifier trained with F.cross_entropy use?

Applying softmax first is the double-softmax bug from the course home page.

Where are GELU and SiLU most commonly used?

They behave like ReLU but are smooth and never have a zero-gradient region.
