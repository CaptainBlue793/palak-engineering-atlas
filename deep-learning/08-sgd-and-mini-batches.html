Stochastic Gradient Descent & Mini-Batches

Gradient descent is simple: compute the gradient of the loss over the training set and step against it. Computing it over the whole training set for every step is not: for a dataset of a million images it means a million forward and backward passes per update. Stochastic gradient descent estimates the gradient from a small random mini-batch instead, and takes many cheap, noisy steps rather than a few exact ones. Almost every network is trained this way, and two numbers decide how well it works: the batch size and the learning rate.

Both are measured here on Fashion-MNIST. A mini-batch of 8 gave a gradient whose direction agreed with the true full-data gradient by a cosine of only 0.18; a batch of 8,192 agreed to 0.99. Yet for a fixed budget of five epochs, batch 8 reached 87.8% test accuracy and the exact full-batch gradient only 64.6%, because one took 37,500 steps and the other 5. A learning-rate sweep showed the best rate at 0.3 for one epoch, and divergence from 1.0. And on a two-dimensional ravine, gradient descent converged at a learning rate of 0.095 and exploded at 0.105, on either side of the limit 2/λ that theory predicts.

Full-batch vs stochastic

The training loss is an average over N examples, L(θ) = (1/N) Σᵢ ℓᵢ(θ), so its gradient is the average of the per-example gradients. A mini-batch of B random examples gives an unbiased estimate of that average: right on average, wrong by some noise each time. The cost of a step is proportional to B; the noise shrinks roughly as 1/√B. That is the whole trade.

w = w - lr * grad(L, w)                                # full batch: exact gradient, N examples per step
for xb, yb in DataLoader(ds, batch_size=128, shuffle=True):   # mini-batch SGD: N / 128 steps per epoch
    loss = F.cross_entropy(model(xb), yb)              # mean over 128: an estimate of the full loss
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()       # w -= lr * (noisy gradient)
# one epoch = one pass over the data = N / B iterations (steps)

The relative error halves each time the batch quadruples, exactly the 1/√B law: 3.88, 1.93, 1.01, 0.52, 0.24, 0.13. At the common batch size of 128 the noise is as large as the signal. SGD works anyway because errors in different steps point in different directions and average out over many steps, while the true gradient’s direction is consistent and accumulates.

Mini-batch gradient noise and the batch size

If big batches give better gradients, why not always use the biggest that fits in memory? Because for a fixed amount of computation, the number of examples processed, a bigger batch means fewer steps, and progress is made per step. Each configuration below trained for exactly five epochs, with the best learning rate from a grid of five:

Per epoch, small batches won: more steps, each one useful. Per second, large batches won: batch 1,024 ran a hundred times faster than batch 8, because the GPU does one big matrix multiply as quickly as many small ones (P7). The practical answer sits in between. Pick a batch size that keeps the GPU busy (here 128 to 1,024), then give it enough steps; train larger batches for more epochs, or raise their learning rate, which is the subject of Chapter 24. Note the best learning rate rose with batch size, from 0.01 at batch 8 to 0.3 at 1,024: less noisy gradients tolerate larger steps.

The learning rate

The learning rate is the most important hyperparameter in deep learning. Too small and training crawls; too large and each step overshoots, the loss bounces, and past some point it diverges to infinity or NaN. One epoch at batch 128:

Over three orders of magnitude the result moved from barely trained to good, and the best rate sat right next to the edge of divergence, which is typical. That is why learning rates are searched on a logarithmic grid, why schedules start small and grow (warm-up) or start large and shrink (decay), and why a range test, raising the rate during one short run and watching the loss, is the fastest way to find the edge (Chapter 12).

Divergence and oscillation

Why is there an edge at all? Near a minimum the loss looks like a bowl, ½λx² along each direction of curvature λ. Gradient descent on it updates x ← x − lr·λx = (1 − lr·λ)x. The distance to the minimum shrinks if |1 − lr·λ| < 1, that is if lr < 2/λ; at exactly 1/λ it lands in one step; between 1/λ and 2/λ it overshoots and oscillates while converging; above 2/λ every step overshoots by more than the last and it diverges. A real loss has many directions with different curvatures, and the steepest one sets the limit.

lam = torch.tensor([1.0, 20.0])                    # curvatures along x and y: a ravine, condition number 20
p = torch.tensor([-4.0, 1.0])
for t in range(60): p = p - lr * lam * p               # exact gradient of 0.5 * (x² + 20 y²)
# lr = 0.095 (< 2/20): f = 8.3e-5 after 60 steps;  lr = 0.105 (> 2/20): f = 927,091

The ravine also shows the other half of the problem. The flat direction (λ = 1) needs a large step to make progress, the steep one (λ = 20) tolerates only lr < 0.1, and one learning rate has to serve both. At lr = 0.02 the steep direction converges smoothly but the flat one crawls (f = 0.71 after 60 steps); at 0.095, close to the limit, the steep direction zig-zags across the ravine while the flat one finally moves. The ratio of the largest to the smallest curvature, the condition number, decides how bad this is. Momentum and adaptive optimisers (Chapter 11) and normalisation layers (Chapter 14) all exist largely to fix it.

Use case: moving training to a bigger GPU

A team moves its image-moderation model from a 16 GB card to an 80 GB one and raises the batch size from 64 to 512 to use the memory. Epochs run six times faster, but after the same number of epochs the model is 2 points worse, and they nearly roll back. The cause is the table above: eight times fewer steps. Raising the learning rate (they use the linear scaling rule with warm-up, Chapter 24) and training for the same number of steps rather than epochs recovers the accuracy and keeps most of the speed-up.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Comparing batch sizes at equal epochs: batch 1,024 had 8× fewer steps than batch 128 and scored 3 points lower.
  • Keeping the learning rate when the batch size changes: the best rate here went from 0.01 at batch 8 to 0.3 at 1,024.
  • Searching learning rates on a linear grid: results changed over orders of magnitude; use 1, 3, 10, 30… steps.
  • Treating noisy mini-batch losses as signal: at batch 128 the gradient noise was as large as the gradient.
  • Full-batch training for big data: 5 steps in 5 epochs reached 64.6%.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Why do we train with mini-batches instead of the whole dataset?

How a strong answer sounds
The gradient over the whole dataset is exact but costs a pass over all of it for one step. A random mini-batch gives an unbiased estimate of it for a tiny fraction of the cost, so you can take many more steps for the same compute, and noisy steps in roughly the right direction make far more progress than a few exact ones. The noise falls as one over the square root of the batch size; I measured the relative error of the gradient halving each time the batch quadrupled. For five epochs on Fashion-MNIST, batch 128 reached 86.9% and the full batch only 64.6%. The batch also has to be big enough to keep the GPU busy, which is the other side of the trade.

Deeper: Why does training diverge when the learning rate is too large, and what sets the limit?

How a strong answer sounds
Near a minimum the loss is approximately quadratic, and along a direction with curvature λ a gradient step multiplies the distance to the minimum by 1 − lr·λ. If lr is more than 2/λ that factor is larger than one in absolute value, so each step overshoots further and the iterates blow up. The sharpest direction, the largest eigenvalue of the Hessian, sets the limit for the whole network. I checked it on a quadratic with curvatures 1 and 20: lr 0.095 converged and lr 0.105 exploded to a loss near a million. The flat directions then crawl, which is the ill-conditioning that momentum and adaptive methods address.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • A mini-batch gradient is an unbiased, noisy estimate of the full gradient; its error falls as 1/√B (measured: 3.88 at batch 8 to 0.13 at 8,192).
  • At a fixed number of epochs, smaller batches take more steps and won (87.8% at batch 8 vs 64.6% full batch); per second, larger batches win.
  • The learning rate matters most; search it on a log scale; the best rate sat just below divergence.
  • Along a direction of curvature λ, gradient descent converges only for lr < 2/λ (0.095 converged, 0.105 diverged).
  • Ill-conditioning, steep and flat directions together, makes one learning rate serve both badly.
