Residual Connections & Very Deep Networks

With good initialisation (Chapter 9) and normalisation (Chapter 14), deeper networks should be at least as good as shallower ones: a deep network could always copy the shallow one and make its extra layers do nothing. In 2015 He et al. showed that in practice they were worse, and not because of overfitting: deeper plain networks had higher training error. Their fix was one line per block, y = x + f(x): give every block a path that skips it. Residual connections are why networks of 50, 100 and 1,000 layers train, and every transformer is built on them.

The effect is measured here on CIFAR-10 with the same architecture built two ways, with and without the skip, at 8, 20 and 56 layers. At 8 layers the two were indistinguishable (89.0% training accuracy each). At 20 layers the residual version pulled ahead (92.5% against 89.3%). At 56 layers the plain network collapsed to 54.4% training accuracy, worse on the data it was trained on than the 8-layer one, while the residual network reached 93.3% and the best test accuracy of the six, 89.0%. At initialisation the plain 56-layer network’s first-layer gradient was 238, against 5.7 with skips.

The degradation problem

Overfitting means low training error and high test error. Degradation is different: the deeper network’s training error is higher. That cannot be a capacity problem, since the deeper network contains the shallower one as a special case (set the extra layers to identity maps). It is an optimisation problem: gradient descent cannot find that solution, or anything as good, in a very deep plain stack. Measured with the CIFAR ResNet layout (three stages of 16, 32 and 64 channels, 6n + 2 layers), identical training for every run:

The plain network got no better from 8 to 20 layers and much worse at 56, where its training loss after 15 epochs was 1.30 against 0.23 for the residual network. The residual version improved steadily with depth on both training and test data. Note what the plain 56-layer network’s gradient looked like at initialisation: not vanishing but exploding, 42 times the residual network’s. With BatchNorm in every layer, deep plain stacks are known to develop exploding gradients at initialisation (Yang et al., 2019); either way, the early layers of the plain network receive gradients that bear little relation to useful updates.

Identity shortcuts

A residual block computes y = x + f(x), where f is a small stack of layers, here two 3 × 3 convolutions with BatchNorm. If the best thing for the block to do is nothing, f only has to learn to output zero, which is easy, rather than to reproduce its input through two convolutions and two non-linearities, which is hard. When the block changes the shape (a stride-2 stage boundary or more channels), the shortcut needs a projection, a 1 × 1 convolution or, as in this chapter’s network, a separate downsampling layer between stages.

class Block(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, c, residual=True):
        super().__init__(); self.res = residual
        self.f = nn.Sequential(nn.Conv2d(c, c, 3, padding=1, bias=False), nn.BatchNorm2d(c), nn.ReLU(),
                               nn.Conv2d(c, c, 3, padding=1, bias=False), nn.BatchNorm2d(c))
    def forward(self, x):                          # x (B, c, H, W)
        return F.relu(x + self.f(x)) if self.res else F.relu(self.f(x))   # the only difference is "x +"
# shape change (stride 2, more channels): shortcut = nn.Conv2d(c_in, c_out, 1, stride=2) on x

Gradient flow with and without skips

The backward pass makes the reason concrete. For y = x + f(x), ∂y/∂x = I + ∂f/∂x. Through L blocks, the gradient reaching the input is a product of L such factors, which expands into a sum over every path through the network, including the path that skips every block and contributes the identity. Even if every ∂f/∂x is badly behaved, that direct path carries the output’s gradient to the first layer unchanged. A plain stack has only the product of the ∂f/∂x terms, and a product of 56 Jacobians can shrink or blow up exponentially. Residual networks behave, as Veit et al. (2016) put it, like ensembles of many relatively shallow paths.

Pre-norm vs post-norm

Where the normalisation sits relative to the addition matters. The original ResNet and the original transformer are post-norm: normalise after adding, y = Norm(x + f(x)), which puts a normalisation on the main path itself. Pre-norm normalises only the branch’s input, y = x + f(Norm(x)), which leaves the identity path completely clean from input to output. Pre-norm is markedly easier to train deep, and is what GPT-2 onwards and most modern transformers use; post-norm can reach slightly better results when it trains, but needs careful warm-up and learning rates. Chapter 12 measured it on a 6-layer transformer: at Adam 10⁻³ the post-norm model never left chance (50.1%), with or without warm-up, while the pre-norm model reached 99.4%.

Depth measured

With residual connections, depth paid on both training and test accuracy here, from 86.0% test at 8 layers to 89.0% at 56, while parameters grew 7.4 times and training time 5 times (16 to 80 seconds for 15 epochs). The returns diminish (20 to 56 layers bought 0.6 points), and the same budget could have gone into width instead; Chapter 36 looks at scaling width and depth together. But without the skips, depth beyond 20 layers was not a trade-off at all: it was a broken model.

🛰️
Use case: the segmentation network that would not go deeper

A team segmenting satellite images grows its encoder from 10 to 30 convolutional layers to see finer structure and finds the training loss gets worse, not better. They suspect a learning-rate problem and spend a week tuning it. The actual issue is the one on this page: a plain stack past 20 layers degrades. Adding identity shortcuts around each pair of convolutions (and 1 × 1 projections at the downsampling points) makes the 30-layer encoder train to a lower loss than the 10-layer one in the first run. Since then their architecture template has residual blocks by default, even for shallow models.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Reading degradation as overfitting: the 56-layer plain network’s training accuracy was 54.4%.
  • Deep plain stacks: past about 20 layers they degraded here; add residuals.
  • Shape mismatch on the shortcut: when channels or resolution change, project x (1 × 1 convolution) before adding.
  • Post-norm in a deep transformer without care: it never learned at Adam 1e-3 here; use pre-norm and a final norm.
  • A ReLU or norm on the identity path in a pre-norm design: keep the main path clean.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What problem did residual connections solve?

How a strong answer sounds
The degradation problem: deeper plain networks had higher training error than shallower ones, which is an optimisation failure, not overfitting, because a deeper network could in principle copy the shallow one. A residual block outputs x + f(x), so doing nothing only requires f to output zero, and in backprop the identity term gives every layer a direct path to the loss gradient. I reproduced it on CIFAR-10: a 56-layer plain CNN reached 54% training accuracy while the same network with skips reached 93%, and the plain one’s first-layer gradient at initialisation was 42 times larger, so its early layers were getting unhelpful updates.

Deeper: Why do modern transformers use pre-norm rather than post-norm?

How a strong answer sounds
Post-norm normalises after the residual addition, so the main path passes through a normalisation in every block and the gradient to early layers depends on all of them; deep post-norm stacks need careful warm-up and modest learning rates to train. Pre-norm normalises only each branch’s input, leaving a clean identity path from the embeddings to the output, so gradients reach every layer directly and training is stable at depth and at higher learning rates; it needs a final norm before the output head. In a 6-layer experiment of mine, post-norm at Adam 10⁻³ stayed at chance with or without warm-up, while pre-norm trained to 99.4%.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Degradation: deep plain networks train worse, not just generalise worse (56 layers: 54.4% training accuracy).
  • A residual block outputs x + f(x); doing nothing only needs f = 0.
  • In backprop the identity term gives every layer a direct path to the gradient; the plain 56-layer net’s first-layer gradient was 42× larger at init.
  • With skips, depth helped: 86.0% → 89.0% test from 8 to 56 layers.
  • Pre-norm keeps the identity path clean and trains stably; post-norm needs care.
