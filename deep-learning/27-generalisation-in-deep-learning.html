Generalisation: Memorisation, Double Descent & Grokking

Classical learning theory says a model generalises when its capacity is limited relative to its data: enough capacity to fit the signal, not enough to memorise the noise. Deep networks break that story. They have far more parameters than training examples, they can memorise anything, and yet on real data they generalise well. Three experiments, each small enough to reproduce here, made the puzzle concrete, and each changes how you should read a training curve.

A CIFAR-10 CNN with 0.56 million parameters fitted 10,000 images with random labels to 100% training accuracy in 69 epochs, about twice as long as the true labels took, and scored chance on the test set. With 40% of labels corrupted, the same network still found 54.7% test accuracy on the way, before memorising the noise. In random-features regression, test error rose a hundred-thousand-fold exactly where the number of features equalled the number of training points, then fell again as features kept growing: double descent. A trained MLP showed a milder version as width grew. And a small transformer learning modular addition reached 99% training accuracy at step 400 but did not generalise until step 13,700, when test accuracy jumped from a plateau near 26% to 99.9%: grokking, which happened only with weight decay.

Fitting random labels

Zhang et al. (2017) trained standard networks on images whose labels had been replaced at random. Measured here: a 5-convolution CNN with BatchNorm, 10,000 CIFAR-10 training images, SGD with momentum at a constant learning rate, no weight decay and no augmentation, so nothing stops it memorising:

Training dataEpochs to 100% training accuracyTest accuracy
True labels3676.6%
Random labels (fixed once)699.7% (chance)
Pixels shuffled by one fixed permutation4211.9%
Gaussian noise images, true labels17–

The network fitted labels that carry no information at all, and needed only twice as long as for the real ones. Noise images were the fastest to memorise, because independent random inputs in 3,072 dimensions are easy to tell apart. This rules out the classical explanation: a model class that can fit any labelling has, by every capacity measure that ignores the data, unlimited capacity, so capacity alone cannot be why it generalises on real labels. Whatever makes it generalise lies in the interaction of the data, the architecture and the training procedure: real images have structure that gradient descent finds before it resorts to memorising individual examples.

Label noise and memorisation

The same network with a fraction of its training labels replaced at random, trained for 100 epochs. Every run reached 100% training accuracy, so every run ended by memorising its wrong labels too:

With heavy noise the curve has a clear shape: test accuracy rises while the network learns the patterns shared by the correctly labelled majority, peaks, and then falls as it memorises the wrong labels one by one. At 60% noise, stopping at the best epoch kept 49.7% where training to the end left 37.6%. Networks learn simple, shared structure first and memorise exceptions later (Arpit et al., 2017), which is why early stopping on a clean validation set is an effective defence against noisy labels, and why a validation curve that rises and then falls points at label noise as much as at overfitting.

Double descent

The classical U-curve says test error falls as a model grows, then rises as it starts fitting noise. Belkin et al. (2019) showed that if you keep going, it falls again. The clearest case needs no neural network: random-features regression. Map each of 1,000 Fashion-MNIST images through p fixed random ReLU features and fit a least-squares classifier on top. When p is less than the number of training points n there is a unique best fit; when p is greater there are infinitely many exact fits, and the minimum-norm one is chosen.

At exactly p = n, the interpolation threshold, there is precisely one way to fit every training point, the system is barely solvable, and the fit chases noise with enormous coefficients: test error was thousands of times higher than at 950 or 1,050 features, and over 100,000 times higher than at 200. Past the threshold, the extra freedom lets the minimum-norm solution spread the fit across many features with small weights, and error kept falling: the largest model, 16 times overparameterised, was the best. A ridge penalty of 0.01 cut the peak by a factor of 7,600, and λ = 1 removed it, because the peak is a failure of unregularised interpolation, not of size.

A trained network shows a gentler version. A one-hidden-layer MLP on 4,000 Fashion-MNIST images with 20% noisy labels, trained 400 epochs at each width: test accuracy rose to 65.0% at width 4, fell to 63.8% at width 16, the width at which it first fitted nearly all of the training set (96.8%), then climbed steadily to 76.7% at width 4,096, with test loss peaking at width 32. Gradient descent from small initial weights acts like the minimum-norm choice, which is one explanation for why huge, unregularised-looking networks generalise.

Grokking

Power et al. (2022) trained small transformers on algorithmic tasks such as a + b mod 97 with part of the table held out, and saw something unexpected: training accuracy reached 100% early, test accuracy stayed near chance, and then, long after any sensible early-stopping rule would have halted, test accuracy shot up to 100%. Reproduced here with a one-layer transformer and full-batch AdamW:

Training fraction of the 9,409 pairsWeight decayTraining accuracy ≥ 99% at stepTest accuracy ≥ 99% at stepFinal test accuracy
30%1.040013,70099.9%
30%01,600never (30,000 steps)26.3%, the same plateau
50%1.05004,800100%
20%1.0400never13.0%

Three conditions decided whether it happened. Weight decay: without it the network memorised the training pairs and stayed there. Data: with 50% of the table it generalised after 4,800 steps, with 30% after 13,700, and with 20% not within 30,000. And time: in the grokking run, training accuracy was perfect for 13,000 steps of apparently pointless training. During those steps the weight norm fell under weight decay, from 115 to 55, and dropped again to 37 as test accuracy jumped; without weight decay it grew to 137. The explanation that has held up (Nanda et al., 2023) is that two solutions compete: a memorising one, found quickly but with large weights, and a general algorithm (for modular addition, one built on discrete Fourier features), found slowly but with smaller weights. Weight decay keeps pushing towards the smaller one until it takes over.

What this means in practice

  • Memorisation capacity is not a warning sign. A network that can fit random labels can still generalise; judge it on held-out data, not on parameter counts.
  • Noisy labels are learned last. Early stopping on a clean validation set protected accuracy under heavy label noise; a rising-then-falling validation curve is a symptom to investigate.
  • Avoid the interpolation threshold, or regularise. Error peaks where a model only just fits its data; go clearly smaller or clearly larger, and a little weight decay or ridge removes the peak.
  • Bigger can be better past the threshold. Width 4,096 beat width 16 by 13 points on the same noisy data.
  • Training past 100% training accuracy is not always wasted, but grokking needed weight decay, enough data and patience; on ordinary tasks, stop on validation.
🏷️
Use case: a product classifier trained on crowd-sourced labels

An online marketplace trains an image classifier on 2 million product photos labelled by sellers, and an audit finds about 25% of the labels wrong. Training to convergence, validation accuracy on a small, carefully checked set rises for three epochs and then slides for the next twenty while training accuracy approaches 100%: the network is memorising sellers’ mistakes. The team stops at the validation peak, raises weight decay, and uses the model’s confident disagreements with the given labels to queue 40,000 photos for relabelling. The retrained model gains 6 points on the checked set. They also stop worrying that their model is “too big for the data”: its larger sibling, with the same early stopping, is better still.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Equating parameter count with overfitting: the widest MLP was the best one on noisy data.
  • Training to 100% on noisy labels: at 60% noise, the end of training was 12 points below the best epoch.
  • Sitting at the interpolation threshold without regularisation: test error rose a hundred-thousand-fold at p = n.
  • Taking grokking as a reason to train for ever: it needed weight decay and enough data; without decay it never came.
  • Reading a falling validation curve only as overfitting: it is also the signature of memorised label noise.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: A network can fit random labels perfectly. Why does it still generalise on real ones?

How a strong answer sounds
Because what it can represent is not the same as what training finds. A CIFAR CNN I trained fitted 10,000 images with random labels to 100% in 69 epochs and scored chance on test, so it has the capacity to memorise anything. On the true labels it fitted in 36 epochs and scored 77%. Real images share structure, and gradient descent picks up the shared patterns first and memorises individual exceptions later; with noisy labels you can watch it happen, as test accuracy peaks and then falls while training accuracy keeps climbing. So generalisation depends on the data, the architecture and the optimiser together, and capacity bounds that ignore the data cannot explain it.

Deeper: What is double descent, and does it matter in practice?

How a strong answer sounds
Test error as a function of model size first follows the classical U, then peaks at the interpolation threshold, where the model has just enough capacity to fit the training data exactly, and then falls again as the model grows further. In random-features regression with 1,000 training points the test error at exactly 1,000 features was a hundred thousand times higher than on either side, and the largest model was the best. It matters in two ways: avoid sizing a model at the threshold, and use regularisation, because a ridge of 0.01 cut the peak 7,600-fold and λ = 1 removed it. In trained networks the effect is milder; an MLP on noisy Fashion-MNIST dipped from 65% to 64% around the width where it first fitted the data, then rose to 77%.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • A CNN fitted random labels to 100% (69 epochs) and scored chance: capacity alone does not explain generalisation.
  • Networks learn shared patterns first and memorise noise later; early stopping kept 49.7% vs 37.6% at 60% noise.
  • Double descent: error peaked 100,000-fold at p = n and fell beyond it; ridge removed the peak.
  • A trained MLP dipped near its interpolation width and then improved with size, to the widest.
  • Grokking: test accuracy jumped long after training accuracy, but only with weight decay and enough data.
