The Neuron & the Perceptron

Every network in this course is built from one small part: a neuron that takes a weighted sum of its inputs, adds a bias, and passes the result through a function. In 1958 Frank Rosenblatt’s perceptron used the simplest such function, a hard threshold, and a learning rule so simple it fits on one line: when the neuron gets an example wrong, add that example to the weights. It provably finds a separating line whenever one exists. It provably cannot learn XOR. The way out of that dead end, smooth activations and hidden layers trained by gradients, is the rest of deep learning.

This chapter runs the perceptron live and measures it. On separable 2-D data it needed on average 4 updates when the classes were far apart and 105 when they almost touched, far fewer than the classical bound of (R/γ)². On XOR its weights returned to exactly zero at the end of every one of 1,000 epochs. Replacing the step with a sigmoid made gradients exist, and two sigmoid hidden units solved XOR in 15 of 20 random starts. On Fashion-MNIST a perceptron’s accuracy jumped between 77% and 81% from epoch to epoch; averaging its weights gave 84.5%, the same as logistic regression.

A neuron as a weighted sum

A neuron computes z = w · x + b: one weight per input, summed, plus a bias. Then an activation φ turns z into its output, a = φ(z). Geometrically, w · x + b = 0 is a line in 2-D, a plane in 3-D, a hyperplane in general; w points perpendicular to it and b shifts it away from the origin. The neuron answers “which side of the hyperplane is x on, and how far?”

def neuron(x, w, b, act):        # x (d,), w (d,), b scalar
    z = x @ w + b                    # the pre-activation: a signed distance, scaled by |w|
    return act(z)
step    = lambda z: (z > 0).float()         # the perceptron: 0 or 1
sigmoid = lambda z: 1 / (1 + torch.exp(-z))   # logistic regression: a probability
relu    = lambda z: z.clamp_min(0)            # the modern default (Chapter 6)

With a step activation, one neuron is a linear classifier. With a sigmoid and a cross-entropy loss it is exactly logistic regression. The rest of the course stacks these units into layers (Chapter 5) and chooses better activations (Chapter 6), but the unit itself never changes.

The perceptron rule

Labels are ±1. Go through the examples; whenever the neuron predicts the wrong side, nudge the hyperplane towards the example:

w = torch.zeros(d + 1)                     # weights plus bias (x gets a constant 1 appended)
for epoch in range(max_epochs):
    mistakes = 0
    for x, y in data:                        # y is +1 or -1
        if y * (w @ x) <= 0:                  # wrong side (or on the line)
            w += y * x; mistakes += 1          # the whole learning rule
    if mistakes == 0: break                # every example correct: converged

Adding y·x moves w towards x when x is positive and away when it is negative, so the score y(w · x) for that example rises by |x|². The perceptron convergence theorem (Novikoff, 1962) says that if some hyperplane separates the classes with a gap of γ on each side, and every input has length at most R, the rule makes at most (R/γ)² updates and then stops for good. Measured on 200 random points in the square with a guaranteed gap around the true line:

Linear separability and the XOR problem

The convergence guarantee needs a separating hyperplane to exist. For XOR, output 1 when exactly one input is 1, none does: (0, 1) and (1, 0) must be on one side, (0, 0) and (1, 1) on the other, and any line that puts both positives on one side puts at least one negative there too. The best a single line can do is three out of four. Run the perceptron anyway and something tidy happens. Starting from w = 0, it gets every one of the four points wrong in the first epoch, and the four updates add up to y₁x₁ + y₂x₂ + y₃x₃ + y₄x₄ = 0, exactly. So it ends each epoch back at w = 0 and repeats: measured over 1,000 epochs, 4,000 updates and no progress at all. In 1969 Minsky and Papert’s book Perceptrons made limits like this famous, and funding for neural networks dried up for over a decade.

From step to smooth activations

Why not just train a two-layer perceptron with gradient descent? Because the step function’s derivative is zero everywhere except at 0, where it does not exist. A tiny change to a weight either changes nothing or flips an output, so the gradient carries no information about which way to move. Autograd confirms it: (z > 0).float() does not even have a gradient; it has requires_grad=False. The sigmoid is a smoothed step whose derivative σ(z)(1 − σ(z)) is positive everywhere (0.25 at z = 0, 0.018 at z = ±4), so every weight receives a signal proportional to how much it would help.

A single sigmoid neuron on XOR is still only a line: trained with gradient descent it settles on the least bad answer, probability 0.5 for all four points and a loss of log 2 = 0.693. Add a hidden layer of two sigmoid units and gradient descent finds XOR. In seed 0, hidden unit 1 learned roughly OR (7.5x₁ + 7.5x₂ − 3.4), hidden unit 2 roughly NAND (−6.8x₁ − 6.8x₂ + 10.2), and the output unit fires only when both are on: OR and NAND, which is XOR. It did not always work: 5 of 20 random starts got stuck at a loss of 0.348, classifying three points correctly, a local minimum. Wider hidden layers make that much rarer, which is one reason networks are built wider than strictly necessary (Chapter 27).

A perceptron on real data

For ten classes, keep one weight vector per class, predict the class with the largest score, and on a mistake add the input to the true class’s weights and subtract it from the predicted class’s. On Fashion-MNIST the plain multi-class perceptron scored 79.2%, 76.9%, 79.2%, 77.0% and 81.4% after its five epochs: the data are not separable, so the weights never settle, and the final answer depends on which examples happened to come last. The averaged perceptron, which predicts with the mean of the weights over every step, removed the jitter and reached 84.0% after one epoch and 84.5% after five, the same as logistic regression (84.0%). One hidden layer of 256 ReLU units trained with Adam reached 88.4%: the first of many points in this course where a hidden layer buys accuracy a linear model cannot reach.

📨
Use case: the averaged perceptron still in production

Online learning systems that must update on every example, with almost no compute, still use perceptron-style linear models: a mail server tagging messages with millions of sparse token features, or an ad system updating click weights continuously. Each mistake costs one sparse vector addition, and averaging the weights, kept cheaply as a running sum, gives a stable model that does not depend on the last few messages seen. When the decision needs feature interactions the linear model cannot express, the same teams add hashed feature crosses or move to a small neural network, the step this chapter ends on.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Expecting one neuron to learn a non-linear rule: XOR is impossible for it, however long it trains.
  • Using the last perceptron weights on noisy data: they jumped 77–81% between epochs; average them (84.5%).
  • Trying to train step activations with gradients: the gradient is zero almost everywhere.
  • Forgetting the bias: without it every hyperplane passes through the origin.
  • Assuming gradient descent always finds the answer: two hidden units stuck in a local minimum in 5 of 20 starts.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Why can’t a single perceptron learn XOR?

How a strong answer sounds
A single perceptron is one hyperplane: it outputs 1 on one side of the line w · x + b = 0 and 0 on the other. XOR needs (0, 1) and (1, 0) on one side and (0, 0) and (1, 1) on the other, and no straight line does that, so it is not linearly separable; the best one line can do is three of four. The perceptron rule never converges on it. Starting from zero weights it even cycles exactly: it gets all four points wrong, the four updates sum to zero, and it is back where it started every epoch. The fix is a hidden layer: two hidden units can learn OR and NAND, and an output unit that ANDs them computes XOR, but training that needs smooth activations and backpropagation.

Deeper: What does the perceptron convergence theorem guarantee, and what does it not?

How a strong answer sounds
If the data are linearly separable with margin γ and all inputs have norm at most R, the perceptron makes at most (R/γ)² mistakes and then classifies the training set perfectly. It does not say the line it finds generalises well, since it stops at the first separator, often close to some points, which is what a maximum-margin method fixes. It says nothing if the data are not separable; then the weights keep changing and the last ones depend on the order of examples, which is why averaging helps. In practice the bound is loose: with a gap of 0.01 I measured about 105 updates against a bound of nearly 29,000.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • A neuron is a weighted sum plus a bias, passed through an activation; geometrically, a hyperplane.
  • The perceptron rule adds misclassified examples to the weights and converges whenever the data are separable (4 to 105 updates here).
  • XOR is not linearly separable; the perceptron’s weights returned to exactly zero every epoch.
  • Step functions have no useful gradient; sigmoids do, which makes gradient-based training possible.
  • Two hidden units solved XOR (OR and NAND, combined), in 15 of 20 starts; an averaged perceptron matched logistic regression on Fashion-MNIST.
