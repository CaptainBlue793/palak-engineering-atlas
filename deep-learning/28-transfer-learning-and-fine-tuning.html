Transfer Learning & Fine-Tuning

Almost nobody trains a vision or language model from random weights any more. A network pretrained on a large, varied dataset has learned features that are useful far beyond it, and a new task usually starts from those weights. The Computer Vision course shows that this works and how much data it saves. This chapter opens up how it works: which layers change during fine-tuning, how the learning rate decides whether the pretrained knowledge survives, and what happens to the features the new task does not use.

Measured with an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18 on CIFAR-10 at 96 pixels. With 10,000 labelled images, fine-tuning reached 91.7% against 77.1% from scratch, but at a learning rate ten times too high it kept only 79.9%, and with 1,000 images a too-high rate fell to 45%, below training from scratch. During fine-tuning the first layers barely changed (their features stayed 99% similar to the pretrained ones by linear CKA) while the last stage’s features changed the most, to 52% similarity, even though its weights moved the least. Layer-wise learning-rate decay added half a point; linear probing before fine-tuning, a recommended refinement, did worse here. And fine-tuning on half the classes did not erase the features needed for the other half, unless the learning rate was large.

  • Explain why pretrained features transfer and which layers carry the general ones
  • Choose between a linear probe and full fine-tuning, and pick a fine-tuning learning rate
  • Measure how much each layer changes, in weights and in representations
  • Apply layer-wise learning rates and linear-probe-then-fine-tune, and judge them
  • Recognise when fine-tuning destroys general features and when transfer helps less

Why transfer works

A network trained on ImageNet’s 1.28 million photos of 1,000 classes has to build features for edges, textures, parts and objects that work across all of them. Most of those are useful for any natural photograph, which is why the representation is worth reusing. The same experiment at three scales, CIFAR-10 images upsampled to 96 × 96, 1,500 SGD steps of 128 images, each learning rate swept:

Labelled CIFAR-10 imagesFrom scratch (best lr)Linear probe on frozen featuresFull fine-tuning (best lr)
1,00051.7%75.8%84.0%
10,00077.1%81.8%91.7%

With 1,000 images, the frozen features alone, read by a linear classifier, beat a network trained from scratch by 24 points; fine-tuning everything added 8 more. The Computer Vision course measures the same pattern at higher resolution and with more data sizes; the rest of this chapter is about what happens inside the network while it fine-tunes.

Feature extraction vs fine-tuning, and the learning rate

A linear probe keeps the pretrained features exactly and learns only the head; fine-tuning lets every weight move. How far they move is set by the learning rate, and it decides whether fine-tuning builds on the pretrained solution or abandons it:

Fine-tuning learning rate1,000 images10,000 images
0.00183.1%89.9%
0.00383.7%91.5%
0.0184.0%91.7%

From scratch, best rate (0.1)51.7%77.1%

The best fine-tuning rate, 0.01, was ten times smaller than the best rate for training from scratch. At the from-scratch rate of 0.1, fine-tuning with 1,000 images scored 45%, below training from scratch: the first large updates, driven by a randomly initialised head, wrecked the pretrained features before the head had learned anything useful, and with so little data the network could not rebuild them. With 10,000 images it recovered more of the loss. The pretrained weights are only an advantage if the learning rate lets the network stay near them.

Which layers change

Two ways to see what fine-tuning did to each stage of the network: how far its weights moved, relative to their size, and how similar its outputs remain to the pretrained network’s, measured by linear centred kernel alignment (CKA, Kornblith et al., 2019), where 1 means the same representation up to rotation and scale. At the best learning rate, 10,000 images:

StageRelative weight changeCKA to the pretrained features
Stem (first convolution)6.6%0.99
Layer 110.7%0.94
Layer 212.4%0.94
Layer 312.3%0.84
Layer 4 (last stage)8.0%0.52

The early layers’ representations were almost untouched: edges and textures are what any photograph needs, and the new task had no reason to change them. The last stage changed most in what it computed, to about half its pretrained representation, even though its weights moved the least in relative terms, because small changes in many layers compound: layer 4 sees inputs that layers 1 to 3 have already shifted, and its filters were ImageNet-specific to begin with. At the too-high learning rate of 0.1 the weights moved by 40–70% everywhere and even the stem’s CKA fell to 0.89: the network was being retrained, not fine-tuned.

Layer-wise learning rates

If the early layers should change least, give them smaller steps. Layer-wise learning-rate decay (Howard and Ruder, 2018, as “discriminative fine-tuning”) gives the head the full rate and each earlier stage the rate of the stage after it times a factor. The head’s rate was doubled for the decayed runs so the late layers kept a similar step:

Decay per stage1,000 images10,000 images
1.0 (one rate for all)83.95%91.71%
0.583.68%92.18%
0.381.96%90.74%
0.178.86%86.87%

A mild decay of 0.5 added half a point with 10,000 images and cost a quarter of a point with 1,000; strong decay hurt both, because the early stages then barely moved at all and the network could not adapt its middle layers to 96-pixel upsampled images. On this pair of datasets, close in kind, one well-chosen rate for the whole network was nearly as good. Layer-wise decay earns its place when source and target differ more, and in large language models, where it is standard.

Linear probe, then fine-tune

Kumar et al. (2022) argued that fine-tuning with a randomly initialised head distorts the pretrained features, because the early gradients through a bad head are large and arbitrary, and proposed LP-FT: first train only the head (a linear probe), then fine-tune everything from that good starting head. Measured with the probe trained for 500 steps, then the same fine-tuning as above:

Method1,000 images10,000 imagesLayer-4 CKA to pretrained (10,000)
Probe only74.6%80.1%1.0 (frozen)
Fine-tune from a random head84.0%91.7%0.52
LP-FT73.6%89.1%0.09

LP-FT did worse here, on both sizes, and by the CKA measure it changed layer 4 more, the opposite of its purpose. This contradicts the published result, and the honest reading is that it depends on details: Kumar et al.’s gains were largest on out-of-distribution test sets, which this experiment did not have, and the probe here was trained at a learning rate of 0.1 with weight decay, which may have left the head with large weights that then drove strong gradients into the backbone. The general lesson stands: a refinement that helps in a paper is a hypothesis for your setting, to be checked against the plain method.

Catastrophic forgetting of general features

Fine-tuning optimises for the new task only. Does it erase the general features the network had, the ones that would serve other tasks? Fine-tune on CIFAR-10 classes 0–4 only (airplane to deer), then freeze the network and train a linear classifier on its features for classes 5–9 (dog to truck), which fine-tuning never saw:

BackboneAccuracy on classes 0–4Probe on unseen classes 5–9
Randomly initialised–60.7%
Pretrained, untouched–93.5%
Fine-tuned on 0–4, lr 0.00395.7%95.0%
Fine-tuned on 0–4, lr 0.0196.3%93.6%
Fine-tuned on 0–4, lr 0.176.7%73.5%
LP-FT on 0–4, lr 0.0195.4%91.1%

At sensible learning rates, fine-tuning on five CIFAR classes did not erase what the other five needed: a gentle fine-tune even improved the features for the unseen classes (95.0% against 93.5%), because they share the new domain’s low resolution and statistics. At the large rate the general features were destroyed along with the pretrained advantage, and the probe on unseen classes fell 20 points. Forgetting during fine-tuning is mostly a learning-rate problem; Chapter 33 deals with the harder case of learning many tasks in sequence.

When transfer hurts, or helps less

Transfer is weakest when the source and target differ in kind. Fashion-MNIST, grey 28-pixel product silhouettes, is far from ImageNet’s colour photographs:

Fashion-MNIST labelled imagesFrom scratchProbe on ImageNet featuresFine-tuned ImageNet
1,00083.9%79.3%85.7%
10,00090.3%83.8%92.4%

Frozen ImageNet features were worse than training from scratch here, the opposite of CIFAR-10, because the last-stage features describe photographs, not silhouettes. Fine-tuning still won by about 2 points, since the early features and the initialisation remain useful. Combined with Chapter 20’s case, where features from a narrow source task did worse than raw pixels, the rule is: the further the target from the source, the less you can freeze, and the more the advantage comes from a good starting point rather than from the features themselves.

Use case: one backbone, several hospital tasks

A medical-imaging group fine-tunes one pretrained chest X-ray backbone for pneumonia detection, and later wants to reuse the same fine-tuned backbone, frozen, for a fracture-detection probe to save compute. The first fine-tune used the learning rate from their from-scratch recipe; the fracture probe on its features scores 9 points below a probe on the original backbone. Refitting at a tenth of the rate keeps the pneumonia accuracy within half a point and brings the fracture probe level with the original. They now keep the pretrained backbone as the shared asset, fine-tune per task at a low rate, and check one probe from another task as a guard against erasing general features.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Fine-tuning at a from-scratch learning rate: with 1,000 images it fell to 45%, below training from scratch.
  • Judging layer change by weights alone: layer 4 moved least in weights and most in features.
  • Adopting LP-FT or layer-wise decay without testing: here LP-FT lost 2.6 points; strong decay lost 5.
  • Freezing for a distant domain: on Fashion-MNIST the frozen features lost to training from scratch.
  • Assuming fine-tuning erases general features: at sensible rates it did not; at 0.1 it cost 20 points.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How do you choose the learning rate for fine-tuning a pretrained model?

How a strong answer sounds
Much lower than for training from scratch, typically about ten times, and I sweep it. The pretrained weights are the asset, and a large rate destroys them before the new head is useful. Fine-tuning an ImageNet ResNet-18 on 1,000 CIFAR-10 images, the best rate was 0.01, giving 84%; at the from-scratch rate of 0.1 it scored 45%, worse than training from scratch at 52%. With more data the damage is smaller, 80% against 92% at 10,000 images, but still large. A short warm-up helps for the same reason.

Deeper: What happens inside a network during fine-tuning?

How a strong answer sounds
The early layers barely change and the late ones change most. Measuring linear CKA between each stage’s pretrained and fine-tuned outputs, the first convolution stayed at 0.99 and the last stage fell to 0.52, even though the last stage’s weights moved the least in relative terms, because changes compound through the network and its features are the most specific to the source task. That is the argument for smaller learning rates on early layers: a decay of 0.5 per stage added half a point with 10,000 images. And the general features survive sensible fine-tuning: a probe for classes the fine-tuning never saw scored as well afterwards as before, unless the learning rate was too high.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Pretrained features beat training from scratch by 24 points at 1,000 images as a frozen probe; fine-tuning added 8 more.
  • The fine-tuning rate was best at a tenth of the from-scratch rate; at the from-scratch rate it fell below training from scratch.
  • Early layers stayed 99% similar; the last stage changed most in features while moving least in weights.
  • Layer-wise decay added half a point; LP-FT hurt here. Test refinements against plain fine-tuning.
  • Sensible fine-tuning kept general features; for a distant domain frozen features lost, fine-tuning still won.
