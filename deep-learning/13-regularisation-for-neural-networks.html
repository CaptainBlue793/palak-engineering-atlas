Regularisation: Weight Decay, Dropout & Early Stopping

A network with far more parameters than training examples can memorise its training set, and given enough epochs it will: training accuracy 100%, training loss near zero. Regularisation is everything that pushes it towards solutions that generalise instead: shrinking the weights (weight decay), randomly removing units during training (dropout), stopping before memorisation completes (early stopping), and the strongest regulariser of all, more data. This chapter measures each on a deliberately over-sized network and reports what they actually changed, which is not quite what the folklore says.

The setup: a 784-1024-1024-10 MLP with 1.86 million parameters, trained for 100 epochs on only 5,000 Fashion-MNIST images, so it reached 100% training accuracy. Without regularisation it scored 85.5% on the test set. With L2 penalties, AdamW weight decay, dropout from 0.2 to 0.8, or combinations, the final test accuracy ranged from 83.8% to 85.5%: none helped by a measurable amount. What they did change was confidence. Validation loss at the end fell from 1.26 to as low as 0.49, because the regularised networks stopped making their mistakes with near-certainty. Early stopping on validation loss stopped at epoch 5, five points short. And training on 50,000 images instead of 5,000 added three points that no penalty could.

  • Implement L2 regularisation and decoupled weight decay, and know why they differ under Adam
  • Use dropout correctly in training and evaluation
  • Apply early stopping on the metric that matters
  • Tell what a regulariser changed: accuracy, confidence, or weight size
  • Rank data against penalties as a regulariser

Weight decay and L2

An L2 penalty adds ½λ‖w‖² to the loss, whose gradient λw pulls every weight towards zero in proportion to its size. With plain SGD that is the same as weight decay, multiplying the weights by (1 − lr·λ) every step. Smaller weights mean a smoother function that cannot fit every quirk of the training set. Measured: the unregularised network’s weights had a total norm of 53.0; L2 at λ = 10⁻³ shrank it to 13.4, AdamW’s weight decay of 1.0 to 21.5.

loss = F.cross_entropy(model(xb), yb)
loss = loss + 0.5 * lam * sum((p ** 2).sum() for n, p in model.named_parameters() if n.endswith("weight"))   # L2 in the loss
opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3)                       # L2 goes through Adam's scaling
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3, weight_decay=0.1)   # decoupled: w -= lr * wd * w, outside the scaling
# biases and normalisation weights are usually excluded from decay: pass two parameter groups

AdamW: decoupled decay

Under Adam the two are no longer the same. An L2 term’s gradient λw is added to the loss gradient and then divided, like everything else, by Adam’s per-parameter √v̂ (Chapter 11). Weights whose gradients are large, which are the ones most likely to be fitting noise, get the least decay. AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019) applies the decay directly to the weights, outside the adaptive scaling, so every weight shrinks at the same relative rate. That is why AdamW is the default optimiser for transformers and why weight-decay values for AdamW (0.01 to 0.1, sometimes 1.0) look nothing like L2 coefficients (10⁻⁵ to 10⁻³). On this small problem both families behaved alike once their strength was tuned: the best of each was within a point of no regularisation, and the strongest of each (λ = 10⁻³ L2, wd = 1.0) cost about one point of final accuracy while halving the final validation loss.

Dropout

Dropout (Srivastava et al., 2014) sets each unit’s output to zero with probability p during training and scales the survivors by 1/(1 − p), so the expected activation is unchanged; at evaluation it does nothing. No unit can rely on any particular other unit being present, which discourages fragile co-adaptations, and the trained network behaves like an average over the exponentially many sub-networks it sampled. Two practical points. It must be switched off for evaluation, which is what model.eval() does (P4). And it slows training: with p = 0.8 the network had still not memorised the training set after 100 epochs (90.9%).

h = F.dropout(h, p=0.5, training=self.training)      # training: zero half, scale the rest by 2
model.train()   # dropout on
model.eval()    # dropout off: deterministic, full network

Early stopping

Stop training when validation loss stops improving, and keep the best checkpoint. It is the cheapest regulariser and almost always used. Here it would have been a mistake. Without regularisation the validation loss was lowest at epoch 5 (0.53), where the test accuracy was 80.0%; by epoch 100 the loss had risen to 1.26 and the test accuracy to 85.5%. The same thing happened in Chapter 10: as training continues, the network gets more examples right while growing more confident on the ones it gets wrong, and the loss only sees the second. Early stopping works when you stop on the metric you care about; on validation accuracy it would have kept going here.

Data as a regulariser

The same network with no regularisation at all, trained on more of the data: 1,000 images gave 81.2% test accuracy, 5,000 gave 85.5%, 20,000 gave 87.0%, and 50,000 gave 88.5%. Going from 5,000 to 50,000 images was worth three points; the best penalty on 5,000 images was worth 0.04. With 50,000 images the network no longer memorised everything (93.2% training accuracy after 10 epochs), which is what regularisation is trying to achieve by other means. When more data exists, collect or augment it before tuning penalties.

Why did the penalties not help accuracy here, when they clearly do in many papers? Partly because the network’s errors on Fashion-MNIST are mostly the genuinely ambiguous garments of Chapter 10, which no amount of smoothing fixes; partly because a single seed with 10,000 test images has a noise floor of about ±0.3 points; and partly because modern recipes rarely use penalties alone: weight decay matters most in long training runs with large models and data, where it also stabilises optimisation (Chapter 27 returns to why heavily over-parameterised networks generalise anyway). What the penalties reliably did was calibrate: they halved the final validation loss, which matters whenever the probabilities are used.

Use case: a triage model with honest probabilities

A hospital’s triage classifier flags scans for urgent review when the predicted probability of a finding exceeds 0.3. Trained without regularisation, it scores well on accuracy but its mistakes are made at 0.99 confidence, so the threshold catches few of them. Adding dropout and AdamW weight decay leaves accuracy unchanged, as on this page, but brings the validation loss down sharply: the wrong predictions now sit near 0.5 instead of 0.99, and the 0.3 threshold flags far more of the cases radiologists would have wanted to see. The improvement shows up in the hospital’s recall-at-threshold metric, not in accuracy.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • L2 in the loss with Adam, expecting weight decay: use AdamW, whose decay is not rescaled per parameter.
  • Decaying biases and normalisation weights: exclude them with parameter groups.
  • Early stopping on validation loss when you report accuracy: it stopped at epoch 5 (80.0%) instead of 85.5%.
  • Leaving dropout on at evaluation, or forgetting it slows convergence.
  • Judging a regulariser by accuracy alone: here it changed confidence, not accuracy.
  • Tuning penalties before trying more data: 10× data gave +3 points; no penalty gave more than +0.04.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is the difference between L2 regularisation and weight decay, and why does it matter for Adam?

How a strong answer sounds
L2 regularisation adds ½λ‖w‖² to the loss, so its gradient λw joins the loss gradient. Weight decay shrinks the weights directly, w ← w − lr·λ·w, each step. With plain SGD the two are identical. With Adam they are not: the L2 gradient is divided by Adam’s per-parameter scale like every other gradient, so parameters with large gradients get relatively little decay. AdamW decouples the decay from the adaptive step so every weight shrinks at the same relative rate, which is why it is the standard for transformers. In my small experiment, once each was tuned, both shrank the weights and lowered validation loss by similar amounts.

Deeper: You add dropout and weight decay and test accuracy does not change. Did they do anything?

How a strong answer sounds
Look beyond accuracy. Check the validation loss or a calibration metric, the weight norms, and the gap between training and validation accuracy. In a measured case on a 1.86-million-parameter MLP trained on 5,000 images, no regulariser moved test accuracy by more than about a point and a half, but the final validation loss fell from 1.26 to about 0.5, because the network stopped being near-certain about its mistakes, and the weight norm fell by up to four times. If the model’s probabilities feed a threshold or another system, that is a real improvement. If only accuracy matters, more data or a better architecture is the lever: ten times more data added three points.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • L2 and weight decay are the same for SGD and different for Adam; use AdamW, and exclude biases and norms.
  • Dropout zeroes random units in training only; it slows convergence and must be off at evaluation.
  • On a memorising MLP, no penalty moved test accuracy by more than ~1.5 points; all of them cut over-confidence (validation loss 1.26 → 0.49).
  • Early stopping must use the reported metric: validation loss stopped 5 points short.
  • Data is the strongest regulariser: 5,000 → 50,000 images, +3 points.
