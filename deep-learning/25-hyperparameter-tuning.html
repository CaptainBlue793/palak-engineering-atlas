Hyperparameter Tuning for Neural Networks

Every training run depends on choices made before it starts: the learning rate, the batch size, the amount of regularisation, the width. Tuning them is a search in which each evaluation costs a full training run, so the question is not only which method finds the best settings but which finds good ones for the least compute, and how far to trust the winner it reports.

This chapter answers with measurements on a Fashion-MNIST MLP with five hyperparameters, starting from 300 random runs trained to the end. One hyperparameter dominated: the learning rate explained 30% of the variation in validation accuracy, and none of the other four more than 2.4%. 17 of the 300 runs diverged or failed to learn. Bayesian optimisation reached more in 20 trials than random search did in 100. Successive halving, which stops weak runs after one epoch, found better settings than random search with the same compute, and it could afford to because a run’s rank after one epoch predicted its final rank with a correlation of 0.97. And the search’s winner was not the best configuration: retrained with 5 seeds, the runner-up beat it every time.

  • Know which hyperparameters to tune first, and on what scale
  • Compare grid search, random search and Bayesian optimisation at the same budget
  • Use successive halving and Hyperband to stop bad runs early, and know when that is safe
  • Correct for the optimism of picking the best of many noisy results
  • Plan a tuning budget for a real project

What to tune first

The search space: learning rate (10⁻⁵ to 10⁻¹, log scale), weight decay (10⁻⁶ to 10⁻¹, log), dropout (0 to 0.6), batch size (32 to 1,024) and width (64 to 1,024 units in each of two hidden layers), with AdamW, a cosine schedule and 9 epochs per run. 300 configurations were drawn uniformly at random and every one trained to the end, scored on a 10,000-image validation split carved from the training data. Splitting each hyperparameter’s range into five bins and asking how much of the spread in validation accuracy the bin explains:

HyperparameterShare of the variance explained on its own
Learning rate29.7%
Dropout2.4%
Weight decay1.8%
Width1.1%
Batch size1.0%

The learning rate mattered more than the other four together, by a factor of five. The rest of the variance came from interactions between hyperparameters and from noise. This is the usual picture (Bergstra and Bengio, 2012, found the same on many problems): a few hyperparameters matter, which ones depends on the problem, and the learning rate is nearly always among them. The practical order is to tune the learning rate first, on a log scale, then the one or two others with the biggest effect for your model, and leave the rest at sensible defaults. 17 of the 300 runs, all at learning rates near the top of the range, ended below 20%.

Grid, random and Bayesian search

Three ways to spend the same budget. A grid tries every combination of a few values per hyperparameter. Random search draws each configuration independently. Bayesian optimisation fits a model (here a Gaussian process) to the results so far and picks the next configuration where the expected improvement is largest. Random search was scored by resampling the 300-run pool many times, which gives its expected result at each budget:

One random configuration is a gamble: 79% on average, with one draw in ten at 65% or below. Five random trials already reached 88.4%, after which returns diminished quickly. Bayesian optimisation, starting from 8 random points, was ahead of random search at every budget and reached in 20 trials more than random search reached in 100. A typical hand-built grid, three learning rates by three weight decays by three dropouts with batch size and width fixed, 27 runs, found 89.97% validation and 89.41% test: as good as 27 random trials here, because one of its three learning rates, 10⁻³, happened to be a good one. The weakness of a grid is that it tests only three values of the one hyperparameter that matters, while 27 random trials test 27; on a problem whose best learning rate fell between grid points, the grid would have nothing near it.

Stopping bad runs early

Most of a random search’s compute goes on configurations that are obviously bad within the first epoch. Successive halving (Jamieson and Talwalkar, 2016) starts many configurations on a small budget, keeps the best third, gives those three times the budget, and repeats. With 27 configurations and budgets of 1, 3 and 9 epochs it spends 63 epochs, the cost of 7 full runs. Hyperband (Li et al., 2018) hedges against stopping too early by running several such brackets with different starting budgets.

Method, same computeEpochs usedBest validation (mean of 4 runs; 2 for 81 configurations)Its test accuracy
Random search, 7 full runs6388.8%88.5%
Successive halving, 27 configurations6389.5%89.2%
Hyperband, 3 brackets6989.5%89.3%
Random search, 21 full runs18989.5%89.2%
Successive halving, 81 configurations18989.6%89.2%

At a budget of 63 epochs, successive halving matched what random search needed three times the compute for. It can only work if a run’s early performance predicts its final performance, and here it did: the Spearman correlation between a configuration’s rank after 1 epoch and after 9 was 0.971. That is a property of this problem, not a law. A low learning rate that starts slowly and finishes best, or a schedule whose decay happens late, ranks badly early, and with a cosine schedule every run’s early epochs are at high learning rates. Check the correlation on a few full runs before trusting early stopping. With 189 epochs of budget both methods were already near the top of what the pool contained, and halving added nothing.

Tuning on a budget, and trusting the winner

Picking the maximum of many noisy validation scores picks the luckiest one as well as the best. In the pool, the winner’s validation accuracy overstated its test accuracy by 0.15 points for a single trial and by 0.33 points for the best of 100; the gap grows with the number of trials. More striking: the best and second-best configurations of the 300, retrained with 5 new seeds each, scored 89.40% ± 0.05 and 90.03% ± 0.12 on test. The runner-up was better on every seed. Its configuration (a lower learning rate, batch 32, width 1,024) had simply had a less lucky first run.

A practical plan follows from the measurements. Spend the first runs on a learning-rate sweep on a log scale, since it explains most of the variance. Use Bayesian optimisation or successive halving for the next few dozen runs, checking first that early ranks predict final ones. Then take the top three to five configurations, retrain each with several seeds, and choose on the mean, so the final choice is not decided by one lucky run. Report test accuracy once, for the configuration chosen that way.

🔊
Use case: tuning a speech-command model on a fixed GPU budget

A team has 200 GPU-hours to tune a keyword-spotting network, at 2 hours per full run. A grid over learning rate, weight decay, dropout, width and augmentation strength at three values each would need 243 runs, more than the budget. Instead they sweep the learning rate with 6 runs, fix it, and run Hyperband over the other four with a minimum budget of a quarter of an epoch, which spends about 40 full-run equivalents across 160 configurations. A check on 10 early full runs shows that rank after a quarter of an epoch predicts the final rank at 0.9, so the early stopping is trustworthy. They retrain the five best with three seeds, discover that the search’s winner is third on average, and ship the configuration that is best on the mean.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Tuning everything at once: one hyperparameter explained 30% of the variance and four together 6%.
  • A linear scale for the learning rate: it varies over orders of magnitude; search it in log space.
  • A coarse grid on the important axis: three learning rates is three guesses.
  • Early stopping without checking that early ranks hold: here they did (0.97); with late-decaying schedules they may not.
  • Trusting the single best run: the runner-up beat the winner on all 5 reseeded runs.
  • Reporting the best validation score: it overstated test accuracy, more so the more trials were run.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Why is random search usually better than grid search?

How a strong answer sounds
Because only a few hyperparameters matter, and a grid wastes runs on combinations of the unimportant ones while testing very few values of the important ones. In a measured search over five hyperparameters, the learning rate explained 30% of the variance and none of the others more than 2.4%. A 27-point grid tests three learning rates; 27 random trials test 27. In that case the grid happened to include a good learning rate and did as well as random search, which shows the risk is that a grid can easily miss the good region entirely. Random search also lets you stop at any budget, and Bayesian optimisation does better still: in 20 trials it beat what random search reached in 100.

Deeper: How would you tune a model when each run takes hours?

How a strong answer sounds
Spend early runs on the learning rate on a log scale, since it usually dominates. Then use a method that kills bad runs early, successive halving or Hyperband, after checking on a few full runs that early performance predicts final performance: I measured a rank correlation of 0.97 between epoch 1 and epoch 9, and halving matched random search with a third of the compute. Bayesian optimisation is the alternative when runs cannot be stopped and resumed cheaply. Finally, I retrain the top few configurations with several seeds and choose on the mean, because the best single run is optimistically biased: in one search the runner-up beat the winner on every one of five new seeds.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • The learning rate explained 30% of the variance; the other four hyperparameters 1–2.4% each. Tune it first, on a log scale.
  • Random search improves fast for a few trials, then slowly; Bayesian optimisation beat 100 random trials in 20.
  • A grid tests few values of what matters; it worked here only because it happened to hit a good learning rate.
  • Successive halving matched random search with a third of the compute, because early ranks held (ρ = 0.97).
  • The best run is optimistic; reseed the top few and choose on the mean (the runner-up won here).
