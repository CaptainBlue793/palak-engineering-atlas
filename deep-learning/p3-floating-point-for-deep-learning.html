Floating Point for Deep Learning

A GPU does not compute with real numbers. It computes with floats: a sign, an exponent and a few bits of fraction, so every result is rounded to the nearest number the format can hold. Usually the rounding is invisible. Deep learning is where it stops being invisible, because training multiplies millions of tiny numbers, sums thousands of terms, exponentiates logits, and now does most of it in 16-bit formats with only three or four significant digits.

This prerequisite explains fp32, fp16 and bf16 and measures where each one breaks. Adding 0.01 a hundred thousand times reached 1,000.7 in fp32, stalled at exactly 32 in fp16 and at 4 in bf16. A textbook softmax returned NaN for logits of 100 in fp32 and of 12 in fp16, where the log-sum-exp version did not. The textbook variance formula gave −8 for numbers whose true variance is 1. And of the 250,000 gradients in a small network, 5.7% were too small for fp16 to hold at full precision.

  • Read a float as sign, exponent and mantissa, and know each format’s range and precision
  • Predict overflow, underflow and absorption in fp32, fp16 and bf16
  • Compute softmax and cross-entropy without overflowing, using log-sum-exp
  • Recognise catastrophic cancellation and accumulate sums safely
  • Choose between fp16 and bf16 for training, and know why fp16 needs loss scaling

How floats are stored

A float stores a number as ± 1.m × 2e: one sign bit, an exponent e that sets the scale, and a mantissa m, the significant digits. The exponent bits decide the range (how large and how small a number can be); the mantissa bits decide the precision (how close two neighbouring numbers are). The gap between 1 and the next representable number is called machine epsilon; every result is rounded with a relative error of at most half of it.

FormatBits (sign / exponent / mantissa)Largest valueSmallest normalEpsilon (precision)Significant digits
fp321 / 8 / 233.4 × 10³⁸1.2 × 10⁻³⁸1.2 × 10⁻⁷about 7
fp16 (half)1 / 5 / 1065,5046.1 × 10⁻⁵9.8 × 10⁻⁴about 3
bf16 (bfloat16)1 / 8 / 73.4 × 10³⁸1.2 × 10⁻³⁸7.8 × 10⁻³about 2

The two 16-bit formats make opposite trades. fp16 keeps more mantissa and so more precision, but its 5 exponent bits give it a tiny range: nothing above 65,504, and below 6.1 × 10⁻⁵ it can only hold “subnormal” numbers with ever fewer significant bits, down to 6 × 10⁻⁸, under which everything becomes 0. bf16 keeps fp32’s 8 exponent bits, so it has fp32’s range, and pays with a mantissa of only 7 bits. It was designed for deep learning, where range problems crash training and precision problems mostly average out.

torch.tensor(2048., dtype=torch.float16) + 1      # 2048: the next fp16 number after 2048 is 2050
torch.tensor(256., dtype=torch.bfloat16) + 1      # 256: bf16 has only 8 significant bits
torch.tensor(300., dtype=torch.float16) * 300    # inf: 90,000 is above 65,504
torch.tensor(12., dtype=torch.float16).exp()      # inf; exp(11) = 59,872 still fits
torch.tensor(89.).exp()                         # inf in fp32; exp(88) = 1.65e38 fits
torch.tensor(0.1) + torch.tensor(0.2) == torch.tensor(0.3)   # True in fp32 (False in Python's float64)

The last line is a reminder that rounding is a property of the format: in float64, 0.1 + 0.2 lands one step away from 0.3; in float32 the coarser grid happens to round both to the same number. Never test floats for equality; use a tolerance (torch.allclose).

Overflow, underflow and absorption

Three things go wrong, and each has a typical place in a network.

  • Overflow: a result above the largest value becomes inf, and the next subtraction or division turns it into NaN. Exponentials are the usual culprit: in fp16, e12 already overflows. Attention scores, softmax and exp-based losses all exponentiate.
  • Underflow: a result below the smallest representable magnitude becomes 0. Gradients are the usual victim, because they are products of many small factors.
  • Absorption: adding a small number to a large one changes nothing when the small one is below half the gap between neighbouring large numbers. Accumulators are the victim: a running sum, a loss averaged over steps, a weight update much smaller than the weight.

Absorption is easy to see. Adding 0.01 to a running total 100,000 times should give 1,000:

AccumulatorResult after 100,000 additions of 0.01Why
fp321,000.665Small rounding errors add up: 0.07% off
fp1632.0, reached by step 3,000 and never leftThe gap between fp16 numbers at 32 is 0.03125; 0.01 is under half of it
bf164.0, already stuck at the first sample (step 1,000)At 4 the gap is 0.03125 too, because bf16 has only 7 mantissa bits
torch.sum of 100,000 fp16 values of 0.011,000.0It accumulates in fp32 internally, then rounds the result

The same thing happens to weights. An update is lr × gradient, often a millionth of the weight itself. In bf16, a weight of 1.0 cannot change by less than about 0.004; any smaller update is absorbed and lost. This is why mixed-precision training (Chapter 21) keeps a master copy of the weights in fp32 and only computes in 16 bits.

Log-sum-exp

Softmax computes exp(zᵢ) / Σⱼ exp(zⱼ). Written literally, it overflows as soon as any logit is large, even though the answer is perfectly ordinary: softmax only depends on the differences between logits, so [1000, 999, 998] has the same answer as [2, 1, 0]. The fix is to subtract the maximum first. Mathematically nothing changes; numerically the largest exponent is now e0 = 1:

def softmax_naive(z):  e = z.exp(); return e / e.sum()     # NaN for z = [100, 99, 98] in fp32
def softmax_stable(z):
    m = z.max()                                         # subtract the largest logit
    e = (z - m).exp()                                  # every exponent is now <= 0, so every term is in (0, 1]
    return e / e.sum()
def logsumexp(z):  m = z.max(); return m + (z - m).exp().sum().log()   # what torch.logsumexp does

Measured: the naive version returned NaN for logits [100, 99, 98] in fp32 and for [12, 11, 10] in fp16, where the first exponent overflowed to inf and inf/inf is NaN; torch.softmax returned (0.665, 0.245, 0.090) for every case, the same as for [2, 1, 0]. The same idea is why losses take logits: F.cross_entropy computes log-softmax with log-sum-exp in one step, and binary_cross_entropy_with_logits avoids taking the log of a probability that has rounded to 1. With a logit of 20, sigmoid(20) is exactly 1.0 in fp32, so −log(1 − p) for a negative example is inf; binary_cross_entropy on the probability hides it by clamping the log to −100 and reports a loss of 100 for both z = 20 and z = 40. The logits version reports the true values, 20.0 and 40.0.

Catastrophic cancellation

Subtracting two nearly equal numbers loses the digits they share. The textbook variance formula, Var(x) = E[x²] − E[x]², is a classic victim. For a million values with mean 10,000 and standard deviation 1, both terms are about 10⁸, fp32 can only hold them to about 8 units, and their difference, which should be 1, came out as −8.0: a negative variance. With mean 10⁶ it came out as exactly 0. torch.var, which subtracts the mean first and then squares, returned 0.9992, matching float64. Normalisation layers (Chapter 14) compute exactly this statistic on every batch, which is why their implementations centre first.

bf162.9 × 10⁻³1.7 × 10⁻³

Most of the 16-bit error is already there before any arithmetic, from rounding the inputs; the multiply itself accumulates in fp32 inside the tensor cores, so the error does not grow with the 4,096-term sums. bf16 is about eight times less precise than fp16, which matches its three fewer mantissa bits (2³ = 8). Networks tolerate errors of this size, which is why bf16 training works; they do not tolerate an inf.

Do real gradients fit in fp16?

Gradients span many orders of magnitude. In the small MLP measured here, after 400 training steps the median gradient element was 7.8 × 10⁻⁴ and the smallest 7 × 10⁻¹²: thirteen orders of magnitude, far wider than fp16’s range. 5.7% of the gradient elements were below fp16’s smallest normal number and would be stored as subnormals with reduced precision, and 0.03% would become exactly 0. bf16 represented all of them. For this shallow network fp16 underflow is a small effect; in deep networks, with small losses and long chains of multiplications, the left tail moves further left. The standard fix is loss scaling: multiply the loss by a large constant before backward(), which multiplies every gradient by it too, then divide it out before the optimiser step. Scaling by 1,024 moved all but 0.001% of these gradients into fp16’s range. Chapter 21 measures the whole recipe.

Use case: the loss that went NaN at step 31,000

A team trains a transformer in fp16 to save memory. It runs well for a day and then the loss prints nan. The cause is a single attention score that grew past 11 as the model sharpened: e12 overflows fp16, inf/inf is NaN, and one NaN in a forward pass poisons every weight it touches in the backward pass. The fixes, in the order they apply them: compute attention softmax in fp32 (what autocast does by default), switch the run to bf16, whose range is fp32’s, and add a check that skips a step and logs when any gradient is not finite. The run they restart from the last checkpoint never sees the problem again.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Exponentiating logits directly: naive softmax was NaN at logits of 100 in fp32 and 12 in fp16. Use torch.softmax, logsumexp and losses that take logits.
  • Passing probabilities to a log loss: sigmoid(20) is exactly 1 in fp32; BCE on it is clamped at 100 and stops saying anything useful.
  • Accumulating in 16 bits: a running sum stalled at 32 (fp16) and 4 (bf16). Keep accumulators and master weights in fp32.
  • E[x²] − E[x]² for a variance: it gave −8 here. Centre first.
  • Testing floats with ==: 0.1 + 0.2 == 0.3 is True in fp32 and False in fp64. Use torch.allclose.
  • fp16 training without loss scaling: small gradients become subnormal or 0. Or use bf16.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is the difference between fp16 and bf16, and which would you train with?

How a strong answer sounds
Both are 16 bits. fp16 has 5 exponent bits and 10 mantissa bits: about three significant digits, but a maximum of 65,504 and a smallest normal number of 6 × 10⁻⁵. bf16 has 8 exponent bits and 7 mantissa bits: fp32’s range, but only about two significant digits. Training is far more sensitive to range than to precision, because overflow produces inf and NaN and underflow zeroes gradients, while small rounding errors average out over many steps. So on hardware that supports it I would train in bf16 with fp32 master weights and fp32 accumulation, and only use fp16 with dynamic loss scaling. In a measured matmul, bf16’s error was about eight times fp16’s, which networks tolerate.

Deeper: Why does PyTorch’s cross-entropy take logits rather than probabilities?

How a strong answer sounds
Because computing it from logits can be done in a numerically stable way and computing it from probabilities cannot. Cross-entropy is −log softmax(z)[y], which equals logsumexp(z) − z[y]; log-sum-exp subtracts the maximum logit first, so nothing overflows, and no probability is ever formed, so nothing rounds to 0 or 1 before the log. If you pass probabilities, a confident prediction can round to exactly 1, its log to 0 and the other class’s log to −inf. I measured sigmoid(20) as exactly 1.0 in fp32: BCE on probabilities clamped the loss at 100, while the logits version gave the exact value, 20. Passing already-softmaxed values into cross_entropy is a different bug: it applies softmax twice and shrinks the gradients.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • A float is sign, exponent (range) and mantissa (precision); epsilon is 1.2 × 10⁻⁷ in fp32, 9.8 × 10⁻⁴ in fp16, 7.8 × 10⁻³ in bf16.
  • fp16 overflows above 65,504 and underflows below 6 × 10⁻⁸; bf16 has fp32’s range and less precision.
  • Measured: a running sum of 0.01 stalled at 32 in fp16 and 4 in bf16; keep accumulators and master weights in fp32.
  • Subtract the max before exponentiating: log-sum-exp kept softmax finite where the naive form returned NaN.
  • Losses should take logits; E[x²] − E[x]² cancels catastrophically (−8 for a true variance of 1).
  • Real gradients span 13 orders of magnitude: fp16 needs loss scaling, bf16 does not.
