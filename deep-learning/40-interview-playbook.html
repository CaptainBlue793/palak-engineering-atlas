The Deep Learning Interview Playbook

Deep learning interviews test four things: whether you understand the mechanisms (why residual connections work, why Adam needs decoupled weight decay), whether you can do the arithmetic (parameters, memory, FLOPs, output shapes), whether you can debug a run that is going wrong, and whether you can design a training and evaluation plan for a new problem. This chapter collects what the course measured into a form you can use in those conversations: the formulas, three live calculators, concept answers with the number that backs each one, a debugging order, a design template, and the habits that make answers sound like experience rather than recitation.

Every number quoted here was measured in an earlier chapter, on one RTX 5070 Ti, and the chapter is named so you can go back to the experiment. Quote them as what they are: one measurement on one setup, which is far more convincing than a slogan and far less convincing than a claim that something is always true.

  • Recognise the four question types and what a strong answer does in each
  • Recall the core formulas and compute shapes, parameters and memory quickly
  • Answer concept questions with a mechanism, a measured number and a limit
  • Debug a training run in a fixed, defensible order
  • Structure a design round from requirements to monitoring

Debugging questions

The interviewer wants an order of operations, not a guess. The one this course measured (Chapter 39): look at the data (examples next to their labels), check the initial loss (≈ ln C), overfit one batch, then train and watch gradient norms, then evaluate properly (eval mode, a baseline, a fresh split). Each check catches a different class of bug.

Design rounds

Most deep-learning design prompts fit one structure. Spend the first minutes on requirements, because they decide everything else: what the output is, which error costs more, how much labelled data exists, the latency and hardware budget, and how the model will be monitored.

Say what you would measure, not only what you would build. “I would fit gradient boosting first, because on tables it usually wins” is a decision with a reason. “I would check homophily before choosing a GNN”, “I would quantise to int8 and measure latency on the device, since pruning does not speed up dense kernels”, “I would report the median of five seeds”: each of these is a sentence an interviewer remembers, because it shows you have seen the failure it prevents.

🧑‍💼
Use case: a design round, answered well

Prompt: “Build a model that flags cracked solar panels from drone photos.” A strong candidate asks how many labelled photos exist (3,000, 4% cracked), what a missed crack costs versus a false alarm (a missed crack is ten times worse), and where the model runs (on the drone, a small ARM board). They propose an ImageNet-pretrained CNN fine-tuned with a low learning rate, because 3,000 images are too few to train from scratch, with a split by site so test panels come from farms never seen in training. They choose recall at a fixed false-alarm rate as the metric, plan five seeds, check calibration because inspectors will triage by score, and say they will quantise to int8 and measure latency on the board itself. Each choice comes with the number or failure behind it, and the interviewer spends the remaining time on follow-ups rather than on missing pieces.

⚠️
Common mistakes in interviews
  • Slogans without mechanisms: say what happens to the gradient, the activation or the update.
  • Arithmetic without a sanity check: 87M parameters in fp32 AdamW is 1.4 GB before activations.
  • Model first in a design round: requirements and the metric come first.
  • One number as a law: quote measurements as measurements, with their setup.
  • Ignoring variance: a difference smaller than the seed spread is not a difference.
  • No baseline: “90%” means nothing without the simple model’s score next to it.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How much GPU memory does training an 87M-parameter transformer need?

How a strong answer sounds
Fixed costs first: in fp32 with AdamW, 4 bytes each for weights and gradients and 8 for the two moments, 16 bytes per parameter, so about 1.4 GB. Then activations, which depend on the batch: in a measurement at 8 sequences of 1,024 tokens they were 4.7 GB, 80% of a 5.8 GB peak, growing linearly with batch × length because fused attention does not store the attention matrix. bf16 autocast cut the peak by a third. If it does not fit, I would use gradient accumulation, which is exact without BatchNorm, then activation checkpointing, which cut memory by 69% for 24% more time.

Deeper: You have one week and one GPU to get the best model for a new image dataset. Plan it.

How a strong answer sounds
Day one: look at the data, define the metric, make group- or time-based splits, and get a baseline: a linear probe on a pretrained network’s features, which takes minutes. Then fine-tune a pretrained CNN with a learning-rate sweep on a log scale, since the learning rate usually explains most of the variance (30% of it in one measured search, against at most 2.4% for anything else). Make training fast before tuning: bf16, an input pipeline that keeps the GPU busy, and torch.compile, which together took one CNN step from 11.9 ms to 3.7 ms. Then a small search with successive halving, checking that early ranks predict final ones. Finally retrain the top few with several seeds, pick on the mean, check calibration on shifted data, and report test accuracy once.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Four question types: concept, arithmetic, debugging, design. Each has a shape a strong answer follows.
  • Concept answers: mechanism, a measured number with its source, and the limit of that number.
  • Arithmetic: write the formula, plug in, check the magnitude (16 bytes per parameter, ln C at initialisation).
  • Debugging: data, initial loss, overfit one batch, gradients, proper evaluation, in that order.
  • Design: requirements and metric before the model, a baseline before the network, seeds and shifted data before the claim.
