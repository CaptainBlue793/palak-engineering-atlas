Deep Learning for Tabular Data

Chapter 1 found that gradient boosting beat a neural network on two tables and promised a proper comparison: both sides tuned with the same budget, and neural architectures built for tables. This is that comparison. Tables are where most of the world’s business data lives, and they are the one common data type on which deep learning has not taken over. Understanding why tells you when a network is worth trying on one.

On three real tables, California housing, Adult income and covertype, histogram gradient boosting, an MLP with entity embeddings and an FT-Transformer each had a random search with the same number of trials for boosting and the MLP. Boosting won all three, by 0.03 in R² on housing, 1.4 points on income and 3.9 points on forest cover, and tuning it took seconds where the networks took minutes. Two controlled changes to the data showed why: adding uninformative columns cost the MLP 0.09 in R² and boosting only 0.02, while randomly rotating the columns, which mixes every feature into every input, cost boosting 0.11 and the MLP 0.03. Boosting relies on individual columns meaning something; networks do not, and tables are exactly the data where individual columns do.

Why tables are hard for nets

An image has structure a network can exploit: neighbouring pixels are related, and the same pattern means the same thing anywhere in the frame. A table has none. Each column is a different quantity with its own units and distribution, the column order means nothing, many columns are irrelevant, and the target often depends on sharp thresholds of single columns (“income above £50,000”, “age under 25”). Decision trees split on one column at a threshold, which matches that structure exactly. Grinsztajn et al. (2022) isolated the difference with two transformations, repeated here:

A random rotation replaces every column with a different mixture of all of them; it loses no information, and a network that starts with a dense linear layer can undo it, so the MLP barely moved. Boosting lost 0.11 in R² and 6.3 points of accuracy, because after rotation no single column carries a clean threshold to split on: it depends on the columns meaning something. Uninformative columns cut the other way on the smaller table: the MLP had to learn to ignore 16 noise inputs from 12,000 examples, and its R² fell by 0.09 where boosting, which simply never splits on a useless column, lost 0.02. On covertype’s 100,000 rows both suffered, boosting more than expected, from twenty noise columns. Real tables are full of weak or irrelevant columns and of meaningful individual features, which is the regime where trees have the advantage.

Preparing inputs: scaling and embeddings

Trees are indifferent to monotone transformations of a column; networks are not. The same MLP on California housing with three input treatments:

Numeric inputs to the MLPTest R²
Raw values (population in thousands next to latitude)0.005
Standardised (zero mean, unit variance)0.723
Quantile-transformed to a normal distribution0.800

On raw inputs the network learned nothing at all: a few columns with values in the thousands dominated the first layer, training stopped improving, and early stopping ended it after 19 epochs. Standardising fixed the scale but not the heavy tails of columns such as population and rooms per household; mapping each column through its empirical quantiles to a normal distribution fixed both and added 0.08 R². For a network on a table, a quantile transform is the default first step.

Categorical columns become learned embeddings, one vector per category (Chapter 20), or one-hot vectors. On these tables it made no difference: 85.61% against 85.51% on Adult, whose largest categorical has 42 values, and 89.40% both ways on covertype’s 4 wilderness areas and 40 soil types. Embeddings pay off when a column has thousands of levels with shared structure, as Chapter 20’s 2,000 cities did; with a few dozen levels one-hot is just as good.

Nets vs gradient boosting, tuned

Each method got a random search over its usual hyperparameters, the same number of trials for boosting and the MLP, selection on a validation split and one look at the test set:

California housing (R²)0.8440.8080.815
Adult income (accuracy)87.4%85.6%86.0%
Covertype (accuracy)93.3%89.4%88.1%

Boosting won on all three, and its untuned default already beat both tuned networks on every table (0.841, 87.0% and 93.0%). The FT-Transformer (Gorishniy et al., 2021), which turns each column into a token and lets attention model interactions between them, edged out the MLP on two tables and not on covertype, where its 12-epoch cap was tightest; it was also the most sensitive to its settings, with trials ranging from 74.8% to 88.3% on covertype. On the cost side, a boosting trial on the two smaller tables took under a second on the CPU against several seconds for each network trial on the GPU. This matches the larger benchmarks (Grinsztajn et al., 2022; Shwartz-Ziv and Armon, 2022): on typical tables of up to a few hundred thousand rows, tuned boosting is as good or better and far cheaper, and the honest default is to start there.

When a network is worth it on a table

  • Mixed inputs: a table plus text, images or sequences, where a network can learn from all of them end to end.
  • High-cardinality categoricals with shared structure: user, product or merchant IDs with millions of levels, where embeddings learn similarity (Chapter 20).
  • Very large tables and online learning, where networks train in streaming mini-batches and boosting must refit.
  • An ensemble component: averaging a network with boosting often beats either, because their errors differ.
  • A differentiable model is required: as part of a larger system trained end to end.

Use case: fraud detection on card transactions

A payments team’s fraud model is gradient boosting on 300 engineered columns. A data scientist proposes a deep network and shows it beating the production model by 2 points of recall, but the comparison used boosting’s defaults against a tuned network. With both given the same 40-trial search on the same time-based split, boosting is ahead by half a point. The network is not wasted: the team adds an embedding for the merchant ID, which has 4 million values and which the boosting model could only use through hand-made aggregates, and averages the network’s score with boosting’s. The ensemble beats either alone by a point, and the merchant embeddings turn out to be useful for two other models.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Comparing a tuned network with default boosting: here boosting’s defaults beat both tuned networks.
  • Feeding raw columns to a network: California housing gave R² 0.005; a quantile transform gave 0.80.
  • Keeping every column: noise columns cost the MLP four times what they cost boosting on the smaller table.
  • Expecting embeddings to help small categoricals: no difference with 4 to 42 levels.
  • Random splits for time-ordered data: use time-based splits, or the comparison flatters every model.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Why does gradient boosting usually beat neural networks on tables?

How a strong answer sounds
Because tables reward what trees do: split one meaningful column at a threshold, ignore useless columns, and not care about scale or units. Networks mix all inputs from the first layer and must learn to ignore irrelevant ones. Two measurements make it concrete: adding noise columns to California housing cost an MLP 0.09 in R² and boosting 0.02, while randomly rotating the columns, which destroys their individual meaning without losing information, cost boosting 0.11 and the MLP only 0.03. With equal tuning budgets on three real tables, boosting won all three, and its defaults beat both tuned networks.

Deeper: You still want to try a network on a table. How do you set it up and test it fairly?

How a strong answer sounds
Quantile-transform the numeric columns, fitted on training rows only: raw inputs gave an R² of 0.005 on housing, standardising 0.72 and the quantile transform 0.80. Embed high-cardinality categoricals and one-hot the small ones, since embeddings made no difference below about 50 levels. Use an MLP with dropout and weight decay, or an FT-Transformer, with early stopping. Then give boosting and the network the same tuning budget on the same validation split and look at the test set once. And consider averaging the two, because where a network does not win alone it often helps in an ensemble.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • With equal tuning, boosting beat an MLP and an FT-Transformer on all three tables; its defaults beat both tuned networks.
  • Noise columns hurt the MLP more (−0.09 vs −0.02 R²); rotating columns hurt boosting more (−0.11 vs −0.03).
  • Networks need scaled inputs: raw columns gave R² 0.005, a quantile transform 0.80.
  • Embeddings help for many-level categoricals with shared structure, not for a few dozen levels.
  • Use networks on tables for mixed inputs, huge ID columns, streaming data or ensembles.
