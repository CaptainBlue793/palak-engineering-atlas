Probability, Likelihood & Loss Functions

Where do loss functions come from? Not from a list. Almost every loss in this course is the same idea applied to a different kind of output: assume the data was generated by some probability distribution whose parameters the network predicts, and choose the weights that make the observed data most likely. Assume Gaussian noise and you get mean squared error; assume Laplace noise and you get mean absolute error; assume a categorical outcome and you get cross-entropy. Knowing this tells you which loss to pick, what its value means, and what to do when the standard ones do not fit.

Every claim here is measured. Five per cent of gross outliers moved a mean-squared-error fit’s intercept from 1.0 to 1.53 and a mean-absolute-error fit’s only to 1.06. A network trained with the Gaussian likelihood learned not just the mean but its own noise level, within 0.02 of the truth. An untrained 10-class network scored a loss of 2.312, almost exactly log 10. And squared error on softmax outputs, a loss that looks reasonable, gave gradients up to 450 times smaller than cross-entropy for confidently wrong predictions, and trained visibly slower with plain SGD.

  • Derive MSE from Gaussian noise and MAE from Laplace noise, and know what each is robust to
  • Derive cross-entropy from a categorical output, through softmax and logits
  • Use the expected value of an untrained loss as a sanity check
  • Read entropy, cross-entropy and KL divergence, and the identity that links them

Likelihood and log-likelihood

A model with weights θ defines a probability for each observed target given its input, p(y | x; θ). If the examples are independent, the probability of the whole training set is the product over examples: the likelihood. Maximum likelihood estimation chooses the θ that makes it largest. Products of thousands of probabilities underflow (P3), and gradients of products are awkward, so we take logs, which turn the product into a sum without moving the maximum, and flip the sign so that it is something to minimise:

loss(θ) = −(1/N) Σᵢ log p(yᵢ | xᵢ; θ)

That is the negative log-likelihood, NLL, averaged over the batch. Everything else in this chapter is choosing p.

Gaussian noise gives MSE; Laplace noise gives MAE

For regression, say the target is the network’s prediction plus Gaussian noise: y = f(x; θ) + ε, ε ~ N(0, σ²). The log-density of one target is −(y − f)²/(2σ²) − log σ − ½ log 2π. With σ fixed, everything except (y − f)² is a constant, so maximising the likelihood is exactly minimising the squared error. If instead the noise is Laplace, with density proportional to exp(−|ε|/b), the same steps give the absolute error.

The choice encodes what you believe about errors. A Gaussian makes large errors astronomically unlikely, so MSE pays enormously for them and bends the whole fit to reduce them. A Laplace distribution has heavier tails, so MAE tolerates a few large errors. MSE’s best constant prediction is the mean of the targets; MAE’s is the median. Measured on 1,000 points from y = 1.5x + 1 with Gaussian noise (σ = 0.5):

Learning the noise as well

Nothing forces σ to be a constant. Let the network output two numbers per input, a mean μ(x) and a log-variance s(x), and minimise the full Gaussian NLL, ½[s + (y − μ)²/eˢ]. The network is now rewarded for admitting uncertainty where the data is noisy: a large predicted variance shrinks the squared-error term, and the s term stops it from claiming infinite variance everywhere. Trained on data whose true noise rose from σ = 0.1 to σ = 0.9 across the input range, the network predicted σ = 0.12, 0.52 and 0.91 at the left end, the middle and the right end, against 0.10, 0.50 and 0.90. Predicting the log of the variance keeps it positive without a constraint. PyTorch’s F.gaussian_nll_loss computes the same thing (it matched to six decimals here); Chapter 31 uses this for uncertainty.

mu, logvar = net(x).unbind(-1)                     # (B,), (B,)
nll = 0.5 * (logvar + (y - mu) ** 2 / logvar.exp()).mean()   # Gaussian NLL up to a constant
# equivalently: F.gaussian_nll_loss(mu, y, var=logvar.exp())

Categorical outputs give cross-entropy

For classification into K classes the network outputs K real numbers, logits z, and softmax turns them into a categorical distribution: pₖ = ezₖ / Σⱼ ezⱼ. The likelihood of the observed class y is py, so the NLL of one example is −log py = −zy + log Σⱼ ezⱼ. That is cross-entropy, and its gradient with respect to the logits is remarkably simple: p − onehot(y). The binary case, with one logit and a sigmoid, gives binary cross-entropy.

logits = model(xb)                         # (128, 10): any real numbers
loss = F.cross_entropy(logits, yb)          # = -log_softmax(logits)[range(128), yb].mean()
# an untrained net predicts roughly uniform probabilities, so the loss starts near -log(1/10) = log(10) = 2.303

That last comment is a free sanity check. Measured on a freshly initialised MLP: 2.312. If a new 10-class model starts at 8, or at 0.5, something is wrong before training has even begun: logits far too large from a bad initialisation, a softmax applied twice, the labels leaking into the input. Chapter 39 uses this as the first item of a debugging checklist.

Why not simply penalise the squared distance between the softmax probabilities and the one-hot target? It looks reasonable and it does train, but it is not the likelihood of a categorical outcome, and it pays for that in the gradient. When the model is confidently wrong, with py = 0.001, the cross-entropy gradient with respect to the logits had norm 1.05; the squared-error gradient had norm 0.0023, 450 times smaller, because softmax is saturated there and the squared error has no log to undo it. With plain SGD that made a visible difference: after one epoch, 75.8% against 62.6% test accuracy, and after five, 83.5% against 81.1%. With Adam the gap almost vanished (88.3% against 87.9%), because Adam divides each update by the gradient’s recent size; a good optimiser can hide a poor loss, but it should not have to.

Entropy and KL divergence

Three quantities from information theory turn up in every later chapter. The entropy of a distribution, H(p) = −Σ pₖ log pₖ, measures its uncertainty: 0 for a certain prediction, log K (2.303 nats for 10 classes) for a uniform one. The cross-entropy of a model q against a target distribution p, H(p, q) = −Σ pₖ log qₖ, is the loss above, with p one-hot. The KL divergence, KL(p ‖ q) = Σ pₖ log(pₖ / qₖ), is how much worse q is than the ideal, and they are linked by

H(p, q) = H(p) + KL(p ‖ q)

Checked numerically with label-smoothed targets (0.91 on the true class, 0.01 elsewhere) against the trained model’s predictions: cross-entropy 1.4998 = entropy 0.5003 + KL 0.9995. With one-hot targets H(p) = 0, so minimising cross-entropy is minimising KL. KL is not symmetric, and it is not a distance; distillation (Chapter 32) and VAEs (Computer Vision) both minimise one direction of it. Entropy is also a useful readout of a trained model: on the test set, predictions it got right had a mean entropy of 0.195 nats, predictions it got wrong 0.778. Wrong answers are less confident on average, but many are still confident, which is the calibration problem of Chapter 31.

Use case: forecasting API latency with error bars

A platform team predicts the p50 latency of each API request from its route, payload size and current load, to decide whether to route it to a slower region. A plain MSE model gives one number per request, and the routing rule has no idea when that number is a guess. Switching the output to a mean and a log-variance, trained with Gaussian NLL, costs one extra output unit. The predicted σ is small for cached GET routes and large for search queries under heavy load, and the router now only moves a request when the predicted saving exceeds two standard deviations. On a skewed latency distribution the team later moves to predicting the log of the latency, which is the same idea with a log-normal noise model.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • MSE on data with gross outliers: 5% of them moved the intercept from 1.0 to 1.53; MAE or Huber held at 1.06.
  • Squared error on softmax probabilities for classification: gradients up to 450× smaller when confidently wrong; slower with SGD.
  • Ignoring the starting loss: an untrained K-class model should start near log K (2.303 for 10 classes; measured 2.312).
  • Predicting a variance directly instead of its log: it can go negative or to zero and the NLL explodes.
  • Using softmax and cross-entropy for multi-label problems: labels that can co-occur need one sigmoid and BCE per label.
  • Treating KL as a symmetric distance: KL(p ‖ q) ≠ KL(q ‖ p).

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Why is mean squared error the default loss for regression?

How a strong answer sounds
Because it is the negative log-likelihood under the assumption that targets are the prediction plus Gaussian noise of constant variance. Writing out the Gaussian log-density, everything except the squared difference is a constant, so maximising the likelihood is minimising squared error, and the best constant prediction is the mean. The assumption matters: Gaussian tails make large errors very unlikely, so MSE is pulled hard by outliers. If the noise is heavy-tailed I would use absolute error, which is the Laplace likelihood and targets the median, or Huber. In a measured fit, 5% of gross outliers moved the MSE intercept from 1.0 to 1.53 and the MAE intercept only to 1.06.

Deeper: Why is cross-entropy better than squared error on the softmax output for classification?

How a strong answer sounds
Cross-entropy is the likelihood of a categorical outcome, and its gradient with respect to the logits is simply p minus the one-hot target, so a confidently wrong prediction gets a large corrective gradient. Squared error on softmax outputs has no log to cancel the exponential in softmax; when the model is confidently wrong the softmax is saturated and the gradient nearly vanishes. I measured it: with the true class at probability 0.001 the cross-entropy gradient norm was 1.05 and the squared-error one 0.0023. With plain SGD on Fashion-MNIST that meant 75.8% against 62.6% after one epoch. Adam nearly hid the gap because it rescales gradients, but the loss is still the wrong model of the data.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • A loss is a negative log-likelihood: choose the distribution of the target, let the network predict its parameters.
  • Gaussian noise gives MSE (the mean); Laplace noise gives MAE (the median), which resists outliers.
  • A network can predict its own variance with Gaussian NLL; here it learned σ within 0.02 of the truth.
  • Softmax plus categorical likelihood is cross-entropy; its logit gradient is p − onehot(y), and an untrained K-class model starts near log K.
  • Cross-entropy = entropy + KL divergence; minimising it with one-hot targets minimises KL.

Check your understanding

Minimising mean squared error is maximum likelihood under which assumption?

The Gaussian log-density is −(y − f)²/2σ² plus terms that do not depend on the weights.

A new 10-class classifier’s first-batch loss is 2.31. What does that suggest?

Measured on a fresh MLP: 2.312. A start far from log K points to a bug.

What is the gradient of cross-entropy with respect to the logits?

Large when the model is confidently wrong, which is why it trains well; squared error on softmax lacks this.

Why predict log σ² rather than σ² in a Gaussian NLL output?

A raw variance output could reach zero or go negative and make the NLL infinite.

Which identity links cross-entropy, entropy and KL divergence?

Checked here: 1.4998 = 0.5003 + 0.9995. With one-hot targets H(p) = 0.
