Continual Learning & Catastrophic Forgetting

Every chapter so far trained on a fixed dataset, shuffled, with all the data available at once. Real systems often cannot do that: new classes appear, the data distribution drifts, and the old data may be gone, too large to keep or not allowed to be kept. Train a network on the new data alone and it forgets the old, quickly and almost completely: catastrophic forgetting (McCloskey and Cohen, 1989). Continual learning is the study of how to learn a sequence of tasks without that.

This chapter measures forgetting and the two classic remedies on split CIFAR-10 (five tasks of two classes each) and permuted MNIST (ten tasks, each the digits with their pixels shuffled differently), in the three standard settings. When one shared classifier had to tell all classes apart, plain fine-tuning kept nothing of the old tasks: accuracy on task 1 fell from 88% to 0.0% within ten steps of training on task 2. Elastic weight consolidation, which protects the weights important for earlier tasks, helped substantially when the task identity was given or the label set stayed the same, and did nothing when classes had to be told apart across tasks. Experience replay, mixing a small buffer of old examples into each batch, helped in every setting, and a buffer of 5,000 examples came within 3 points of training on all the tasks at once on split CIFAR’s easier setting.

  • Distinguish task-, domain- and class-incremental learning, and why they differ so much in difficulty

Catastrophic forgetting

Train on task 1, then on task 2 with task 1’s data gone, then task 3, and record the accuracy on every task seen so far after each stage. The result is an accuracy matrix: row t gives accuracy on tasks 1 to t after learning task t. Two summary numbers come from it: the average accuracy over all tasks at the end, and backward transfer, the average change in accuracy on each earlier task between just after learning it and the end (negative means forgetting). Plain fine-tuning on split CIFAR-10, with a separate output head per task and the task identity given at test time:

Each two-class task had been learned to 83–93%, and at the end the first two were barely above the 50% of guessing: an average of 67.3% and a backward transfer of −26.3 points. Nothing tells the network to keep the old features; gradient descent on task 5’s loss moves every weight that helps task 5, including the ones tasks 1 to 4 relied on.

Task-, domain- and class-incremental

How bad forgetting is depends on what the network is asked at test time (van de Ven et al., 2022). In task-incremental learning the task identity is given, so each task can have its own output head and the network need only choose between that task’s classes. In domain-incremental learning the label set is the same for every task but the inputs change (permuted MNIST: always the ten digits, each time with a new pixel shuffle), and one shared head must work without knowing which task an input comes from. In class-incremental learning new classes arrive with each task and one head must choose among all classes seen so far, without being told the task.

Class-incremental fine-tuning ended with exactly 0.0% on every old task and 90.6% on the last: the network simply predicted only the two newest classes. It happened almost at once. Tracking task 1 during training on task 2, its accuracy was already 0.0% after the first 10 optimiser steps, because the output layer’s biases and weights for the new classes quickly dominate every input. In the task-incremental version the same 10 steps cost only 2 points and the decline was gradual. The difference is not in the representation but in the decision: with a shared head, “which task is this?” becomes part of the problem, and nothing in the new data teaches it.

Replay

The most direct remedy is to keep some old data. A replay buffer holds a fixed number of past examples, chosen by reservoir sampling so that every example seen so far has the same chance of being in it, and each training batch for the new task is mixed with a batch drawn from the buffer:

for i, (x, y) in enumerate(stream, start=1):                 # reservoir sampling: buffer of size M
    if len(buf) < M: buf.append((x, y))
    elif (j := random.randrange(i)) < M: buf[j] = (x, y)      # keep with probability M / i
batch = cat(new_batch, sample(buf, 64))                     # train on new and old together

Replay helped everywhere, and even 200 stored examples, about 20 per digit, lifted the domain-incremental MNIST average from 41% to 84%. On the three easier settings 5,000 examples came within 0.4 to 3 points of training on everything; the class-incremental gap stayed large at 15 points. One likely reason: half of every batch covers just the two newest classes while the replayed half is spread over all the old ones, so the head still leans towards the newest; rebalancing the batch or correcting the output layer’s bias are the usual next steps.

Elastic weight consolidation

When no old data can be kept, the alternative is to protect what matters. EWC (Kirkpatrick et al., 2017) estimates how important each weight was for an old task with the diagonal of the Fisher information, the average squared gradient of the log-likelihood, and adds a quadratic penalty that makes important weights expensive to move:

F = mean over examples of (∂ log p(y|x) / ∂θ)²          # per weight, after learning task t
loss = task_loss + Σ_t (λ/2) · Σ_i F_t,i · (θ_i − θ*_t,i)²    # stay near each old solution where it mattered

EWC worked where the old tasks’ decisions could be preserved by preserving weights: on permuted MNIST it raised the task-incremental average from 78% to 92% and the domain-incremental one from 41% to 85%, with no stored data at all. It did nothing for class-incremental learning, where the failure is in the output layer’s new classes overriding the old, which no penalty on old weights prevents. And a strong penalty showed the other side of the trade, the stability–plasticity dilemma: at λ = 1,000 on split CIFAR, backward transfer became positive, so almost nothing was forgotten, but task 3 was learned only to 55.7% instead of about 90%, because the network could no longer move enough to learn it. On domain-incremental MNIST the strongest penalty froze the network into the first few tasks and the last ones reached only 23–30%.

Measuring forgetting

A continual-learning result needs the whole accuracy matrix, or at least three numbers: the final average accuracy, backward transfer (how much old tasks lost) and the accuracy each task reached when it was learned (plasticity, which EWC at λ = 1,000 sacrificed). Report the setting explicitly, since the same method scored 78% task-incremental and 15% class-incremental on the same data, and compare against two reference points: fine-tuning, the lower bound, and joint training on all data, the upper bound. Order matters too: the first task sees the most subsequent training, so averages over a few task orders are more trustworthy than one.

🛒
Use case: a product-image classifier that gains categories every month

An online shop’s classifier must add new product categories each month, and the image archive for older categories is too large to retrain on in full. Fine-tuning on each month’s new categories alone makes the classifier label nearly everything as one of the newest categories within an hour of training, the class-incremental collapse. The team keeps a reservoir of 20 images per old category, about 2% of the archive, mixes it into every batch, and rescales the output layer so new categories do not start with an advantage. Accuracy on old categories stays within 2 points of a full retrain, and the full retrain is scheduled once a quarter instead of every month.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Fine-tuning on new classes alone with a shared head: old-task accuracy hit 0.0% within 10 steps.
  • Reporting task-incremental numbers for a class-incremental product: the same data gave 67% and 18%.
  • Using EWC for class-incremental learning: it moved the average from 18.1% to at most 18.2%.
  • A very strong penalty: forgetting stopped, and so did learning (task 3 at 55.7%).
  • Reporting only the final average: show backward transfer and the accuracy each task first reached.
  • Comparing without the upper bound: joint training shows how much forgetting is left to fix.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is catastrophic forgetting, and why does it happen?

How a strong answer sounds
When a network is trained on a new task without the old data, gradient descent changes whatever weights reduce the new loss, including those the old tasks depended on, so old performance collapses. On split CIFAR-10 with a separate head per task, fine-tuning through five tasks dropped the first task from 86% to 52%. With one shared classifier over all classes it was far worse: the first task went to exactly 0% within ten steps of the second, because the output layer learned to predict only the newest classes. That difference, task- versus class-incremental, is the first thing I would establish about any continual-learning problem.

Deeper: Compare replay and elastic weight consolidation.

How a strong answer sounds
Replay keeps a buffer of old examples, reservoir-sampled, and mixes them into each new batch; EWC keeps no data and instead penalises moving weights in proportion to their Fisher information for earlier tasks. In my measurements replay helped in every setting and 5,000 examples came within 3 points of joint training on the easier settings; even 200 examples took domain-incremental MNIST from 41% to 84%. EWC did well when task identity or the label set was fixed, 78% to 92% on task-incremental MNIST, failed completely on class-incremental CIFAR, and at high strength stopped the network learning new tasks. So I would use replay wherever storing some data is allowed, and EWC-style penalties when it is not and the setting suits them.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Sequential training forgets: backward transfer −26 points on split CIFAR with per-task heads.
  • The setting decides the difficulty: class-incremental fine-tuning fell to 0.0% on old tasks within 10 steps.
  • Replay helped everywhere; 5,000 examples came within 3 points of joint training on three of four settings.
  • EWC worked for task- and domain-incremental MNIST (78% → 92%, 41% → 85%) and not at all for class-incremental.
  • Stability costs plasticity: strong EWC stopped forgetting and stopped learning.
