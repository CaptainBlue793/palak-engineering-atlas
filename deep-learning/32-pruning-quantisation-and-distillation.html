Pruning, Quantisation & Distillation

A trained network is usually bigger and slower than it needs to be. Three families of techniques shrink it after the fact or during a second round of training. Pruning removes weights or whole channels. Quantisation stores and computes with fewer bits per number. Distillation trains a smaller network to imitate the large one. Each trades a little accuracy for size or speed, and each has a trap: a smaller model on disk is not necessarily a faster one.

This chapter applies all three to a CIFAR-10 CNN with 559,000 parameters and 90.8% accuracy. Pruning 90% of its weights and fine-tuning for 5 epochs kept 88.2%, but the pruned network ran at exactly the dense network’s speed, because dense GPU kernels multiply zeros like any other number. Removing half the channels instead kept 88.2% and ran 2.6 times faster. Converting to int8 cost no accuracy at all and ran 3.2 times faster on a CPU. At 4 bits post-training quantisation lost 6 points, and quantisation-aware training recovered all but one. A student a sixteenth of the size gained 1.8 points from learning on the teacher’s soft predictions.

  • Prune by magnitude, globally or per layer, and know why fine-tuning is essential
  • Tell unstructured from structured sparsity, and which one makes a network faster
  • Test the lottery-ticket hypothesis and explain why rewinding helps
  • Quantise weights and activations, per tensor and per channel, and calibrate activation ranges
  • Use quantisation-aware training and knowledge distillation when post-training methods fall short

Magnitude pruning

The simplest pruning rule is that small weights matter least: set the smallest fraction s of them to zero, keep a binary mask so they stay zero, and fine-tune the rest. Global pruning ranks all weights in the network together; layer-wise pruning removes the same fraction from every layer.

Without fine-tuning, accuracy collapsed beyond half the weights: at 90% the network was at chance. Five epochs of fine-tuning with the mask fixed brought 90% sparsity back to 88.2%. The pruned weights were small, but the remaining ones had been trained to work alongside them, and need a short retraining to compensate. Global pruning beat layer-wise at high sparsity because layers differ in redundancy: at 90% overall, it removed only 31% of the first convolution, which has just 1,728 weights and sees raw pixels, and 98.5% of the last convolution, which has the most. Pruning gradually, 20% of the remaining weights per round with 2 epochs of fine-tuning each, was no better than one shot here: 87.6% at 89.3% sparsity and 85.4% at 95.6%, despite 28 epochs of fine-tuning in total.

Structured pruning and real speed

A network with 90% of its weights set to zero is still stored and run as dense matrices, so it does exactly the same arithmetic. Measured inference throughput on the RTX 5070 Ti, bf16, batch 1,024:

ModelParametersAccuracyImages per second
Dense559k90.79%106,496 (1.0×)
90% of weights zeroed (unstructured)57k non-zero88.20%107,431 (1.0×)
25% of channels removed315k89.71%136,880 (1.3×)
50% of channels removed141k88.22%271,911 (2.6×)
75% of channels removed36k81.52%566,117 (5.3×)

Structured pruning removes whole output channels, here the ones whose BatchNorm scale γ is smallest, and rebuilds a genuinely smaller dense network: half the channels in every layer means a quarter of the multiplies in every convolution. At the same 88.2% accuracy as the 90%-unstructured network, it was 2.6 times faster. Unstructured sparsity pays off only with hardware or kernels that skip zeros: sparse kernels at very high sparsity, or the 2:4 pattern (two zeros in every four weights) that recent NVIDIA tensor cores accelerate. Otherwise it saves only storage, and only if the zeros are stored compressed.

Lottery tickets

Frankle and Carbin (2019) asked whether the sparse sub-network found by pruning could have been trained in isolation from the start. Their test: keep the mask, reset the surviving weights to their original initial values, and train only those. Measured with the global masks at 90% and 97% sparsity, 30 epochs each:

Surviving weights start from90% sparse97% sparse
Their original initial values (the “winning ticket”)87.34%79.44%
Their values after 1 epoch of training (rewinding)88.79%80.70%
A fresh random initialisation, same mask85.83%78.92%

The original initialisation beat a random one by 1.5 points at 90% sparsity, so the mask and the initial values do work together. Rewinding to the weights after one epoch, as Frankle et al. later proposed for larger networks, did better still. The gaps shrank at 97%, and none of the sparse networks trained from the start matched simply pruning the trained dense network and fine-tuning (88.2% at 90%), which needed far less compute. The lottery ticket is a finding about how training works more than a practical shortcut. The case study at the end of the course follows it further.

Post-training quantisation

Quantising a number to b bits maps it to one of 2b evenly spaced levels between a minimum and a maximum, so the work is choosing that range. For weights the range is known: symmetric around zero, either one scale for a whole tensor or one per output channel. For activations, which change with every input, the range is estimated by running a few hundred calibration images through the network and recording what each layer sees, either the full min-to-max or a percentile range that ignores rare outliers. BatchNorm is first folded into the preceding convolution, since at inference it is just a per-channel scale and shift.

Eight bits is free for a CNN like this: every variant was within 0.1 points of fp32. Run for real with PyTorch’s int8 CPU kernels, the network was 3.8 times smaller (2.13 MB to 0.56 MB), ran a batch of 64 in 4.6 ms instead of 14.5 ms, and scored 90.85% against 90.80%. At 4 bits the choices start to matter. Per-tensor weight scales lost 8.5 points because one channel with large weights forces a coarse grid on all the others; per-channel scales cut the loss to 1.7. With 4-bit activations as well the loss was 6 to 7 points, and at 2-bit weights the network was at chance.

Quantisation-aware training

When post-training quantisation loses too much, train with the rounding in the loop. The forward pass quantises weights and activations exactly as deployment will; the backward pass uses a straight-through estimator that treats rounding as the identity, since its true gradient is zero almost everywhere. The network learns weights that sit well on the quantisation grid.

QAT recovered all but 1.1 points at 4 bits and turned a useless 2-bit network into a 76% one. One honest note from running it: the first attempt used the original training learning rate, 0.01, with no warm-up, and QAT destroyed the network (4-bit accuracy fell to 19.8%). With BatchNorm folded away, nothing renormalises the activations, and large updates on top of rounding noise diverged. A learning rate ten times smaller worked; three times smaller again recovered less in the same 10 epochs.

Knowledge distillation

A small network trained on hard labels learns only which class is right. A teacher’s full probability vector also says which wrong classes are plausible (a cat that looks somewhat like a dog), and distillation (Hinton et al., 2015) trains the student to match it. Both distributions are softened with a temperature T to expose those small probabilities, and the KL divergence between them is mixed with the ordinary loss. Teacher: twice as wide, 2.2M parameters, 91.98%. Student: a quarter as wide, 36,000 parameters.

The student gained 1.75 points at 30 epochs, more than doubling its training time gained without a teacher (+1.4), and the two gains added up: distilled for 60 epochs it reached 85.9%. A temperature of 4 to 8 worked better than 1, which leaves the teacher’s probabilities almost one-hot. The student remained 6 points short of the teacher: a sixteen-fold cut in parameters has a cost that no training signal removes, but distillation made the cut cheaper.

Use case: a defect detector on a factory camera

A manufacturer runs a defect classifier on a small ARM board next to a production line, and needs 30 frames per second where the fp32 model manages 9. Unstructured pruning to 80% shrinks the file but leaves the frame rate unchanged, which surprises the team until they profile: the board’s kernels are dense. Int8 post-training quantisation, calibrated on 500 frames from the line, gives 2.7 times the speed at no measurable accuracy cost. For the remaining margin they distil the model into a student with half the channels, which with int8 reaches 34 frames per second and loses 0.6 points of recall, a trade the quality engineers sign off after checking it on a month of labelled defects.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Expecting unstructured sparsity to run faster: 90% zeros ran at exactly the dense speed.
  • Pruning without fine-tuning: 90% sparsity fell to chance; 5 epochs restored 88.2%.
  • Per-tensor scales at low bit widths: 4-bit weights lost 8.5 points per tensor, 1.7 per channel.
  • Forgetting to fold BatchNorm or calibrate activations before quantising.
  • QAT at the original learning rate: it collapsed the BN-folded network to 19.8%.
  • Counting parameters instead of measuring latency on the device you deploy to.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: You need a model twice as fast at inference. What do you try?

How a strong answer sounds
First int8 post-training quantisation, because it is cheap and usually free in accuracy: on a CIFAR CNN it cost nothing and ran 3.2 times faster on a CPU. If that is not enough, I make the network genuinely smaller: structured pruning of whole channels, which at half the channels was 2.6 times faster for 2.6 points, or distillation into a smaller student. I would not rely on unstructured pruning for speed; 90% zeros ran exactly as fast as the dense model on a GPU, because dense kernels still multiply the zeros. And I measure latency on the target hardware rather than counting parameters.

Deeper: Why does 4-bit quantisation lose accuracy, and how do you get it back?

How a strong answer sounds
With 16 levels, the rounding error is large compared with the spread of the weights, and one outlier channel can force a coarse grid on a whole tensor. Per-channel scales fix the outlier problem: 4-bit weights lost 8.5 points with one scale per tensor and 1.7 with one per channel. For activations, a percentile range that ignores rare extremes helps a little. Beyond that, quantisation-aware training puts the rounding in the forward pass and passes gradients straight through it, so the weights adapt to the grid: 4-bit weights and activations went from 84.8% to 89.7%, and 2-bit weights from chance to 76%. It needs a smaller learning rate than the original training, or it diverges.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Magnitude pruning plus fine-tuning kept 88.2% at 90% sparsity; global beat layer-wise.
  • Unstructured zeros gave no speed-up; removing half the channels gave 2.6×.
  • Lottery tickets are real (original init beat random) but rewinding beat them, and neither beat prune-then-fine-tune.
  • int8 was free and 3.2× faster on a CPU; at 4 bits, per-channel scales and QAT matter.
  • Distillation at T = 4 gave a 16× smaller student 1.75 points, on top of longer training.
