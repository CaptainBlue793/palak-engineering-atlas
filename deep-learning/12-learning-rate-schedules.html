Learning-Rate Schedules & Warm-up

Chapter 8 showed that the best learning rate sits just below the edge of divergence. It also moves during training: early on, a large rate makes fast progress across a rough landscape; late on, the same rate keeps the weights bouncing around a minimum they could settle into. A schedule changes the rate as training goes: decaying it towards the end, and often warming it up from a small value at the start. Almost every modern training recipe uses one, and on a fixed budget the choice is worth several points.

Five schedules were run on the same CIFAR-10 CNN, the same optimiser (SGD with Nesterov momentum, peak rate 0.1) and the same 15 epochs. A constant rate reached 80.1%; dropping it tenfold twice reached 87.4%; cosine decay 88.3%; a short warm-up then cosine 89.1%; and the one-cycle schedule 89.2%. A one-epoch range test, raising the rate from 10⁻⁵ to 10, found the loss lowest between 0.03 and 0.14, consistent with the 0.1 that worked. And on a 6-layer transformer, the textbook warm-up story did not hold: with post-norm blocks at Adam 10⁻³ the model never learned, with or without 1,000 warm-up steps, while the same model with pre-norm blocks trained to 99.4% with no warm-up at all.

Constant vs decayed

With a constant rate, SGD makes fast progress and then stalls at a noise floor: each mini-batch gradient points somewhere slightly different, and steps of a fixed size keep the weights jittering around the minimum rather than settling in. Shrinking the step lowers that floor. Measured, the constant-rate run’s training loss was still 0.39 after 15 epochs, and its test accuracy swung from epoch to epoch (72.8% after epoch 8); every decaying schedule ended with training loss between 0.22 and 0.31 and test accuracy 7 to 9 points higher. The decay is not a refinement; on a short budget it is most of the result.

def lr_at(kind, t, peak, T):                           # t = step, T = total steps
    if kind == "step":   return peak * 0.1 ** ((t >= 0.5 * T) + (t >= 0.75 * T))
    if kind == "cosine": return peak * 0.5 * (1 + math.cos(math.pi * t / T))
    if kind == "warm-up + cosine":
        w = int(0.05 * T)                                 # 5% of steps rising linearly from 0
        return peak * t / w if t < w else peak * 0.5 * (1 + math.cos(math.pi * (t - w) / (T - w)))
for group in opt.param_groups: group["lr"] = lr_at(kind, step, 0.1, total)   # set every step
# or the built-ins: torch.optim.lr_scheduler.CosineAnnealingLR, OneCycleLR, LinearLR + SequentialLR

Step, cosine and one-cycle

Step decay divides the rate by 10 at fixed points, here at 50% and 75% of training; the classic ResNet recipe. Each drop gives a sudden fall in loss. Cosine decay (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2017) lowers the rate smoothly along half a cosine to zero, with no milestones to choose. One-cycle (Smith, 2018) climbs from a small rate to the peak over the first 30% of training and then decays to near zero, spending the middle of training at high rates for fast exploration.

Warm-up

Warm-up starts the rate small and raises it to the peak over the first few hundred or thousand steps. The usual explanation has two parts. At initialisation the network is in an unusual region where gradients can be large and unrepresentative, and a full-size step there can throw the weights somewhere they never recover from. And Adam’s second-moment estimate v is built from very few gradients at first, so its step sizes are unreliable until it has seen a few hundred (bias correction fixes its average, not its noise). Transformers in particular, especially the original post-norm design, which normalises after adding the residual, were found to diverge without warm-up.

The measurement here did not reproduce that story, which is itself the lesson. With post-norm blocks and Adam at 10⁻³, the loss stayed at log 2 = 0.693 (chance for two classes) for all 4,000 steps, and 1,000 warm-up steps did not change that. At 3 × 10⁻⁴ the post-norm model trained either way (98.8% without warm-up, 99.2% with). Switching to pre-norm blocks, which normalise each branch’s input and leave a clean residual path (Chapter 15), let the model train at 10⁻³ with no warm-up (99.4%). In this small setup the problem at 10⁻³ was the architecture’s stability at that rate, not the first few hundred steps, and the fixes that worked were a lower peak rate or a more stable block. Warm-up remains cheap insurance and helped the CNN’s first epoch, but it is not a cure for a rate the model cannot tolerate.

The learning-rate range test

Instead of a grid of full runs, train once for a short time while raising the rate exponentially every step, and watch the loss. At first nothing happens; then the loss starts to fall; it falls fastest somewhere in the middle; then it becomes erratic and finally explodes. Measured over one epoch from 10⁻⁵ to 10, the smoothed loss barely moved below 10⁻⁴, fell steadily from 10⁻³ to 10⁻², reached its lowest value of 1.63 between 0.03 and 0.14, became erratic above that, and blew up as it approached 10. The usual readings are “the rate where the loss falls fastest” or “a little below the minimum”: both point at roughly 0.01 to 0.1 here, and the 15-epoch runs above used a peak of 0.1 with good results. It costs one epoch instead of a dozen runs; it gives a range, not an answer, and the answer still needs checking with a real run.

⏳
Use case: the training run that was cut short

A team schedules cosine decay over 100 epochs for a nightly model, then has to stop it at epoch 60 to meet a deadline. The checkpoint is noticeably worse than last week’s 60-epoch model trained with a 60-epoch schedule, because at epoch 60 of 100 the rate is still at 35% of its peak and has not yet entered its low phase. Cosine and one-cycle schedules are tied to a total length; if the budget may change, use a schedule whose decay can be triggered on demand (a constant phase followed by a short final decay, sometimes called warm-up–stable–decay), or keep the planned length honest.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • No decay at all: the constant rate finished 9 points behind on the same budget.
  • Comparing schedules mid-training: step decay led at epoch 8 and finished behind.
  • Treating warm-up as a cure for a rate that is too high: it did not rescue post-norm at 10⁻³ here.
  • Stopping a cosine schedule early: the rate never reached its low phase.
  • Calling the scheduler per epoch when it was configured per step, or the reverse: the schedule runs at the wrong speed.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Why do we decay the learning rate during training?

How a strong answer sounds
Because mini-batch gradients are noisy. Early in training a large rate makes fast progress and the noise even helps explore; but near a minimum, steps of a fixed size keep the weights bouncing around it at a level set by the noise and the step size. Lowering the rate lets them settle into the bottom. I measured it on a CIFAR-10 CNN with a 15-epoch budget: a constant rate ended at 80.1%, step decay at 87.4%, cosine at 88.3%, and warm-up plus cosine or one-cycle at about 89.2%. On a short budget the decay is most of the result.

Deeper: A transformer diverges or will not train at your chosen learning rate. Would you add warm-up?

How a strong answer sounds
I would add it, since it is cheap and helps Adam’s early estimates, but I would not assume it is the fix. I would also try a lower peak rate and check the block design: post-norm transformers are much less stable than pre-norm ones, which keep a clean residual path. In a small experiment of mine, a 6-layer post-norm encoder with Adam at 10⁻³ never learned, with or without 1,000 steps of warm-up; at 3 × 10⁻⁴ it trained, and the pre-norm version trained at 10⁻³ without any warm-up. I would also add gradient clipping and look at the gradient norm early in training.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Decay the rate: on the same budget a constant rate scored 80.1%, decaying schedules 87.4% to 89.2%.
  • Smooth decays (cosine, one-cycle) beat step decay slightly here and need no milestones.
  • Warm-up helped the first epoch (50.7% vs 43.2%) but did not rescue a post-norm transformer at too high a rate.
  • A one-epoch range test brackets the peak rate (here 0.03 to 0.14); confirm it with a real run.
  • Judge schedules at the end of the budget, and keep the planned length honest.
