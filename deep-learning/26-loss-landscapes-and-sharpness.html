Loss Landscapes & Sharpness

Training moves a point through a space with as many dimensions as the network has parameters, downhill on the loss. The shape of that space, its valleys, ridges and plateaus, decides where training ends up, and a long-standing idea holds that the shape around the end point predicts generalisation: solutions in wide, flat valleys generalise better than those in narrow, sharp ones. This chapter looks at the landscape directly, in the few ways a million-dimensional surface can be looked at, and then tests the flat-minima idea.

On a plain CIFAR-10 CNN with 0.67 million parameters, the straight line from initialisation to the trained solution ran steadily downhill. The straight line between two solutions trained from different seeds rose to a loss barrier where accuracy fell to 12%, yet a curved path trained between them stayed above 81% the whole way. Larger batches did end in sharper minima, as the theory predicts, with the top Hessian eigenvalue rising from 11 at batch 32 to 118 at batch 2,048. But making a solution flatter on purpose did not make it better: sharpness-aware minimisation cut the eigenvalue from 14.8 to 8.7 and lowered test accuracy slightly. And one sharpness measure turned out to be arbitrary: rescaling two layers in a way that leaves the network’s function identical multiplied the top eigenvalue by five.

  • Measure sharpness with the Hessian’s top eigenvalue and with random perturbations
  • Relate batch size to sharpness, and recognise the confounds
  • Explain mode connectivity: barriers on straight lines, none on trained curves
  • Implement sharpness-aware minimisation and judge what it buys

Plotting a loss landscape

A network’s loss is a function of all its weights at once, so any picture is a slice. The simplest is a line: evaluate the training loss at θ(t) = (1 − t)·θ₀ + t·θ₁ between two weight vectors. Between the random initialisation and the trained solution of a CNN, measured on 5,000 training images:

Fraction of the way from initialisation to the solution00.250.50.751
Training loss2.302.291.930.620.15
Test accuracy8.8%10.6%37.9%82.7%88.9%

The loss fell monotonically along the straight line, with no hills to climb, as Goodfellow et al. (2015) observed: the path training actually took was curved and noisy, but the landscape between start and finish is simple in this direction. For a two-dimensional picture, pick two random directions and evaluate the loss on a grid around the solution. Directions must be filter-normalised (Li et al., 2018): each filter of the random direction is scaled to the norm of the matching filter in the network. Without that, a ReLU network’s scale invariance makes the picture meaningless, because shrinking one layer and growing the next changes nothing about the function but everything about how far a step of size 1 moves it.

Flat vs sharp minima

Two ways to put a number on sharpness. The top eigenvalue of the Hessian, the curvature of the loss along its steepest direction, found by power iteration on Hessian-vector products without ever forming the Hessian. And the average rise in loss under random perturbations of fixed, filter-normalised size, which looks at all directions rather than the worst one. The slices in the panel show the difference in shape: around the batch-128 solution the loss rose from 0.15 at the centre to 23 at the corners of the slice; around a batch-8,192 solution, from 1.52 to only 2.41. That flatness is not a virtue here: the batch-8,192 network had trained for 30 epochs of 7 steps each and sat at a loss ten times higher, on a broad plateau rather than in a valley.

Batch size and sharpness

Keskar et al. (2017) reported that large-batch training finds sharper minima that generalise worse. Measured with 30 epochs at each batch size:

Up to batch 2,048 the top eigenvalue rose elevenfold as accuracy fell, the pattern Keskar reported. But two confounds sit inside it. The large-batch runs were also undertrained, with a training loss six times higher at 2,048, so they are not minima at all, and sharpness is being compared at different points of training. And the batch-8,192 run, the least trained, was less sharp than 2,048. A known mechanism links the two: gradient descent with learning rate η cannot settle where the curvature exceeds 2/η (Chapter 8), so small, noisy steps are pushed out of sharp regions, and training at large learning rates ends near the stability edge (Cohen et al., 2021). The relationship between batch size and sharpness is real; that sharpness causes poor generalisation does not follow from it.

Mode connectivity

Two networks trained from different random seeds reach different weight vectors with almost identical accuracy, 88.88% and 88.34% here. Are they in separate valleys? Along the straight line between them, yes: the loss rose to 2.50 at the midpoint and accuracy fell to 12.0%, no better than chance. Garipov et al. (2018) and Draxler et al. (2018) showed that the valleys are nonetheless connected. Training a quadratic Bézier curve θ(t) = (1 − t)²θ_A + 2t(1 − t)θ_C + t²θ_B, optimising only its middle point θ_C so that random points along it have low loss, found a path between the same two solutions on which accuracy never fell below 81.8%.

The good solutions of a network form connected sets, not isolated points, which is part of why training from almost any initialisation succeeds. It also explains why averaging the weights of two independently trained networks fails (the midpoint is in the barrier), while averaging checkpoints from one run, or fine-tunes of one pretrained model, works: those lie in the same valley (Chapter 28).

Sharpness-aware minimisation

If flat minima generalise better, why not seek them directly? SAM (Foret et al., 2021) replaces each gradient step with two: first move to the worst point within a small radius ρ, w + ρ·g/‖g‖, then take the gradient there and apply it to the original weights. It minimises the highest loss in a neighbourhood rather than the loss at a point, at twice the cost per step:

loss = loss_fn(model(x), y); loss.backward()
e = [rho * p.grad / grad_norm for p in params]; add_(params, e)    # climb to the worst nearby point
opt.zero_grad(); loss_fn(model(x), y).backward()          # gradient there
sub_(params, e); opt.step()                                   # step from the original weights

SAM did exactly what it promises to the landscape, more than halving the top eigenvalue at ρ = 0.1, and it did not improve accuracy: both SAM runs were slightly worse than plain SGD, and spending SAM’s extra compute on more SGD epochs gave the best result by more than a point. Published gains from SAM are largest on large models with strong augmentation, long schedules and label noise; on a small network that is still under-fitting at 30 epochs, a smoother landscape was not what it needed. The landscape changed; generalisation did not follow.

A sharpness that means nothing

Dinh et al. (2017) pointed out a problem with curvature measures. A ReLU is positively homogeneous: multiplying the first layer’s weights and bias by α and dividing the second layer’s weights by α leaves every output unchanged. Done to the trained batch-128 network:

The same function, to the last digit of accuracy and loss, had a top eigenvalue anywhere from 15 to 79 depending on an arbitrary choice of scale. Any claim that a solution generalises well “because it is flat”, measured this way, has to survive that. The filter-normalised perturbation measure did not move at all, because it scales each direction to the weights it perturbs, which is why landscape studies use it.

🧪
Use case: merging two fine-tuned models

A team fine-tunes one pretrained image model on two related datasets and wants a single model for both. Averaging the two sets of weights works: both fine-tunes started from the same pretrained point and stayed in its valley, so their midpoint has low loss on both tasks. Encouraged, they try averaging two models trained from scratch with different seeds, and get chance accuracy, the barrier measured in this chapter. Separately, a colleague proposes SAM to make their production model “more robust”; they run it alongside equal-compute ordinary training on their real data before adopting it, and keep it only because it helps on their noisy-label slices.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Unnormalised random directions: scale invariance makes the picture depend on layer scales, not on the function.
  • Comparing sharpness of runs at different stages: the large-batch runs were undertrained, not in minima.
  • Equating flatness with generalisation: SAM halved curvature and lowered accuracy here.
  • Trusting the Hessian’s top eigenvalue alone: a function-preserving rescaling multiplied it by five.
  • Averaging independently trained networks: the midpoint sat in a barrier at 12% accuracy.
  • Comparing SAM with SGD at equal epochs: it costs two gradients per step; equal compute favoured SGD.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is the flat-minima hypothesis, and does it hold?

How a strong answer sounds
That solutions in wide, flat regions of the loss generalise better than those in narrow, sharp ones, because small shifts between training and test data change the loss less. There is evidence for a correlation: on a CIFAR CNN the top Hessian eigenvalue rose from 11 at batch 32 to 118 at batch 2,048 as accuracy fell. But it is confounded by training stage, and causation is weaker: sharpness-aware minimisation cut the eigenvalue from 15 to 7 and did not improve accuracy. And the measure itself is fragile: rescaling two layers without changing the function moved the top eigenvalue from 15 to 79, so flatness needs an invariant definition such as filter-normalised perturbations.

Deeper: What is mode connectivity, and why does it matter?

How a strong answer sounds
Two networks trained from different seeds end at different weights with similar accuracy. The straight line between them crosses a high-loss barrier: in my measurement accuracy fell to 12% at the midpoint. But a simple curved path, a Bézier curve with one trained middle point, connected them with accuracy above 81% everywhere, so good solutions form connected sets rather than isolated basins. Practically, it explains why averaging weights works for checkpoints of one run or fine-tunes of one pretrained model, which share a basin, and fails for independently trained models unless their neurons are aligned first.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Loss landscapes are seen through slices; use filter-normalised directions.
  • Initialisation to solution was downhill all the way; solution to solution crossed a barrier (12% accuracy).
  • A trained curve connected the two solutions above 81%: good minima are connected.
  • Larger batches ended sharper (eigenvalue 11 → 118) but were also undertrained; SAM made solutions flatter without making them better.
  • The Hessian’s top eigenvalue changed fivefold under a function-preserving rescaling; filter-normalised perturbations did not.
