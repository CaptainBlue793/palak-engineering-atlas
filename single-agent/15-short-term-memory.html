Short-Term Memory: Summaries & Compaction

A long session outgrows the context window, and something has to give. Short-term memory is the set of techniques for deciding what: keep only the recent turns, replace older turns with a summary, or have the agent take notes as it goes and keep those instead. Each keeps the window small. They differ in what they lose, and whether you find out.

This chapter measures four of them with qwen3:14b on three simulated incident sessions of about 28,000 tokens each: forty turns, mostly pasted logs, with ten facts stated along the way ("the on-call for db-main is Priya Raman", "the incident ticket is INC-4471", "use #inc-checkout for updates"). At the end, ten questions asked for those facts, against each kind of memory.

  • Explain why a long session needs compaction, and what happens without it
  • Use a sliding window, and know exactly what it forgets
  • Summarise history so facts survive and can still be found
  • Keep a scratchpad of notes alongside the conversation
  • Measure recall by where in the session a fact appeared

Sliding windows

The simplest memory keeps the last N turns and drops the rest. Here the window was the last eight exchanges, about 5,900 tokens. It remembered every fact stated in that stretch (9 of 9) and nothing before it: 9 of 30 overall, 0 of 21 earlier facts. The failure is predictable and silent; asked about the on-call for db-main, it answered "I do not know", which is at least honest. Keeping everything is not an alternative: the 28,000-token session exceeded the 16k window, the server dropped the start (Chapter 10), and recall was 18 of 30, with 0 of 11 facts from the first part of the session.

Summarising history

Compaction replaces old turns with a summary written by the model and keeps the recent turns in full. One call per session, about 20 seconds here, asking to "keep every decision, name, number, time, rule and instruction; drop log lines". The result scored 28 of 30, at about 6,300 prompt tokens. The two misses are the instructive part. In the second session the summariser kept a line for every service whose logs had been pasted ("Email logs: mostly normal traffic … nothing that changes the picture", ten such lines) and dropped two facts outright: the payments deploy freeze and the threshold for paging the CTO. Asked about them, the model said it did not know the first and invented "5%" for the second, where the session had said 25. The summariser was told to keep every rule and number, and still judged routine activity more worth keeping than a one-line rule.

def compact(turns: list, keep_recent: int = 16) -> list:
    old, recent = turns[:-keep_recent], turns[-keep_recent:]
    summary = ask("Summarise this part of an incident conversation for your own memory. Keep every decision, "
                  "name, number, time, rule and instruction; drop log lines and routine checks.", old)
    return [SYSTEM, {"role": "user", "content": "Summary of our conversation so far:\n" + summary},
            {"role": "assistant", "content": "Understood."}] + recent

Scratchpads and notes

Notes are written as the session goes: after each user message, a short model call decides whether it contains something worth remembering and, if so, writes one line. The notebook is kept in context instead of the old turns. It scored 30 of 30, every fact from every part of every session, at about 6,200 prompt tokens. It cost a small call per message (40 per session, 10 to 13 seconds in total) rather than one large call. The notebook was not clean (the note-taker sometimes copied a few log lines as if they were facts), but every real fact had its own line, written at the moment it was stated, instead of being one sentence in a paragraph written much later.

def maybe_note(message: str, notes: list[str]) -> None:
    n = ask("You keep an incident notebook. If the message contains a fact, decision, name, number, rule or "
            "instruction worth remembering, write it as one short note. Otherwise reply NONE.", message)
    if not n.upper().startswith("NONE"):
        notes.append(n)

What compaction loses

Three things, in this measurement. A window loses everything older than its edge, completely. A summary loses whatever the summariser judges less important at the moment it writes, and here it kept routine activity over two one-line rules; a fact it drops can then be replaced by an invented one. Notes lose what the note-taker judged unimportant at the time, which is the risk when a fact only becomes important later; here, every fact was stated as one, so none was missed. In real sessions, add an instruction to the summariser and the note-taker to prefer decisions and commitments over routine activity, cap the length of either memory, and test recall of early facts specifically.

Measuring recall

Measure memory the way this chapter did: plant known facts at known positions, ask about each, and break the score down by position. A single overall score hides the shape of the failure: "keep everything" and "sliding window" both looked passable on late facts and scored zero on early ones.

Use case: MemGPT and automatic compaction

MemGPT (Packer and colleagues, 2023) treated the context window like an operating system treats RAM: a fixed "main context" with the system prompt, a working-memory scratchpad and recent messages, and "external context" the agent could page information in from and out to through function calls, summarising old messages as the window filled. The project continued as Letta. Commercial agents adopted the same moves: coding agents such as Claude Code summarise the conversation automatically when the context nears its limit, and also let the user trigger compaction by hand, while keeping a to-do list and notes that survive it. The designs share the lesson measured here: a summary alone is not enough for facts that must be recalled exactly; they need a place of their own.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • No compaction, so the server truncates the start of long sessions.
  • A sliding window for sessions where early facts matter.
  • Summaries that keep everything, burying facts among routine activity.
  • Testing memory with one overall score instead of recall by position.
  • Notes with no cap or clean-up, slowly filling with noise.
  • Trusting numbers read back from a summary without checking the source.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How would you keep a long agent session within the context window without forgetting important things?

How a strong answer sounds
Several layers. Keep the recent turns verbatim, because the next step usually needs them. Keep a notebook of facts, decisions and instructions, written as they come up, one line each, and keep it in context. When the transcript passes a budget, summarise older turns, telling the summariser to keep decisions and numbers and drop routine activity. In a measured long session, a sliding window recalled 9 of 30 facts, a summary 28, and notes 30, at the same context size.

Deeper: After compaction your agent "forgets" a rule the user stated early, and once invents a different value for it. Why, and what would you change?

How a strong answer sounds
The summariser dropped it. Summaries are written long after a rule was stated, by a model deciding what matters in a large block of text, and a rule stated once can lose to many lines of routine activity. In a measured case the summary kept a line for each of ten services' logs and dropped a deploy freeze and a paging threshold; asked about the threshold, the agent invented 5% where the session had said 25. I would keep rules and commitments in a separate notes section, written when they are stated and never rewritten by compaction, tell the summariser to drop routine activity, and test recall of early facts after compaction.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Without compaction the server truncated the start: 0 of 11 early facts recalled.
  • A sliding window forgets everything older than its edge: 9 of 30.
  • A summary kept 28 of 30; it dropped two one-line rules while keeping ten lines of routine log reviews, and the model invented a value for one.
  • Notes written as facts arrived recalled 30 of 30 at the same context size.
  • Measure recall by position; overall scores hide what is forgotten.
