Evaluating LLM Systems

With a classifier you just compute accuracy. With a system that writes text, there's no single right answer. Humans are slow and expensive to ask. Public benchmarks are contaminated (the test questions leaked into training data). And if you use another model as the judge, it has opinions of its own. Evaluation is the hardest engineering problem in applied LLM work, and it decides whether you can ship with confidence.

  • Build a golden set from real traffic that actually discriminates
  • Pick the cheapest grader that measures what you care about
  • Use LLM judges without inheriting their biases
  • Size an eval set and know when a difference is real
  • Run regression evals in CI and keep them honest over time

Three layers of evaluation

Why do you need an eval at all? Because an LLM system changes constantly: you edit a prompt, swap a model, change how documents are chunked. Each change fixes something you were looking at and can quietly break something you weren't. An eval is a fixed set of test cases plus a way of scoring them, so you can re-run it after every change and see what moved. Think of it as the unit-test suite for behaviour that has no single right answer.

Evaluation happens at three levels. They answer different questions, so you need all three. Benchmarks answer “which models are worth trying?”, task evals answer “is this change better for my product?”, and production evaluation answers “is the live system still doing well for real users?”

🏆

Capability benchmarks

Public test sets like MMLU, GSM8K and HumanEval, plus arena rankings. Useful for picking a base model, almost useless for your specific task, and often contaminated (Chapter 16).

🎯

Task evals

Your own set of test cases (the golden set), your scoring rules (the rubric), your prompts. This is the number you make decisions with, and nobody can build it for you.

📡

Production evaluation

Ongoing grading of a sample of live traffic, plus indirect signals: thumbs up/down, escalations, retries, edits. It's the only measurement of the system users actually meet.

📉
A public benchmark score is not a product decision

Two models three points apart on a leaderboard often swap places on your specific task. And contamination pushes headline numbers up, never down. Use benchmarks to make a shortlist. Use your own evals to choose. Without a task eval, you're shipping on gut feeling, and gut feeling leans toward whatever example you looked at most recently.

Building a golden set

A golden set is your curated list of test inputs, each paired with either a reference answer or a description of what a good answer must contain. It is “golden” because you trust it: every item was checked by someone who knows the subject. Every decision later in this chapter rests on it, so a weak golden set makes every score meaningless.

Here is what one item might look like for a support bot: the input is “Can I return shoes I wore once?”; the retrieved source is the returns policy; the expected behaviour is “says unworn items only, links the policy, does not promise an exception”. Notice it describes what must be true rather than an exact wording, because many different sentences would be correct.

PropertyTargetWhy
SourceReal questions from production, sampled across different times and user groupsMade-up questions are consistently easier and better worded than real ones

Why the difficulty mix matters, in numbers: suppose 90% of your set is easy and every reasonable version passes those. Then all versions score between 90% and 100%, and a change that doubles the error rate on hard cases (say from 30% to 60% of the hard 10%) only moves the total by 3 points. The signal you care about is buried. Keeping a real share of hard and tricky items is what lets the score move when quality moves.

“Tricky or unanswerable” items deserve a word. These are questions where the right behaviour is to say “I don't know”, ask for clarification, or refuse, for example a question about a product you don't sell. Systems love to answer these confidently and wrongly, so if the set has none, you will never notice.

🐛
Every bug report becomes an eval case

This one habit pays off faster than any tool. A user complains, you reproduce it, you add the case with the behaviour you expect. Now it can never come back unnoticed. After six months you have an eval set shaped exactly like your product's real failures. No benchmark can give you that.

Choosing a grader

Once you have test cases, something has to decide whether each answer is good. That something is the grader. Graders trade off cost, speed and how well they match human judgement. The rule of thumb: use the cheapest grader that actually measures the thing you care about, and only move up the list when it can't.

✅ ProgrammaticCode checks the answer: exact match, a pattern (regex), valid JSON, unit tests, running the SQL, or checking the cited source exists. Free, always gives the same verdict, no arguments. Use it wherever you can. More tasks allow this than people assume.
📏 Reference-basedCompares word overlap with a gold answer (ROUGE/BLEU), or embedding similarity. Cheap but weak: for open-ended text it matches human judgement poorly.
🧑‍⚖️ LLM judge (rubric)Another model scores the answer against stated criteria and explains why. Cheap, fast, scales. It's also biased in known ways you must correct for.
⚖️ LLM judge (pairwise)“Which response is better, A or B?” More reliable than giving a score out of 10, and it directly tells you how often the new version beats the current one.
👥 HumanThe real answer for anything subjective. Slow, expensive, and inconsistent unless you write good guidelines and measure how often raters agree (Chapter 3).
📈 Indirect production signalsThumbs, copies, edits, escalations, retries, people abandoning the session. Free and plentiful. Biased and late, but an excellent alarm.

A few examples of turning a “fuzzy” task into a code check. A support bot that must cite sources: check that every cited document ID exists in the retrieved set. A text-to-SQL feature: run the generated query and compare the returned rows with the expected rows. An extraction task: parse the JSON and compare each field. A summariser usually can't be checked by code alone, but you can still check parts of it by code, such as “under 120 words” or “mentions the order number”, and leave only the judgement calls to a model or a human.

Why do word-overlap scores like ROUGE fail on open-ended text? Because they count shared words. “Your refund was approved” and “We've accepted your return and the money is on its way” mean nearly the same thing but share almost no words, so they score badly. Meanwhile a wrong answer that copies the reference's phrasing scores well.

When you do use an LLM judge, give it a rubric: a short list of named criteria, each with a small score scale. Here is a real-shaped example for a support assistant:

You are grading a customer-support answer against the retrieved sources. Score each dimension 0–2 and give one sentence of justification: grounded every factual claim is supported by a cited source complete addresses all parts of the customer's question actionable the customer can do something with it concise no padding, no restating the question safe no promises about refunds/policy beyond the sources Then output JSON only: {"grounded":n,"complete":n,"actionable":n,"concise":n,"safe":n,"why":"..."} Grade the ANSWER against the SOURCES only. Do not use outside knowledge. Ignore writing style, length and formatting unless a criterion mentions them.

Specific criteria plus a fixed output format beat “rate this 1–10”. The instruction to ignore length is there on purpose: judges reward long answers unless you tell them not to.

Why 0–2 per criterion instead of one 1–10 score? A 1–10 scale asks the judge to invent fine distinctions (is this a 6 or a 7?) that it can't make consistently, so the same answer drifts between runs. Three levels (“no / partly / yes”) per named criterion are easy to apply the same way every time. Separate criteria also tell you what got worse: a drop in “grounded” points to retrieval or hallucination, a drop in “concise” points to the prompt.

LLM judges have biases — measure them

An LLM judge is itself a model, with habits learned from its training. Those habits make it prefer some answers for reasons that have nothing to do with quality. The danger is that a bias of 7 points looks exactly like a real 7-point improvement in your results. So before trusting a judge, you measure its biases, the same way you would calibrate a scale before weighing things on it.

The simplest test: take pairs of answers that humans rated as equally good and ask the judge to compare them. An unbiased judge should pick A about 50% of the time. If it picks A 62% of the time when A is shown first, you have a 12-point position bias. The four rules for LLM judges
  • Swap and average. Grade (A,B) and then (B,A). If the winner flips, call it a tie. This removes nearly all of the bias toward whichever answer came first.
  • Check it against humans. Have people grade 100–200 of the same items and report how often the judge agrees. An unchecked judge is a random number generator that writes well.
  • Prefer A-vs-B to scores. “Better than the current version?” is far more stable than “score out of 10”, and it's the decision you actually need.
  • Don't let a model grade its own relatives unchecked. Models really do prefer text that sounds like their own. Use a different model as judge, or measure the bias.

How the swap works on a small example. You judge 4 pairs in both orders:

PairOrder (A,B) winnerOrder (B,A) winnerFinal verdict
1AAA wins
2ABTie (the verdict followed the position)
3BBB wins
4ABTie

Judging only in order (A,B) would have said A wins 3 of 4. After swapping, A has 1 win, B has 1 win and there are 2 ties: a 50% win rate if you count a tie as half a win each. The “flips” are exactly the cases where the judge was reacting to position rather than content. A high flip rate is also a warning sign in itself: it means the judge can't really tell the answers apart on this task.

“Agreement with humans” is measured the same way as agreement between two human raters: have people grade a sample, have the judge grade the same sample, and count how often they match. As a reference point, if two careful humans agree 80% of the time, a judge that matches them 78% of the time is about as good as another human rater. A judge at 60% on a pass/fail task is barely better than a coin flip.

How big does the eval set need to be?

Every score from a finite eval set is noisy. If you re-drew 200 different questions from the same traffic, the pass rate would come out a little different each time. The confidence interval (Chapter 7) tells you how big that wobble is. The rough formula for a pass rate p on n items is ±1.96 × √(p(1−p)/n) for a 95% interval.

Worked example: your system passes 72% of 200 items. Then p(1−p) = 0.72 × 0.28 ≈ 0.20, divided by 200 gives 0.001, and the square root is about 0.032. Multiply by 1.96 and you get about ±6 points. So the “true” pass rate could plausibly be anywhere from 66% to 78%. A new version scoring 75% on this set has not shown it is better.

The needed size grows with the square of how fine a difference you want to see: halving the detectable difference needs four times as many items. That's why detecting a 5-point improvement at 72% takes over a thousand items when you compare two separate samples, while a 15-point jump shows up with around 150. Comparing both systems on the same items needs far fewer examples than testing them on two separate samples. Always run both systems on the identical eval set and compare item by item.

Why is item-by-item (“paired”) comparison so much more sensitive? Because most items get the same verdict from both versions: easy items both pass, impossible items both fail. Those tell you nothing about the difference, and in a paired comparison they cancel out. Only the discordant items count, the ones where one version passed and the other failed. If out of 200 items the new version fixes 18 and breaks 6, the evidence is “18 vs 6 among 24 changed items”, which is much clearer than comparing two noisy totals. Looking at those changed items by hand is also the fastest way to learn what the change did.

A bootstrap is the practical way to get an interval without formulas: resample your items with replacement a thousand times, recompute the difference each time, and read off the middle 95% of the results. It works for any metric, including averages of rubric scores.

Evals in CI

CI (continuous integration) is the system that runs checks automatically every time someone proposes a code change. Putting evals there means nobody has to remember to run them, and a prompt edit that breaks something gets caught before it merges rather than after users complain. The trick is to tier the evals by cost: fast and cheap checks on every commit, slower and pricier ones less often.

on: pull_request
  fast-evals:       80 items, programmatic graders only     ~40 s   ← every commit
  judge-evals:      250 items, pairwise vs production        ~6 min  ← on PR label
  full-suite:       1,200 items + slices + safety set        ~35 min ← nightly & pre-release
  human-review:     50 sampled production answers            weekly

gates: no regression >2% on any capability slice
       safety set: zero new failures (hard block)
       parse-failure rate < 0.5%

Reading the config: each tier is a trade between speed and coverage. The 80-item fast tier finishes in under a minute, so developers never wait for it. The full suite takes over half an hour and costs real money in judge calls, so it runs nightly and before a release. The gates are the rules that block a merge. Notice they look at slices (subsets such as “billing questions” or “Spanish”), not just the overall average. A change can raise the average by 1 point while dropping one slice by 8, and the slice gate is what catches it. The safety gate is absolute because a single new harmful answer matters more than any average.

🧭
Eval-driven development

Write the eval before you make the change. It forces you to say what “better” means. It turns a prompt tweak into a measurable experiment. And it stops the usual cycle of fixing one case while quietly breaking three others. Teams that do this ship prompt and model changes every week with confidence. Teams that don't end up with a system nobody dares to touch.

Keeping evals honest

An eval set decays. Your product changes, your users change, the judge model gets updated, and the team slowly learns which prompts please the eval. None of this is visible from the score itself, which keeps looking fine. The table lists the usual ways an eval stops telling the truth, and what to do about each.

RiskSymptomMitigation
SaturationEverything scores 97%, so changes make no visible differenceRemove items everything passes; add harder ones from real production failures
Tuning too hard to the evalThe eval score improves but users don't notice anythingKeep a rotating slice you never use while iterating
ContaminationSuspiciously strong results on public test setsKeep your set private; check whether test text appears in training data
Judge driftScores shift when the judge model is updatedPin the judge's version. Re-baseline on purpose, and write down when you did
Distribution driftThe eval no longer looks like real production trafficResample from recent traffic every quarter
Grading the wrong thingHigh scores, unhappy usersCheck whether eval scores track real outcomes. If they don't move together, the eval is wrong

The last row is the one that matters most, and it has a concrete check. Every month, take the weeks where eval scores went up or down and see whether a real outcome moved with them, such as thumbs-down rate or escalations to a human. If the eval rose 6 points over a quarter while escalations stayed flat, either the gains are in areas users don't touch or the eval is rewarding the wrong thing. Either way, the eval needs work before you trust the next number.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • No task eval at all. Then every change becomes an argument about individual examples.
  • Trusting an unchecked LLM judge. Measure how often it agrees with humans before you believe a single number from it.
  • Ignoring position bias. Always swap A and B and average, or your win rates partly measure the ordering.
  • Only testing the cases that go well. Include unanswerable, tricky and non-English cases, or they'll break in production.
  • Comparing on different eval sets. Run both systems on identical items and compare item by item.
  • Treating a 2-point move on 100 items as real. That's within the noise. Report a confidence interval.
  • Publishing your eval set. It becomes training data and stops measuring anything.

Interview drill

Warm-up: How would you evaluate a customer-support assistant?

How a strong answer sounds
In layers, cheapest first. Code checks where possible: did it cite a source that actually exists, did it produce valid structured output, did it stay within policy, did it refuse when the sources don't contain the answer. Then a rubric-based LLM judge on groundedness, completeness and usefulness, checked against 150 human-graded items so I know how often it agrees. Then an A-vs-B win rate against the current production version, on a golden set sampled from real tickets, including unanswerable and tricky ones. In production: weekly human review of a sample, plus indirect signals as the alarm: escalation rate, thumbs-down, reopened tickets. The offline suite gates releases. The production signals tell me whether the offline suite still measures the right thing.

Deeper: Your judge says the new prompt wins 61% of comparisons. Do you ship?

How a strong answer sounds
Not yet. First, is 61% outside the noise for this sample size? On 200 items the 95% interval is roughly ±7 points, so it's probably real, but not comfortably. Second, was position bias removed: were comparisons run in both orders and averaged? Third, how often does this judge agree with humans on this task? If agreement is 70%, a 61% win rate means much less than it sounds. Fourth, look at the losses, not the wins. 39% of cases got worse. If those cluster in one capability or one language, that's a regression I'd have to fix whatever the average says. Then check the guardrails: length, cost, latency, refusal rate. If all that holds, ship behind a canary and confirm with production signals. Don't treat the offline number as the decision.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Benchmarks narrow the shortlist. Your own task eval makes the decision.
  • Build the golden set from real traffic, keep it private, and add every bug report to it.
  • Prefer code-based graders. When you use an LLM judge, compare A vs B, swap the order, and check it against humans.
  • Report confidence intervals. Small eval sets can't see small differences.
  • Run fast evals in CI, full suites nightly, and keep a steady trickle of human review on real traffic.

Check your understanding

Your LLM judge rates response A higher when it is shown first. What's the standard fix?

This bias toward whatever comes first is well documented. Swapping the order removes most of it, and only costs one extra judging pass.

Which grader should you prefer when the task allows it?

It gives the same verdict every time, costs nothing, leaves no room for argument, and runs instantly. Far more tasks allow a code-based check than teams first assume.

On a 100-item eval, version B scores 3 points higher. What can you conclude?

With 100 items, the 95% interval is roughly ±9 points (at a 72% pass rate: 1.96 × √(0.72 × 0.28 / 100) ≈ 0.088). Compare item by item and use a bootstrap (resampling to estimate the noise), or use a larger set.

Why should a golden eval set stay private?

Contamination is why headline benchmark numbers creep up without matching real-world gains. Your private set is the only one you can trust over time.

Your eval scores keep improving but users are no happier. What's the most likely problem?

Regularly check whether eval scores move with real outcomes. If they don't, the eval is what needs fixing. Also keep a rotating slice you never tune against.
