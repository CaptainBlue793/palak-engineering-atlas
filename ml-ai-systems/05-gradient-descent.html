Learning by Gradient Descent

One algorithm trains almost everything: predict, measure how wrong you were, nudge the parameters downhill, repeat. Build it here on a straight line. Training GPT-sized models is the same loop, just with far more parameters and far more machinery around it.

The smallest possible model

Why start with something this small? Because every model you'll meet later — trees aside — is trained the same way: guess some numbers, measure the error, adjust the numbers to shrink it. A straight line has only two numbers to adjust, so you can see every step with your own eyes. Once the loop makes sense here, a neural network is “the same loop, more numbers”.

Let's predict house price from size: ŷ = w·x + b. That's two parameters. w is how much the price moves per square metre. b is where the line crosses zero. Training means finding the w, b pair that is least wrong across all your data.

The little hat on ŷ (say “y-hat”) marks it as the model's guess, while plain y is the real answer from the data. For example, with w = 3 (thousand per square metre) and b = 50 (thousand), a 100 m² flat gets a predicted price of 3·100 + 50 = 350 thousand. If it actually sold for 320, the model was 30 too high. That gap, ŷ − y, is called the residual or error.

“Least wrong” needs a number. For predicting a quantity (regression), the default is mean squared error:

MSE = (1/n) · Σ (ŷᵢ − yᵢ)²      # average squared residual

Squaring does two useful things. It removes the sign, so being 5 too high is as bad as 5 too low. And it punishes big misses much more than small ones. It also has a smooth slope everywhere, which is what lets us use calculus on it.

A tiny worked example. Three houses, and a model that predicts 350, 200 and 410 when the true prices are 320, 210 and 400. The residuals are +30, −10 and +10. Squared, they become 900, 100 and 100. The average is 1100 / 3 ≈ 367, so MSE ≈ 367. Notice how the one miss of 30 contributes 900 of the 1100 — three times the error, nine times the penalty. That's what “punishes big misses” means in practice. If you'd rather not let outliers dominate like this, mean absolute error (average of |ŷ − y|) treats every unit of error equally.

Where the update rule comes from

You want to know how the loss changes when you nudge each parameter — its derivative. For MSE with one feature, that comes out in two lines:

Here's where those lines come from, one step at a time. Each example adds e² to the loss, where e = w·x + b − y. By the chain rule, the derivative of e² is 2e times the derivative of e itself. Nudging w by a little changes e by x times that little (because w is multiplied by x), so ∂e/∂w = x. Nudging b changes e by exactly the same amount, so ∂e/∂b = 1. Put those together and average over the n examples, and you get the two gradient lines below. The minus sign in the update is there because the gradient points uphill — toward more loss — and you want to go the other way.

# prediction and error for one example
ŷ = w·x + b
e = ŷ − y

# how the loss changes as w and b change
∂MSE/∂w = (2/n) · Σ e·x      # errors weighted by the input
∂MSE/∂b = (2/n) · Σ e        # just the mean error

# the step: move against the gradient
w ← w − η · ∂MSE/∂w
b ← b − η · ∂MSE/∂b

One step by hand. Take two examples with scaled inputs: (x=1, y=3) and (x=2, y=5). Start at w = 0, b = 0 with η = 0.1.

  1. Predictions are 0 and 0, so the errors are e = −3 and e = −5. MSE = (9 + 25) / 2 = 17.
  2. ∂MSE/∂w = (2/2) · (−3·1 + −5·2) = −13. ∂MSE/∂b = (2/2) · (−3 + −5) = −8. Both negative: raising either parameter lowers the loss.
  3. Update: w = 0 − 0.1·(−13) = 1.3 and b = 0 − 0.1·(−8) = 0.8.
  4. New predictions are 2.1 and 3.4, errors −0.9 and −1.6, and MSE = (0.81 + 2.56) / 2 ≈ 1.69. One step cut the loss from 17 to about 1.7.

Repeat that a few hundred times and w, b settle near 2, 1, the line that passes through both points. That is all the live trainer above is doing, just with 40 points instead of 2.

💡
Read the gradient, don't memorise it

Σ e·x says this: if the examples where we predict too high tend to have a large x, then w is too big. That's the whole idea. Every gradient in deep learning has this flavour — “how much did this parameter contribute to the error, and in which direction?”

The learning rate is the whole game

η (eta) sets how big each step is. It's the setting you'll tune more than any other. Once you've seen its failure modes, you can spot them at a glance.

You can see all three behaviours on the simplest bowl there is: loss L = w², lowest at w = 0. Its gradient is 2w, so the update is w ← w − η·2w = w·(1 − 2η). Start at w = 10:

  • η = 0.01: each step multiplies w by 0.98. After 10 steps you're still at about 8.2. Correct direction, painfully slow.
  • η = 0.3: each step multiplies by 0.4. After 10 steps w is about 0.001. Fast and smooth.
  • η = 1.1: each step multiplies by −1.2. w goes 10, −12, 14.4, −17.3… It flips sides and grows every step — the “jumps over the valley” failure, which ends in overflow and NaN.

Real loss surfaces aren't this tidy, but the lesson carries over: there is a threshold (here η = 1) above which each step overshoots by more than it corrects, and training diverges.

🐌

Too small

The loss falls, but painfully slowly. You'll decide the model can't learn, when really you just didn't wait. At scale this wastes weeks of GPU time.

✅

Just right

A fast drop, then a smooth flattening. Usually it's the largest rate that doesn't cause spikes. You find it with a short learning-rate range test (Chapter 9).

💥

Too large

Each step jumps over the valley and lands higher up the other side. The loss bounces around, then becomes NaN (not a number). A classic first-day training failure.

📐
Unscaled features make η impossible to choose

Say rooms goes from 1 to 6 and square_metres goes from 20 to 400. The loss surface is then a long, narrow canyon. A step size that's safe along the steep wall is far too small along the gentle floor, so training zig-zags. Standardizing features (Chapter 4) turns that canyon into a round bowl. It's the single most valuable preparation step for gradient-based models.

Full batch, mini-batch, or one at a time?

The gradient computed over the whole dataset is the “true” downhill direction. But computing it over 400 million examples for every single step is absurd. In practice you estimate it from a mini-batch — a small random sample of examples.

This works for the same reason opinion polls work: the average over a random sample of 1,000 people is a decent estimate of the average over millions. A mini-batch gradient is a noisy but unbiased guess at the full gradient. Two words you'll see everywhere: a step (or iteration) is one parameter update from one batch, and an epoch is one full pass over the training data. With 1,000,000 examples and a batch size of 100, one epoch is 10,000 steps — versus a single step per epoch with full batch. (The trainer above bumps its counter on every update, so in mini-batch and SGD mode it's really counting steps.)

ModeGradient per stepProsCons
Full batchAll n examplesExact direction, smooth loss curveOnly one step per pass through the data — hopeless at scale, and it won't fit in memory
Mini-batch (32–4M tokens)A random subsetUses the hardware well, and the randomness helps escape poor solutionsBatch size has to be tuned together with the learning rate, and it affects how well the model generalises
Stochastic (n=1)One exampleUpdates straight away; handy for learning from a live streamVery jumpy, and it leaves the GPU mostly idle

When to pick which. Full batch only makes sense when the whole dataset fits in memory and is small — a few thousand rows, say, in a classroom or a quick analysis. One-at-a-time updates fit online learning, where examples arrive as a stream (clicks, transactions) and you want the model to adapt immediately. For almost everything else, including every neural network you'll train, use mini-batches: start with something like 32–256 examples and grow it until your GPU memory is well used.

⚖️
Batch size in one sentence

Bigger batches give a steadier gradient, so you can use a bigger learning rate. A common rule is to scale η roughly with the batch size, or with its square root. But past a point, a bigger batch makes each step only slightly better while costing a lot more compute. Chapter 23 shows how this shapes the design of distributed training.

From regression to classification: logistic regression

Now let's predict a probability instead of a quantity. Keep the same straight-line core. Squeeze the result through a sigmoid (an S-shaped curve) so it lands between 0 and 1. Then swap the loss for cross-entropy:

z = w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + b
p = σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e^(−z))          # probability of class 1
L = −[ y·log(p) + (1−y)·log(1−p) ]    # binary cross-entropy

∂L/∂w = (p − y) · x                   # astonishingly, the same shape as before

That last line isn't a coincidence. When you pair sigmoid with cross-entropy (or softmax with its multi-class version), the gradient simplifies to (prediction − truth) × input. That neat result is why these pairings are the standard choice.

Some numbers make both pieces concrete. The sigmoid turns z = 0 into p = 0.5, z = 2 into about 0.88, and z = −2 into about 0.12. Big positive scores approach 1, big negative scores approach 0, and it never quite reaches either. Now the loss, for an example whose true label is y = 1 (only the −log(p) term survives):

Model says pLoss −log(p)Gradient p − yWhat it means
0.90.11−0.1Right and confident: tiny loss, tiny nudge
0.50.69−0.5Unsure: moderate loss, moderate nudge
0.014.61−0.99Confidently wrong: big loss, the largest possible nudge

The loss grows without limit as p heads to 0 for a true positive, so a confident mistake is punished hard. That's also why real code clamps p slightly away from 0 and 1 (or works on the raw score z directly): log(0) is minus infinity, and one such value turns the whole loss into NaN.

Regularization: preferring simpler fits

Nothing stops the optimizer from pushing weights to huge values to squeeze out the last drop of training loss. That's overfitting: the model memorises the training data instead of learning the pattern (Chapter 7). Regularization adds a penalty for complexity right into the thing you're minimising.

L2 (ridge / weight decay)

Add λ·Σw² to the loss. This shrinks every weight smoothly toward zero. It's the default for neural networks, where it's usually built into the optimizer as weight decay.

L1 (lasso)

Add λ·Σ|w|. This pushes some weights to exactly zero, so it picks features for you. Useful when you want a small, explainable model.

λ (lambda) is a dial for how much you care about simplicity versus fitting the training data. λ = 0 switches regularization off. A huge λ makes the cheapest option “all weights zero”, which fits nothing. You tune it like the learning rate: try a few values spread by factors of 10 (0.0001, 0.001, 0.01…) and keep the one with the best validation score.

Why do big weights mean overfitting? A large weight makes the prediction swing a lot when that input changes a little. A model with huge weights can bend itself to pass through every noisy training point, including the ones that are just luck. Small weights force smoother, calmer predictions that tend to hold up on new data. To see how L2 enters the update, note that the derivative of λw² is 2λw. So each step subtracts a small fraction of every weight on top of the usual gradient — the weight literally “decays” a little each step, hence the name. Rule of thumb: reach for L2 by default; pick L1 when you have many features, suspect most are useless, and want the model to tell you which ones matter.

🎒
Analogy: packing for a trip

The loss says “bring whatever makes the trip perfect”. Regularization says “every item costs you weight in the bag”. L2 makes you pack lighter versions of everything. L1 makes you leave some items behind completely. Both stop you carrying a whole wardrobe for every possible occasion — which is exactly what an overfitted model does.

What goes wrong, and what it looks like

Almost everything you learn about a training run comes from one picture: the loss curve, plotted against steps. Each failure below leaves its own recognisable shape on that curve, so learning to read it saves you from guessing. Always plot it on a log scale too — a curve that looks flat on a normal scale is often still falling steadily.

SymptomMost likely causeFix

🔬
The single best debugging trick in ML

Overfit a tiny subset first. Take 16 examples and train until the loss is about zero. If a model can't memorise 16 examples, no amount of data or tuning will save it. You have a bug in the data pipeline, the loss, or how labels line up with inputs. This check takes two minutes and saves days.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Tuning the learning rate before scaling the features. You're working around a problem you could have removed.
  • Using MSE for classification. It gives tiny gradients when the model is confidently wrong — exactly when you need a big correction. Use cross-entropy.
  • Forgetting to reset gradients to zero between steps. Some frameworks add them up, so your update ends up using the sum of every batch so far.
  • Judging a run by training loss alone. The only number that matters comes from data the model has never seen.
  • Assuming that a settled loss means a correct model. Gradient descent will happily settle on a beautiful fit to a leaked feature.
🎤
Interview drill

Warm-up: Your loss goes to NaN in the first 100 steps. Walk me through the diagnosis.

How a strong answer sounds
First, reproduce it with a fixed random seed and log the loss at every step, so I can see whether it blows up gradually or jumps to NaN at once. An instant NaN usually means bad input data: NaNs or infinities in features, a divide-by-zero in normalization, or a log of zero. A gradual explosion means the learning rate is too high or gradients aren't capped. So: check the batch's input statistics, lower the learning rate by 10×, add gradient clipping, make sure the loss is computed in a numerically safe way (use the cross-entropy that takes raw logits, not the log of a softmax output), and add a warmup period. If it survives at a tiny learning rate, it's a rate or stability problem. If it still goes NaN, it's the data.

Deeper: Why does mini-batch noise sometimes improve final quality rather than just being a compute compromise?

How a strong answer sounds
The noise acts like a mild form of regularization. It stops the model settling into a narrow, sharp valley that fits one batch exactly but works badly on new data, and it helps the run escape flat regions where progress stalls. In practice, very large batches with an unchanged schedule often generalise slightly worse. That's why large-batch training goes together with scaling the learning rate, a warmup, and sometimes extra regularization. It's a trade-off: some noise helps generalisation, but too much just wastes steps.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Training = model + loss + optimizer. The loop is predict → measure → step downhill → repeat.
  • The gradient answers “how much did this parameter contribute to the error?”. The learning rate is the stride length.
  • Standardize features before you tune the learning rate. It reshapes the loss surface into something easier to walk down.
  • Mini-batches trade some gradient accuracy for much better hardware use, and the extra noise slightly helps generalisation.
  • Logistic regression is the same machinery plus a sigmoid and cross-entropy. The gradient is still (prediction − truth) × input.

Check your understanding

Your training loss oscillates wildly and occasionally jumps upward. What's the most likely single cause?

The steps jump over the lowest point and land on the opposite slope. Halve the learning rate (or add a warmup and let it shrink over time) and it usually settles straight away.

Why is cross-entropy preferred over MSE for classification?

With MSE and a sigmoid, a confidently wrong prediction sits at the flat end of the S-curve, where the gradient almost disappears. So the model learns slowest exactly when it's most wrong. Cross-entropy fixes that.

Feature A ranges 0–1 and feature B ranges 0–100,000, and you train a linear model with a single learning rate. What happens?

The loss surface becomes a narrow canyon. Any step size that's safe along the steep direction is far too small for the gentle one. Standardize first.

You use L1 instead of L2 regularization. What is the most distinctive effect?

L1 pushes with the same force no matter how small a weight is, so small weights get driven all the way to zero. You end up with a model that uses only some features — automatic feature selection.

A new model's loss is completely flat from step 1. What should you try first?

If the model can't get the loss near zero on just 16 examples, you have a bug: labels not lined up with inputs, gradients not flowing, a frozen layer, or a learning rate of zero. It isn't a lack of data or time.
