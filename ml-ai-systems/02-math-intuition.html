The Math You Actually Need

You don't need a maths degree to build ML systems. You need four ideas — vectors, matrices, derivatives and probability. And it helps most to hold them as pictures, not symbols. This chapter builds those pictures, and you can drag most of them.

  • Read a dot product as “how much do these two things agree?”
  • See a matrix as a machine that transforms vectors, and get its shapes right
  • Understand a derivative as a slope, and a gradient as the direction downhill
  • Make sense of probabilities, likelihood and cross-entropy loss
  • Fix the error you'll hit most often: a shape mismatch
📚

  • What log does: it turns multiplying into adding, and log(1) = 0. P6: Logs, Exponentials & Softmax covers logs and exponentials.

Everything is a vector

A vector is just a list of numbers in a fixed order. A user might be [age, days_since_signup, orders, avg_basket]. A word can be a list of 1,536 numbers made by an embedding model. A 224×224 colour image is 150,528 numbers. Once something is a vector, you can treat it as a point or arrow in space. That geometry is where your intuition comes from.

🧭
Analogy: directions on a map

Two vectors pointing the same way are “about the same thing”, even if one is much longer. That's the whole idea behind embedding similarity: direction carries meaning, length carries intensity. “king” and “queen” point in similar directions. “king” and “photosynthesis” do not.

The dot product: agreement in one number

The dot product multiplies matching entries and adds them up: a·b = a₁b₁ + a₂b₂ + …. A big positive result means “these agree”. Zero means “unrelated”. Negative means “they point opposite ways”.

Why "agreement"? Look at the recipe. A pair of entries adds to the total when both are positive or both negative, and subtracts when they disagree in sign. Say a user's tastes are [action 3, romance 0, comedy 1]. An action-comedy film [2, 0, 1] scores 3×2 + 0×0 + 1×1 = 7. A pure romance [0, 3, 0] scores 0: nothing the user cares about is in it. A film scored [−1, 0, 0] ("anti-action") scores −3. That is, in miniature, how many recommenders rank items: one dot product per item.

If you divide by both vectors' lengths, you get cosine similarity. It ignores size and keeps only direction. It's the score behind every vector database in Chapter 20.

Why divide by length? Take q = [1, 1]. The vectors [2, 2] and [10, 10] point exactly the same way as q, yet their dot products with it are 4 and 20. The raw dot product rewards being long, not just being aligned. After dividing by the lengths, both give a cosine of exactly 1, meaning "same direction". Cosine always lands between −1 and 1, which makes scores comparable across items.

Where you'll meet this

A single neuron computes dot(weights, inputs) + bias. Attention compares a query with every key using a dot product. A vector index ranks documents by cosine similarity. Learn to read a·b as “how strongly does this pattern match this input?” and half of deep learning stops looking like a wall of symbols.

Matrices: many dot products at once

A matrix is a stack of vectors, like rows in a table. Multiplying by a matrix does a whole batch of dot products in one go. That's all it is. A linear layer with 512 inputs and 256 outputs is a 512×256 matrix. Each of the 256 outputs is one dot product with the input.

A tiny version makes this visible. Let W have two rows, [1, 0, 1] and [0, 2, 0], and let x = [3, 1, 2]. The first output is the first row dotted with x: 1×3 + 0×1 + 1×2 = 5. The second is 0×3 + 2×1 + 0×2 = 2. So Wx = [5, 2]. Read each row as a small "pattern detector": row one asks "how much of feature 1 plus feature 3 is there?", row two asks "how much of feature 2, doubled?". A layer with 256 rows is 256 detectors run at once. Learning a layer means learning what each detector looks for.

The rule that causes 80% of your errors

For A·B, the inner dimensions must match: (m×k)·(k×n) → (m×n). Say you see the error mat1 and mat2 shapes cannot be multiplied (32x512 and 256x512). That means a matrix needs flipping (transposing), or your batch dimension is in the wrong place. Read the shapes left to right and say them out loud.

Derivatives: which way is downhill?

Training is a search for the parameter values that make the loss small. The derivative tells you the slope of the loss where you're standing right now. If the slope is positive, moving right makes the loss bigger — so move left.

A gradient is that same slope, worked out for millions of parameters at once. It's a vector pointing in the direction where loss rises fastest. You step the opposite way.

One step with real numbers. Take the loss L(w) = (w − 3)², which is smallest at w = 3. Its slope is 2(w − 3). Stand at w = 1: the loss is 4 and the slope is −4. A negative slope means "going right goes downhill", so you should increase w. With a learning rate of 0.1, the update is w ← 1 − 0.1 × (−4) = 1.4, and the loss drops to (1.4 − 3)² = 2.56. Stand at w = 2.9 instead and the slope is only −0.2, so the step is tiny. That is why steps shrink as you approach the bottom, with no extra code: the slope itself fades to zero there.

⛰️
The fog analogy

You're on a hillside in thick fog and want to reach the valley. You can't see the landscape, but you can feel the slope under your feet. So you step downhill, feel again, and step again. That's gradient descent. The learning rate is your stride. Too small and it takes all week. Too large and you stride right over the valley onto the opposite slope.

The chain rule, in one line

A network is functions inside functions: loss(head(block(embed(x)))). The chain rule says the slope of such a chain is the slopes of each piece multiplied together. So you can get the gradient of a 100-layer network by multiplying small local slopes, working backwards from the loss. That is backpropagation (Chapter 8). Without it, training deep networks would be far too slow to be practical.

# forward
z = w * x        # local: dz/dw = x
a = relu(z)      # local: da/dz = 1 if z > 0 else 0
L = (a - y)**2   # local: dL/da = 2(a - y)

# backward — multiply the local slopes
dL/dw = 2(a - y) * (1 if z > 0 else 0) * x

Plug in numbers to see it work. Let w = 0.5, x = 2 and the target y = 3. Forward: z = 1, a = relu(1) = 1, L = (1 − 3)² = 4. Backward: dL/da = 2(1 − 3) = −4, da/dz = 1 (because z > 0), dz/dw = x = 2. Multiply: dL/dw = −4 × 1 × 2 = −8. The gradient is negative, so increase w. Check: at w = 0.6, z = 1.2 and the loss falls to (1.2 − 3)² = 3.24. Each local slope only needed the numbers at its own step, which is why the backward pass can reuse values saved from the forward pass. (The rule itself is in P4: Derivatives, Gradients & the Chain Rule; here the point is how little each piece has to know.)

Notice also what happens if z had been negative: da/dz = 0, so the whole product is 0 and w gets no update at all. One zero anywhere in the chain blocks the signal. That is the "dead ReLU" problem. Its milder cousin, the vanishing gradient, comes from many slopes smaller than 1 multiplied together: 0.5 across 20 layers is about one in a million. You will meet both in Chapters 8 and 9.

Probability: models output beliefs, not answers

A classifier doesn't say “spam”. It says P(spam | this email) = 0.93 — “93% likely to be spam, given this email”. Everything that happens next — thresholds, calibration, expected cost — is probability. Four ideas cover nearly all of it.

🎲

Distribution

click to flip
How probability is shared out across possible outcomes. A model's output layer is a distribution: a softmax over 50,000 tokens, or a single number for yes/no.
📊

Expectation

click to flip
The average outcome, weighted by how likely each one is. Compare two expected costs: allowing costs P(fraud)×loss, blocking costs (1−P)×friction. Pick the smaller one. This is how a probability turns into a decision.
🔁

Conditional / Bayes

click to flip
P(A|B) — your belief in A after seeing evidence B. Bayes: posterior ∝ likelihood × prior (new belief grows with how well the evidence fits and how likely it was to begin with). Rare things stay rare: a 99%-accurate test for a 1-in-10,000 disease is usually wrong when it says positive.
🌀

Entropy

click to flip
How uncertain a distribution is. Low entropy = confident. Cross-entropy measures how far your predicted distribution is from the true one. It's the loss for nearly every classifier and every LLM.

Turning a probability into a decision. Expectation is where the model's output meets money. Suppose a model says a payment is fraud with probability 0.1, fraud would cost £200, and wrongly blocking a good customer costs about £5 in goodwill. If you allow it, you expect to lose 0.1 × £200 = £20. If you block it, you expect to lose 0.9 × £5 = £4.50. Block. Notice that a probability of just 10% still led to blocking, because the two kinds of mistake cost very different amounts. That is why "use a threshold of 0.5" is rarely the right rule.

Cross-entropy: the loss you'll use most

For classification, the loss for one example is −log(p). Here p is the probability your model gave to the correct answer. Being confidently right costs almost nothing. Being confidently wrong costs a huge amount. Drag the slider and see how lopsided it is.

Probability given to the correct answerLoss −ln(p)What it means
0.990.01Confident and right: almost free
0.90.11Fairly sure and right
0.50.69A coin flip between two options
0.12.30Mostly believed something else
0.014.61Confident and wrong: very expensive

Each time the probability shrinks tenfold, the loss goes up by the same amount, about 2.3. The loss never quite reaches zero and has no upper limit. So the fastest way for a model to reduce its average loss is to stop being confidently wrong, rather than to become slightly more confident on examples it already gets right. Why the minus sign? log(p) is negative for any p below 1, and a loss should be a positive number that shrinks as the model improves.

A language model's perplexity is e^(mean cross-entropy). Roughly, it means “how many equally likely options is the model choosing between?”. Perplexity 1 means it is certain. Perplexity 50,000 (the whole vocabulary) means it has learned nothing. For example, a mean cross-entropy of 2.3 gives perplexity e2.3 ≈ 10: on average the model is about as unsure as if it were picking among 10 equally likely next words.

🧮
Why logs are everywhere

Multiplying many probabilities quickly gives numbers too tiny for a computer to store (underflow). 1,000 tokens at p=0.5 each gives 10⁻³⁰¹. Logs turn multiplying into adding. In log space the same 1,000 tokens give 1,000 × ln(0.5) ≈ −693, an ordinary number. That keeps numbers in a sensible range and makes gradients behave. Whenever you see logits, log_softmax or logsumexp, this is why.

Softmax, worked once. Take three class scores (logits) [2, 1, 0]. Raise e to each: about [7.39, 2.72, 1.00]. Their total is 11.11, so divide each by it: [0.67, 0.24, 0.09]. The results are positive and add up to 1, so they can be read as probabilities. The biggest score still wins, but not everything: the gap of 1 between scores became a ratio of e ≈ 2.7 between probabilities. Adding the same number to every score (say, [12, 11, 10]) gives exactly the same probabilities, which is the trick libraries use to avoid overflow.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Comparing raw dot products between vectors of different lengths. A long, so-so vector can beat a short, perfect match. When you mean “similar”, normalize the vectors or use cosine.
  • Passing probabilities where logits are expected. Logits are the raw scores before softmax. CrossEntropyLoss in most frameworks applies its own softmax. Pass it values that are already softmaxed and softmax runs twice — training quietly breaks.
  • Averaging probabilities in log space, or the other way round. The mean of logs is a geometric mean, not a normal one. Mixing the two quietly changes what you are measuring.
  • Assuming a high-accuracy test means a positive result is probably right. How common the event is (the base rate) matters most. With a 1-in-10,000 event and a 1% false-positive rate, most positives are false. Think in expected counts, not percentages.

Interview drill

Warm-up: Explain cosine similarity to a product manager in two sentences, and say when it is the wrong choice.

How a strong answer sounds
It measures whether two items point the same way, ignoring how big they are. So a short document and a long one about the same topic count as similar. It's the wrong choice when size carries meaning (counts, spend, intensity). It's also wasted work when embeddings already have length 1 — then a plain dot product gives the same answer, faster.

Numeracy: A fraud model outputs P(fraud)=0.02 for a £500 transaction. Blocking a legitimate customer costs you about £8 of goodwill; letting fraud through costs the full £500. Block or allow?

How a strong answer sounds
Expected cost of allowing = 0.02 × 500 = £10. Expected cost of blocking = 0.98 × 8 ≈ £7.84. So block — but only just. That means the decision depends on calibration (whether the model's probabilities are honest). If the model's 2% is really 1.5%, allowing wins. This is why calibration (Chapter 7) matters more than raw AUC when a probability drives a money decision. It's also why thresholds are tuned separately for each price range instead of one threshold for everything.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • A dot product measures agreement. Cosine similarity measures agreement while ignoring size.
  • A matrix multiply is a batch of dot products. Make the inner dimensions match and most bugs disappear.
  • A gradient points uphill. Training steps the other way, and the learning rate is the stride length.
  • Cross-entropy −log(p) punishes confident mistakes very hard. It's the default loss for classification.
  • Models output probabilities. Turning them into decisions needs expected cost, not just a threshold.

Check your understanding

Two embedding vectors have a dot product of 0. What does that tell you?

A zero dot product means the vectors are at 90° (orthogonal): they share no direction at all. Opposite directions would give a large negative dot product instead.

You multiply a batch X of shape (64, 300) by weights W. What shape must W have to produce (64, 128)?

Inner dimensions must match: (64×300)·(300×128) → (64×128). The batch dimension (64) passes straight through.

The gradient of the loss with respect to a weight is +4.0 and the learning rate is 0.1. What is the update?

w ← w − η·g = w − 0.1×4.0. A positive gradient means making the weight bigger makes the loss bigger. So you step the other way and shrink it.

Your classifier assigns probability 0.01 to the correct class. Roughly what is the cross-entropy loss for that example?

−ln(0.01) ≈ 4.6. Being confidently wrong is very expensive. That's why a few wrongly labelled examples can dominate a whole training batch.

A disease affects 1 in 10,000 people. A test has a 1% false-positive rate and catches every true case. You test positive. Roughly what is the chance you have it?

Out of a million people: 100 really have it, and about 10,000 healthy people test positive by mistake. So your chance is ≈100/10,100 ≈ 1%. How rare the event is matters most. The same arithmetic explains why a “99% accurate” fraud or moderation model can still bury reviewers in false alarms.
