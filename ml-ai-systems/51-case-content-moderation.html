Case Study: Content Moderation at Scale

Two billion pieces of content a day: text, images, video and audio, in a hundred languages. The people posting harmful content adapt within hours of any change you ship. Both kinds of mistake hurt real people — leaving something harmful up, and taking down something legitimate. No single setting avoids both. So the real engineering problem is choosing where you're willing to be wrong: on purpose, measurably, and differently for each category.

  • Design a layered pipeline: exact matching of known content, then classifiers, then human review
  • Choose how strict to be based on how severe the harm is and how many people will see it, not one accuracy number
  • Build a review system that produces reliable labels and protects the reviewers
  • Handle adversaries who adapt, which is nothing like ordinary data drift
  • Measure prevalence — what users actually see — rather than how much you took down

What you need before this chapter

1 · Requirements

Two billion items/day is about 23,148 items/second on average; the 40,000 QPS peak is about 1.73 times that. The 200 ms pre-publish budget cannot include a full review of every long video. Define what receives a fast check, what stays pending, and which deeper checks run after upload. Track the views an item receives before a delayed decision takes effect.

🎯
Metrics

Primary: prevalence — the share of views that are of rule-breaking content. You estimate it by taking a random slice of traffic and having trained people label it. Not takedown counts, which rise both when enforcement gets better and when abuse gets worse.

Error metrics: the false-negative rate per policy category (harmful content you missed), and the false-positive rate (legitimate content you removed), measured through how often appeals are upheld, plus audits.

Speed: how long until action is taken on severe categories. Harm adds up with every view before removal.

Operational: how deep the reviewer queues are, how consistent decisions are against known-correct "gold" items, and the appeal backlog.

⚖️
Enforcement counts are not a quality metric

"We removed 40% more violating posts this quarter" fits three very different stories: the system got better, the platform faced 40% more abuse, or the system started removing legitimate content. As stated, there's no way to tell which.

The only sound measurement is prevalence. Sample traffic at random, have trained reviewers label it against the policy, and report what share of views break the rules. It's expensive. It's also the only number you can't improve just by shipping a more aggressive classifier, and every serious moderation programme is built around it.

2 · The moderation path

Use one durable item ID and content version throughout the pipeline. A delayed result for an old version must not overwrite a decision for an edited post. Store the classifier version, policy version, scores and reason for each action. Apply removal and restoration through idempotent operations so duplicate messages do not apply two penalties or lose an appeal outcome.

Perceptual hashes are similarity signals, not proof that two files are identical in meaning or context. Their resistance to cropping and other edits depends on the algorithm and threshold. Keep trusted reference-set provenance and an error-correction process. Exact hashes can identify identical bytes; neither kind of match eliminates the need to handle an incorrect reference label.

One upload, from publish to appealStep-through
  1. Compare a perceptual hash (a fingerprint that still matches after cropping or re-encoding) against databases of known violating media, including hash sets shared across the industry for the most serious categories. This takes microseconds per item, is almost never wrong, and catches the huge volume of people re-uploading the same content. Always run it before any model: it's the cheapest and most certain stage you have.
  2. One small model per policy category, plus a shared encoder that handles text, images, audio transcripts and video frames together. They run side by side within the pre-publish time budget, and they output calibrated scores, not decisions. Calibration — a score of 0.8 meaning it really is right about 80% of the time — matters more than raw accuracy here, because everything downstream compares scores against thresholds.
  3. Scores plus context turn into an action. Context means the poster's history, the size of the audience, whether the post is being boosted, which country it's in, and how severe the category is. This stage is deliberately kept separate from the models: policy changes weekly, models retrain monthly. Tie them together and every small policy tweak becomes a machine-learning project.
  4. There are many options between "allow" and "delete": add context, push it down in ranking, show it only to followers, age-gate it, remove it, or remove it and penalise the account. Most of the value is in the middle. Pushing content down at moderate confidence prevents most of the harm, because harm grows with views. Removal, where a mistake hurts most, is saved for high confidence.
  5. Order the queue by expected harm: projected views × severity × how unsure the model is. Don't order by upload time. A borderline post on a dormant account can wait; the same post going viral cannot. Human reviewers are the scarcest resource in the system, so spend them where a decision changes the most.
  6. Reviewer decisions become training labels. That's also a trap. Those items are mostly ones the models already flagged, so a model trained only on review outcomes learns to agree with itself. Randomly sampled labelled traffic is what keeps the loop honest.
  7. Use separate reviewers, with a turnaround time users can count on. Most usefully, how often appeals are upheld gives you a live estimate of false positives, per category and per model version. If a category's overturn rate jumps after a release, it has just told you something no offline test did.

3 · Deep dive: choosing where to be wrong

Translate error rates into daily cases before choosing a threshold. If 99% of two billion items are legitimate, a 0.1% false-positive rate affects 1.98 million legitimate items/day. Reducing it to 0.01% still affects 198,000. The denominator is legitimate items, not removals. Precision instead asks what fraction of removals were correct; these quantities answer different questions.

Prevalence must be paired with wrongful-action measurements. Removing every post would remove violating views too, while destroying the service. Likewise, an appeal overturn rate describes people who appealed, not all false positives. Sample enforced items independently to estimate wrongful actions, and sample impressions to estimate harmful exposure. The simulator uses illustrative operating points rather than a fitted production ROC curve.

Every classifier gives you one curve of possible trade-offs, and you pick a single point on it. Move the threshold to catch more harmful content, and you also remove more legitimate content. With 2 billion items a day, a tenth of a percent in either direction is two million decisions.

🧭
Severity and reach, not one threshold

The right setting varies by a factor of a hundred across categories, and one number can't express that. Two factors do most of the work:

Severity. Where the harm is catastrophic and can't be undone, act even at low confidence and accept some wrongly removed posts. The imbalance in costs is overwhelming. For contested categories like misinformation or borderline harassment, demand high confidence, and prefer pushing content down or adding context to deleting it. Wrongly removing legitimate speech is itself a serious harm, and it damages trust in the platform.

Reach. Harm grows with views, not with posts. The same borderline item seen eleven times and seen four million times deserve completely different treatment, so expected reach belongs in the decision. This is also what makes the problem manageable: a tiny fraction of items carries almost all the views, so spending human review there achieves far more than checking everything equally.

4 · Deep dive: the human review system

Estimate service capacity by queue and language. Four million reviews/day across 15,000 reviewers averages about 267 cases per reviewer per day. At six productive review hours, that allows roughly 81 seconds per case. Training, breaks, complex cases and appeals reduce available capacity further. Staff rare-language and specialist queues explicitly; spare capacity elsewhere cannot necessarily clear them.

Prevent low-reach items from waiting forever. Combine priority with maximum waiting times, and reserve capacity for appeals and independent audits. Reviewers need the policy version that governed the original action, plus the current policy if it changed. Record whether an overturn reflects a model error, missing context or a policy change so training labels do not mix those causes.

Queue designSort by expected harm — projected views × severity × how unsure the model is — with a firm deadline per category. Sorting by arrival time is wrong, and it's what almost every first version does.
Label qualityReviewers genuinely disagree on hard cases. So measure how often they agree, mix known-correct "gold" items into every queue, and treat a drop in gold accuracy as an incident. If trained people can't apply a policy consistently, a model can't learn it either. That's a finding to take to the policy team, not a machine-learning problem to solve.
Reviewer wellbeingThe worst categories cause real psychological harm. Limits on how long anyone works a queue, images blurred and in greyscale by default, audio muted, stand-in previews, and real clinical support are requirements, not perks. They also improve decisions, because exhausted reviewers are inconsistent reviewers.
ContextA decision needs the surrounding conversation, the account's history, the language and the region. Half the moderation errors that make the news are context failures: satire, slurs reclaimed by the group they target, someone quoting abuse in order to condemn it, or footage documenting an atrocity that looks just like footage celebrating it.
Sampling for measurementKeep a separate, randomly sampled queue whose only job is to estimate prevalence. It must never be prioritised or steered by the models. The moment it is, your one unbiased measurement becomes as biased as the system it's measuring.
EscalationSend new attacks, coordinated campaigns and genuinely unclear policy cases to specialists within hours. The first reviewers to see a new evasion trick are your earliest warning. Capture that signal instead of losing it inside a single yes/no verdict.

5 · Deep dive: adversaries

Behavioural evidence also has failure cases: a legitimate event can create a burst of similar posts, and old accounts can be compromised. Treat coordination signals as evidence to investigate, not automatic proof of wrongdoing. Compare new rules against legitimate bursts and keep a rollback path. Model diversity helps only when the models do not share the same decisive blind spot.

🎭
This is not ordinary drift

Natural drift is slow, unintentional and fairly steady. You retrain monthly and stay ahead of it (Chapter 34). Adversarial drift is a reaction to your decisions. Ship a classifier on Monday, and by Wednesday people are probing exactly where its line sits. The data isn't just changing; someone is deliberately working against you.

🛡️
What actually helps

Signals about behaviour are hard to fake — account age, how fast someone posts, signs of coordination, who shares with whom. They hold up far longer than signals based on the content itself. Using several models retrained at different times stops attackers probing one fixed line. Holding some signals back from anything that gives quick feedback denies attackers a cheap way to test. And adding some randomness to enforcement near the line makes it expensive to map.

Assume the line will be found. The aim isn't a classifier nobody can evade — there isn't one. The aim is to make evasion cost more than the abuse is worth, and to detect the attempt to find the line. That attempt is itself a strong signal: legitimate users don't post forty near-identical versions of the same thing.

6 · Capacity and cost

At the table's assumptions, £1.8 million ÷ 4 million reviews is £0.45 per reviewed item. Sending 20% more items for review adds 800,000 cases, or £360,000/day at the same unit cost. That arithmetic explains why a release needs a review-volume forecast as well as classifier scores. Costs are hypothetical here; actual staffing, case difficulty and local rates determine the production budget.

A 60,000-impression sample with true prevalence 0.1% contains about 60 violations on average. A language representing 1% of traffic contributes only about 600 sampled impressions and 0.6 violations. Overall sample size therefore does not guarantee useful estimates for every language. Oversample small groups when needed, then use sampling weights for an overall estimate and report uncertainty.

These costs turn the usual instinct upside down. A model change that adds a point of accuracy but sends 20% more items to human review is a big net loss. A routing change that sends the same volume to the right reviewers is nearly free. Optimise the whole system, not the classifier.

7 · What breaks

A removal must reach every distribution path, including cached feeds and media delivery. Track propagation delay as part of time-to-action. If a verdict is reversed, restore visibility consistently and undo applicable penalties. A successful classifier result with a failed enforcement message is still a failed moderation outcome.

🎤
Interview drill

Follow-up: “Your model's offline F1 improved from 0.82 to 0.88, but prevalence is unchanged. What happened?”

How a strong answer sounds
Most likely the test set no longer looks like what users actually see. The classic cause is training and testing on reviewed items, which are the ones the previous model already flagged. The model gets better at agreeing with itself, while the content it never surfaced stays invisible. I'd check whether the test set is randomly sampled from live traffic or taken from enforcement, and I'd expect the latter. Second possibility: the gain is real but in places that don't matter. Prevalence is weighted by views, so getting better on the huge tail of posts nobody sees moves F1 and not prevalence. Third: the policy engine isn't acting on the improvement, because the thresholds were tuned for the old model's score distribution and the new model scores differently against the same cutoffs. Fourth: the adversaries moved on, and the categories that improved are the ones they stopped attacking. To settle it, I'd measure prevalence per category on view-weighted random samples, and I'd stop treating offline F1 as a release signal. In this system it has moved again and again without anything real changing.

Follow-up: “Should the threshold be the same for every policy category?”

How a strong answer sounds
No, and it isn't close. The two kinds of error cost completely different amounts in different categories. Where harm is catastrophic and can't be undone, I'd act at low confidence and accept far more wrongful removals, backed by fast appeals. The imbalance is overwhelming and nobody seriously argues otherwise. For contested categories like misinformation or borderline harassment, wrongly removing legitimate content is itself a real harm and damages trust in the platform. So I'd demand high confidence before removing anything, and use ranking demotion, context labels and reach limits across the wide middle. That prevents most of the harm anyway, because harm grows with views. I'd also feed reach in as an explicit input rather than relying on one threshold: the same score on a post expected to get eleven views and one expected to get four million shouldn't produce the same action. In practice that means a separate operating point per category, chosen together with the policy and legal teams, reviewed on a schedule, and written down. Then when a decision is challenged, the answer is a stated position on a trade-off, not an accident of where someone once set a constant.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Measure prevalence on randomly sampled, view-weighted traffic. Takedown counts and offline F1 can both move without anything getting better.
  • Build the pipeline in layers: hash matching, then calibrated classifiers, then a policy engine kept separate from the models, then humans.
  • Use graduated actions. Demoting content and adding context at moderate confidence prevents most harm, because harm grows with views.
  • Choose a setting per category based on severity and reach. One global threshold is wrong everywhere at once.
  • Adversaries react to your decisions, so lean on behaviour signals and make your decision boundary expensive to map.
  • Human review is the biggest cost and the scarcest resource. Route by expected harm, and look after the people doing it.

Check your understanding

Why is "violating posts removed" a poor primary metric?

Prevalence — the share of views that break the rules, estimated from randomly sampled labelled traffic — is the one number you can't improve just by removing more things.

Why should the policy engine be separate from the classifiers?

Models produce calibrated scores. The policy engine turns those scores, plus context, severity and reach, into actions. Different teams own them, and they change at different speeds.

Why does training only on human-reviewed items degrade the system over time?

It's a closed loop feeding itself. Randomly sampled labelled traffic, kept outside the enforcement loop, is the only way to see what the system is missing.

Why does projected reach belong in the enforcement decision?

It also makes the problem manageable. A tiny fraction of items gets nearly all the views, so that's where scarce human review achieves the most.

Why do behavioural signals hold up better than content signals against adversaries?

Getting past a text classifier costs one rewrite. Faking a two-year-old account with real followers costs two years. The goal is to make evasion cost more than the abuse is worth.
