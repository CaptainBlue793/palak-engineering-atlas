Classical ML: Trees & Ensembles

Deep learning rules text, images and audio. But on the spreadsheet-shaped data that runs most businesses, gradient-boosted trees are still the model to beat. They should also be the first model you build: they're fast, hard to break, and almost free to run.

One question at a time

Why learn trees when neural networks get the headlines? Because most business data is a table: one row per customer, order or transaction, and columns like age, amount, country and days-since-signup. On that kind of data, tree ensembles are usually as accurate as anything else, need almost no preprocessing, and run on a plain CPU. Fraud scoring, credit risk, churn prediction and demand forecasting are very often tree models in production.

A decision tree is a flowchart the machine writes for itself. At each point it picks one feature and one cut-off value that best separate the classes, then does the same again on each side. To predict, you walk from the top to a leaf and read off the most common class there (or the average value).

For example, a tiny loan-approval tree might read: “Is income above 40k? If no → reject. If yes → is existing debt above 20k? If yes → reject, if no → approve.” A new applicant with 55k income and 5k debt goes right, then left, and lands in an “approve” leaf. Every prediction is just a short path of yes/no questions, which is why trees are fast and easy to explain.

“Best” needs a score. The usual one is Gini impurity: the chance that two items picked at random from a group have different labels.

Gini = 1 − Σ pₖ²        # 0 = pure node, 0.5 = fifty-fifty (binary)
gain = Gini(parent) − weighted average Gini(children)

pₖ is the fraction of the group that belongs to class k. Let's score one split by hand. A parent node holds 10 rows: 5 “fraud” and 5 “ok”. Its Gini is 1 − (0.5² + 0.5²) = 0.5, as mixed as two classes can be. Now try the split amount > 900:

  • Left child (amount ≤ 900): 6 rows, 1 fraud and 5 ok. Gini = 1 − ((1/6)² + (5/6)²) ≈ 0.28.
  • Right child (amount > 900): 4 rows, 4 fraud and 0 ok. Gini = 1 − (1² + 0²) = 0, perfectly pure.
  • Weighted average of the children: (6/10)·0.28 + (4/10)·0 ≈ 0.17.
  • Gain = 0.5 − 0.17 ≈ 0.33.

The weighting matters: a tiny child that happens to be pure shouldn't count as much as a big one. For a regression tree (predicting a number), the idea is the same but the score is the variance of the target values in each group, and each leaf predicts the average of the rows that land in it.

The tree tries every feature and every possible cut-off, and keeps the split with the biggest gain. That's the whole training algorithm: take the best step available right now (a greedy search), then repeat.

“Every possible cut-off” sounds expensive, but it isn't too bad: sort a column once, and the only cut-offs worth testing are the midpoints between neighbouring distinct values. Splitting stops when a node is pure, when it hits the maximum depth, or when a split would leave fewer rows in a leaf than the minimum you set. Greedy means the tree never looks ahead — it may miss a pair of splits that would only be good together — but in practice this rarely hurts, and ensembles cover for it.

🩺
Analogy: triage questions

A nurse doesn't ask 40 questions in a fixed order. They ask the one that narrows things down fastest — “are you in pain?” — and go from there. A tree is that instinct turned into numbers: at every point, ask the question that separates the outcomes best.

A single deep tree always overfits

Grow it far enough and every training point gets its own leaf. You get 100% training accuracy and a boundary that has memorised the noise. The fix isn't a cleverer splitting rule. It's many trees — which is what ensembles are.

Two words describe what you just saw in the depth slider, and they come up in every ML interview:

  • Bias is being wrong in a consistent way because the model is too simple. A depth-1 tree can only draw one straight cut, so it misses the real shape no matter how much data you give it. Train and test accuracy are both mediocre.
  • Variance is being wrong because the model is too sensitive to the exact training rows. A depth-9 tree reshapes itself around every noisy point. Draw a new sample (the 🎲 button) and the regions change a lot. Train accuracy is high, test accuracy lags — that “gap” stat is variance showing up.

More depth lowers bias and raises variance. The sweet spot is usually somewhere in the middle, and you find it by watching test (or validation) accuracy, never training accuracy. Ensembles are a way to cheat this trade-off: bagging attacks variance, boosting attacks bias.

Bagging: average away the variance

A random forest trains hundreds of deep trees and averages their votes. Each tree sees a different random sample of the rows (drawn with replacement, called a bootstrap sample), and considers only a random subset of features at each split. Every single tree overfits. But their mistakes are partly independent, so averaging cancels much of the noise and keeps the signal.

Here's why averaging helps, with numbers. Suppose each tree on its own is right 70% of the time on some kind of case, and their mistakes are fully independent. Take a majority vote of 3 such trees: the vote is right whenever at least 2 of the 3 are right, which happens 0.7³ + 3·0.7²·0.3 ≈ 0.78 of the time. With 25 trees it climbs above 0.97. Real trees make correlated mistakes, so the gain is smaller than this — which is exactly why the forest works so hard to make its trees different.

“With replacement” means that after picking a row you put it back, so the same row can be picked twice. A bootstrap sample has the same size as the original data, but by chance some rows appear several times and others not at all. The chance that a given row is never picked in n draws is (1 − 1/n)ⁿ, which gets very close to 1/e ≈ 0.37 for large n. That's where the 37% below comes from.

Why the randomness matters

If every tree saw the same rows and features, all the trees would be nearly identical and averaging would gain nothing. Sampling rows (bagging) and columns makes the trees less alike, and that difference is exactly what makes the average better than any one tree.

Free validation

Each tree leaves out about 37% of the rows — its out-of-bag sample. Score each row using only the trees that never saw it and you get an honest estimate without setting data aside. Handy when data is scarce.

Boosting: fix your own mistakes, repeatedly

Gradient boosting does the opposite. Instead of many strong, independent trees, it builds many small, weak trees one after another. Each new tree is trained on what the current model still gets wrong (the residual errors). Step through it:

A small trace first, predicting house prices (in thousands) for three houses priced 200, 300 and 400, with a learning rate of 0.5:

  1. Start with the plainest guess: the average, 300, for every house. Residuals (truth − prediction) are −100, 0 and +100.
  2. Tree 1 is fitted to those residuals, not to the prices. Say it learns them exactly: −100, 0, +100. Add half of that (the learning rate): predictions become 250, 300, 350. New residuals: −50, 0, +50.
  3. Tree 2 fits −50, 0, +50. Add half: predictions 225, 300, 375. Residuals −25, 0, +25.
  4. Each round halves the remaining error. The final model is the starting average plus the scaled outputs of every tree added together.

Why call it gradient boosting? For squared error, the residual y − ŷ is the negative gradient of the loss with respect to the prediction, up to a constant factor of 2 (Chapter 5). So each tree is one gradient descent step — taken in “prediction space” instead of by adjusting weights. Swap in a different loss, such as log loss for classification, and the trees fit that loss's gradient instead; the recipe doesn't change. The learning rate plays the same role as in Chapter 5: without it, a single tree would chase the noise in the residuals as eagerly as the signal.

The three libraries you'll actually use

⚡

XGBoost

The library that made boosted trees famous in competitions. It grows trees a full level at a time, has strong regularization and excellent docs, and runs everywhere.

🌿

LightGBM

Groups values into buckets (histograms) and grows the most useful leaf first. Much faster on large or wide data, so it's usually today's default. Watch for overfitting on small datasets.

🐈

CatBoost

Handles category columns itself, computing their statistics in an order that avoids the leakage of naive target encoding. Its default settings are strong.

Which one to start with? All three reach similar accuracy once tuned, so pick by your data. Big or wide tables where training time matters: LightGBM. Many text-like category columns (city, product ID, merchant) and little time to engineer encodings: CatBoost. A team or platform that already runs XGBoost, or you want the most widely documented option: XGBoost. Switching later is cheap — their APIs look alike and the same features work in all three.

🎚️
The settings worth your time
  • learning_rate + n_estimators — set a low rate (0.03–0.1) and let early stopping decide how many trees.
  • max_depth / num_leaves — how complex each tree may get. This is the main overfitting control.
  • min_child_samples — don't allow leaves built from just a few rows. Vital when one class is rare.
  • subsample + colsample_bytree — use a random part of the rows and columns per tree. This limits overfitting and speeds training up.
  • reg_lambda — L2 penalty on leaf values. Cheap insurance.

Everything else matters far less than better features.

Early stopping, which that list leans on, works like this: you ask for far more trees than you need (say 5,000), and after each new tree the library scores the model on a validation set. When the validation score hasn't improved for, say, 100 trees in a row, training stops and keeps the best version seen. For example, validation loss might bottom out at tree 740 and creep up afterwards; you keep 740 trees. That's how you avoid guessing n_estimators — and why the validation set must be separate from the final test set, since you've now used it to make a choice.

Three traps in that chart
  • Counting splits favours columns with many distinct values. A continuous column offers thousands of possible cut-offs, so it gets picked often without being more useful. Prefer importance based on gain, or permutation importance (shuffle the column and see how much accuracy drops).
  • Related features split the credit in random ways. Two near-identical columns each look half as important as either would alone. Dropping “the unimportant one” might cost nothing, or everything.
  • Importance is not cause. It says the model used the feature, not that changing it changes the outcome. SHAP values (a method that splits one prediction into a contribution from each feature) explain the model's behaviour — which still isn't the same as the real world's.

Permutation importance, worked through. Your fraud model scores 0.92 AUC on the validation set (AUC is a 0-to-1 quality score, covered in Chapter 7; higher is better). Randomly shuffle the merchant_risk_30d column — same values, but now attached to the wrong rows — and score again: 0.81. That feature is worth about 0.11 AUC. Shuffle device_age_days instead and you get 0.915: it barely matters. Shuffling keeps the column's distribution but breaks its link to the label, so the drop measures how much the model relies on that link. Do it on validation data, not training data, or you'll measure how much the model leaned on the feature to memorise. And if two columns are near-copies, shuffling one does little because the model uses the other — the same credit-sharing trap as above.

Trees are a joy to serve

No GPU, no batching, a model file measured in megabytes, and a prediction that's just a few hundred yes/no comparisons. Latency is microseconds, and deploying is “copy a file”. When you weigh a 0.3% accuracy gain from a neural ranker against that, count the serving bill too (Chapter 38).

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • One-hot encoding a 50,000-value column for a tree. Each 0/1 column gives a weak split, and the tree gets overwhelmed. Use the library's built-in category support, or target/ordinal encoding.
  • Standardizing inputs for trees. Harmless but pointless. Splits just compare against a threshold, so rescaling that preserves order changes nothing.
  • Setting n_estimators by hand. Fix the learning rate, then let early stopping on a validation set choose the number of trees.
  • Assuming a random forest can't overfit. It resists it, but with noisy labels and deep trees it absolutely can.
  • Expecting a tree to predict beyond what it has seen. A tree can never output a value outside the range in its training data. For a growing trend, model the trend separately or add a linear part. Example: if yearly demand in training ran from 100 to 180, every leaf holds an average of values in that range, so the model predicts at most 180 for next year even if the trend points to 200.

Interview drill

Warm-up: Why does a random forest generally beat a single deep tree?

How a strong answer sounds
A single deep tree fits the training data closely (low bias) but is very unstable (high variance): change a few rows and you get a completely different tree. Averaging many trees, each trained on a bootstrap sample of rows and a random subset of features, keeps that close fit while cancelling much of the instability, because the trees make partly different mistakes. The randomness is the whole point: identical trees would average out to the same overfitted model.

Deeper: You're asked to justify choosing LightGBM over a deep neural network for a 3-million-row fraud model.

How a strong answer sounds
On table data with mixed column types and very few fraud cases, GBDT usually matches or beats a tuned neural network for a fraction of the effort. It handles missing values and outliers by itself and needs no scaling. It trains in minutes on CPU, so we can retrain daily as fraud patterns change. The served model is a few megabytes and runs in microseconds inside a 10 ms budget, with no GPU bill. I'd still add embeddings from a neural component for high-cardinality IDs and network signals if evaluation shows they help. But the default should be the model that gets a trustworthy baseline into production this week, with the neural net as the challenger.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • A tree always takes the split that most reduces impurity right now. Depth is what controls overfitting.
  • Random forests average many different deep trees to cut instability. Boosting builds small trees in sequence, each fixing what the last got wrong.
  • Tune learning rate, depth, minimum samples per leaf and sampling — and let early stopping pick the number of trees.
  • Feature importance describes the model, not the world. Related features share credit in unpredictable ways.
  • GBDT is still the first thing to try on table data, and it's cheap and easy to serve.

Check your understanding

What does a decision tree maximise when choosing a split?

It tries possible cut-offs and keeps the one that leaves the two groups as pure as possible, weighted by their size. Margins belong to SVMs, not trees.

Which is the key difference between bagging and boosting?

Because each boosted tree depends on the one before, boosting needs careful tuning and early stopping, while bagging is forgiving. It's also why bagging's trees can all be trained at once and boosting's can't.

You lower the GBDT learning rate from 0.3 to 0.05. What else must change?

Each tree now contributes less, so you need roughly that many times more of them. A lower rate with more trees usually works better on new data, but training takes longer.

Your tree model must predict energy demand that grows year on year. What's the catch?

Every leaf outputs a fixed number learned from the training data, so next year's higher demand is simply out of reach. Model the trend separately (for example, predict the ratio to a trend line) or add a linear part.

A feature shows high split-count importance in your forest. What can you conclude?

Columns with many distinct values offer many possible cut-offs, so they get chosen often. Use gain-based or permutation importance, and check by actually removing the feature and re-scoring.
