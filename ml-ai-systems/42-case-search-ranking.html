Case Study: E-commerce Search

Design search for a marketplace with 200 million listings and 40 million searches a day. Unlike a feed, the user has told you exactly what they want. And unlike web search, the “right” answer depends on price, stock, delivery, seller quality and profit margin — not just on how well the text matches.

  • Build a pipeline that works out what a search means: typos, intent and product attributes
  • Combine word matching, meaning-based (vector) search and structured filters into one retrieval step
  • Train a ranking model on click data that is biased
  • Balance relevance against business goals without ruining either
  • Handle searches with no results, stock that changes constantly, and rare queries (the long tail)

Reading the numbers. 40M searches ÷ 86,400 seconds ≈ 460 searches per second on average. The peak of 3,500 is about 7–8× that — much spikier than a social feed, because shopping bunches up: evening hours, paydays, and sale events where a whole country hunts for the same deals in the same minute. You size for the sale-day peak. The query mix matters just as much. 40M searches but only 6M distinct query strings means a small set of popular queries is typed over and over (these have rich click data), while most distinct queries are rare and have almost none. The system has to be good at both, and they need different tools.

🎯
Metrics

Primary (online): revenue per search session, with conversion rate (share of searches that lead to a purchase) second. Offline: NDCG@10 — a score for how good the order of the top 10 is — measured against human relevance ratings for common (“head”) queries, plus a version estimated from click models for rare queries.
Guardrails: how often a search returns nothing, p95 latency, the share of results that are paid (sponsored) listings, exposure for new sellers, and the returns rate (a sign the customer bought the wrong thing).
Baseline: BM25 (a classic word-matching score) over title and attributes, then sorted by overall popularity. It's surprisingly hard to beat on common queries, and easy to beat on rare ones.

2 · Query understanding

NormaliseMake everything lowercase, remove punctuation, spell out units (13in → 13 inch), handle plurals. Cheap to do, and it pays off a lot.
Spell correctLearn from search logs: when users retype a search themselves, that's the best training data you can get (ipone → iphone). Never “correct” a real brand name into an ordinary word.
Segment & tagLabel the parts of the query: red nike running shoes size 9 → colour=red, brand=nike, category=running-shoes, size=9. This turns free text into exact filters.
Intent classifyIs the user after one specific product, browsing broadly (“gifts for dad”), or filtering by attributes? Intent changes everything about how you rank: someone looking for one exact product wants that product, not variety.
ExpandAdd synonyms learned from searches that led to the same clicks (trainers ≈ sneakers), and carefully loosen the query when too few results come back. Expansion easily drags in junk, so only do it when the result count is low.
RewriteFor rare queries, an embedding of the whole query captures meaning that word matching misses. That's exactly where combining both retrieval methods pays off.

A worked trace. Follow one messy query through the steps above:

  1. Raw: Red NIKE runing shoes, size 9!
  2. Normalise: red nike runing shoes size 9 — lowercase, punctuation gone.
  3. Spell correct: runing → running, because the logs show many users typed runing and then retyped running. nike is left alone because it is a known brand.
  4. Tag: colour=red, brand=nike, category=running-shoes, size=9. Nothing is left over as free text.
  5. Intent: attribute search (the user is filtering, not naming one exact product). So the page should show a variety of red Nike running shoes in size 9, not ten listings of one model.
  6. Expand: skipped, because the tagged query already matches hundreds of listings. Expansion only kicks in when results are scarce.

The edge cases are where query understanding goes wrong. apple can be a brand or a fruit, so the tag depends on the category the user is in and what people usually click for that query. 9 can be a size or part of a model name (pixel 9), so a tag is only applied as a strict filter when the tagger is confident. Otherwise the words stay as ordinary text and the ranker sorts it out.

🔤
Tagging the query is worth more than a better ranker

Once size 9 becomes a real filter instead of two words, you stop showing size-11 shoes that happen to say “9” somewhere in the description.

Pulling attributes out of the query turns fuzzy text matching into exact rules. On marketplaces this usually lifts conversion more than any change to the ranking model — because the mistakes it removes are the ones that make users give up and leave.

3 · Retrieval

LegHandlesReturns
Lexical (BM25)Exact words, model numbers, brand names, product codes (SKUs)~800
Vector (bi-encoder)Different wording and general intent: “something to keep coffee hot” → vacuum flasks~600
StructuredThe tagged attributes used as strict filters, plus what's typical for the categoryconstrains both legs
PersonalisedBrands and price ranges this user usually buys; items they viewed recently~200

Merge the lists with reciprocal rank fusion — a method that combines several ranked lists using each item's position (Chapter 20). Then apply strict business filters: in stock, ships to this country, not blocked, seller active. Apply those filters during retrieval, never after. If you filter afterwards and the filter is strict, you're left with three leftover results from the wrong part of the catalogue.

Why both legs, and not just vectors. Embeddings are great at meaning and bad at exact identity. To an embedding model, iphone 14 case and iphone 15 case look almost the same, but a case for one doesn't fit the other. BM25 treats 14 and 15 as different words, so it gets model numbers and product codes right. Vectors, in turn, rescue queries like “something to keep coffee hot”, which share no words with “vacuum flask”. Each leg covers the other's blind spot.

How reciprocal rank fusion scores an item. Each list adds 1 / (k + rank), usually with k = 60. An item ranked 1st by BM25 and 5th by vectors scores 1/61 + 1/65 ≈ 0.0164 + 0.0154 = 0.0318. An item ranked 2nd by BM25 but missing from the vector list scores only 1/62 ≈ 0.0161. Agreement between legs is rewarded, and you never have to compare a BM25 score with a cosine similarity, which live on completely different scales.

Why filtering afterwards fails, with numbers. Suppose each leg returns its top 800 and only then do you filter for size 9, in stock and ships to Germany. If just 0.5% of generic “running shoes” listings pass all three, 800 × 0.005 = 4 results survive. Plenty of matching listings exist in the catalogue — they just weren't in the top 800 before filtering. Filtering inside the search means the top 800 are drawn only from listings that already pass.

4 · Learning to rank

Learning to rank means training a model to put the best results first. A gradient-boosted tree ranker (LambdaMART-style) using around 200 features is still very hard to beat here. A neural ranker is the challenger for rare queries, where understanding the text matters most.

What “LambdaMART” means in plain words. It is gradient-boosted trees (Chapter 6) trained with a signal designed for ordering. Instead of predicting a score for each item in isolation, it looks at pairs of results for the same query. Whenever a worse item sits above a better one, training pushes their scores apart — and pushes harder when swapping them would change NDCG more, so mistakes near the top count most. Why trees win here: the features mix counts, prices, ratios and yes/no flags on very different scales, which trees handle without careful rescaling. They also score 1,000 candidates in a few milliseconds on a CPU, and when a seller complains you can inspect which features drove a decision.

Where the 150 ms goes. A plausible split: query understanding ~15 ms; the retrieval legs, in parallel, ~40 ms; fetching features plus live price and stock for ~1,000 candidates ~20 ms; tree ranking ~15 ms; business blending and page assembly ~10 ms. That's about 100 ms, leaving ~50 ms for the network and slow outliers. If the neural challenger is added, it re-scores only the top ~100 from the trees, so it can't blow the budget.

Query–item features

How well the query matches each field (BM25 per field), how close the embeddings are, how many attributes match, whether the title matches exactly, and whether the category fits.

Item features

Past click-through and conversion rates (corrected for where the item was shown), price compared with the category average, seller rating, delivery speed, return rate, how much stock is left, and how new the listing is.

User & context features

How price-sensitive the user is, which brands they like, their device, their country and language, what they've done this session, and the time of day.

🎣
Your labels are clicks, and clicks are biased

The biggest problem is position bias: items at the top get clicked simply because they're at the top (Chapter 30). Shopping sites have a second trap too — the past click-through-rate (CTR) feature. An item placed near the top gets more attention, which can raise its observed CTR and help it stay near the top. More impressions alone do not raise CTR: the ratio is clicks divided by impressions. Left alone, the ranker just relearns yesterday's ranking.

Controls:

  • Correct labels for position, using how likely each slot was to be clicked. Estimate that by randomly swapping result positions in a small share of traffic.
  • Compute CTR features from an earlier time period than the labels, and for low-traffic items pull the estimate toward the category average.
  • Where you have enough volume, train on purchases rather than clicks. They're rarer, but much harder to game.

The two corrections in numbers. Position: say the random-swap experiments show that users look at slot 1 about 90% of the time but at slot 8 only about 30% of the time. A click at slot 8 is then stronger evidence of relevance, so it is weighted 1 ÷ 0.3 ≈ 3.3, while a click at slot 1 is weighted 1 ÷ 0.9 ≈ 1.1. Pulling low-traffic items toward the average: a new listing with 2 clicks from 5 impressions shows a raw CTR of 40%, which is mostly luck. Blend it with the category average (say 3%) as if you had also seen 100 “typical” impressions: (2 + 0.03 × 100) ÷ (5 + 100) = 5 ÷ 105 ≈ 4.8%. As real impressions pile up, they outweigh the 100 pretend ones and the estimate moves toward the item's true CTR.

5 · Deep dive: mixing relevance with business objectives

Every marketplace wants the ranker to consider profit margin, stock and paid placements. Every marketplace also has a story about the quarter when someone pushed margin too hard. Try it yourself:

Why keep business goals in a separate formula. The tempting shortcut is to bake margin into the training label (“a purchase of a high-margin item counts double”). Then nobody can see how much relevance was traded for margin, and changing the trade-off means retraining. Instead, the ranker predicts relevance and purchase probability, and a small, visible formula adds business terms on top — for example final = relevance + 0.15·margin + 0.25·fast_delivery + sponsored_boost — the sliders below let you play with weights like these. 6 · Deep dive: the tail and zero results

70% of queries are seen once a week or less

Common (“head”) queries have plenty of click data and mostly work already. Rare (“tail”) queries have none: no CTR features, no synonyms learned from clicks. They depend entirely on understanding the words and attributes. That's where embeddings, query tagging and query relaxation pay off — and where an offline score measured on head queries tells you nothing.

Zero results are a product failure

Track how often searches return nothing, as a top-level metric. Then recover step by step: drop the least important attribute filter, widen to the parent category, fall back to meaning-based (vector) search alone, and finally show “similar items” with an honest explanation. Never show an empty page. Log every case — these searches are your best source of missing synonyms.

A worked recovery. The query waterproof red hiking boots size 13 wide returns nothing. Step 1: drop the least important filter. Which one that is can be learned from the logs (which attributes do buyers most often give up?), so wide goes first. Still nothing. Step 2: drop the colour. Now 14 results, all size 13 and waterproof. Show them with a line such as “No exact matches in red — showing other colours”. That explanation matters: quietly changing the query makes users think search is broken.

7 · Deep dive: index freshness

ChangeFreshness needMechanism
Price, stock, delivery estimate< 60 sStream small updates into a store of changeable fields, read at ranking time — don't rebuild the whole index entry
New listing< 5 minAdd it to the index as it arrives, plus a guaranteed amount of early exposure, like Chapter 41
Title, images, attributes< 1 hRe-index just the changed listings and recompute their embeddings
Historical CTR / conversionDailyA nightly batch job, computed from an earlier period, pulled toward the category average for low-traffic items
Embedding model versionQuarterlyRe-embed everything into a hidden copy of the index, then switch over all at once (Chapter 15)

Sizing the fast-changing store. If 5% of the 200M listings change price or stock on a normal day, that's 10M updates ÷ 86,400 ≈ 115 writes per second — easy. But a flash sale can change a large seller's whole catalogue in one minute, so the update stream must absorb bursts of thousands of writes per second without falling behind the 60-second target. Reads are the heavier side: 3,500 searches per second × ~1,000 candidates = 3.5M lookups per second. That's why they're batched into one multi-key read per search against an in-memory key-value store, rather than 1,000 separate calls.

📦
Selling an out-of-stock item is worse than not ranking it

Stock changes much faster than you can rebuild an index, so availability must be looked up at request time, not stored as a fixed index field.

The usual pattern: retrieve plenty of candidates, then check availability during ranking with a fast key-value lookup. Treat a stale “in stock” as a bug with an agreed fix time. A search system that is 2% more relevant but sells items it doesn't have still loses.

🎤
Interview drill

Follow-up: “Conversion improved but returns went up 15%.” What happened?

How a strong answer sounds
The ranker learned to show items people buy, not items that make them happy. Usually that means cheap items, misleading titles, or the wrong variant (size, colour, compatibility). Clicks and purchases are both short-term signals; returns are the slower truth. I'd add returns and other after-purchase signals to the training target as something to predict and penalise. I'd check whether attribute matching is failing in categories with many variants, break returns down by type of query and by seller, and make the returns rate a launch guardrail. The general lesson: when a fast metric improves and a slower one gets worse, you've usually pushed a stand-in metric past the point where it still tracks the real outcome.

Follow-up: “Why not rank everything by conversion probability?”

How a strong answer sounds
Because the probability of a purchase is estimated from data the current ranker produced. It therefore carries position bias and popularity feedback: items that were shown a lot look like good sellers because they were shown a lot. It also ignores the long tail, where there is no data at all. And it optimises one session rather than the long-term relationship — cheap, easy-to-sell items can raise returns and wear down trust. A practical ranker predicts several outcomes at once (click, purchase, return, satisfaction), combines them with weights the product team chooses, trains on labels corrected for bias, and keeps guardrails on the things conversion can't see.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Understanding the query — turning attributes into strict filters — often helps more than a better ranker.
  • Combine word matching with meaning-based search, and apply structured filters inside the search.
  • Correct click labels for bias and damp down CTR features, or the ranker will just relearn yesterday's ranking.
  • Put business goals into an open, weighted formula with guardrails — don't hide them inside the training label.
  • Look up stock and price at request time; treat the zero-result rate and rare-query performance as top-level metrics.

Check your understanding

Why apply “in stock” as a runtime lookup rather than an index field?

Different fields need different freshness. Fast-changing ones belong in a fast store that you read while ranking.

Your ranker uses historical CTR as a feature. What's the danger?

It's a self-feeding loop. Correct for position, compute the feature from an earlier time period, and pull low-traffic items toward the category average.

70% of queries are long tail with no click history. Which investment helps them most?

There's no click history for rare queries. Understanding the words, and matching on meaning and attributes, is all you have.

Offline NDCG improved 4% but the A/B test showed no revenue change. Most likely explanation?

Human-rated sets are mostly common queries, and making results better for things users were already going to buy doesn't add revenue. Break the results down by query type before trusting one overall offline gain.

What's the right response to a zero-result query?

An empty result page is a lost visit — and a free hint about missing synonyms or gaps in the catalogue. The recovery steps rescue the visit, and the logs improve tomorrow's search.
