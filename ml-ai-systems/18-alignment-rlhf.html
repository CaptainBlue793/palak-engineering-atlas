Alignment: RLHF, DPO & Reward Models

A pretrained model just continues text the way the internet usually would. An assistant answers your question, refuses dangerous requests, and admits when it doesn't know. Turning the first into the second is called alignment. It sounds like a reinforcement-learning problem, but it is mostly a problem of good data and good evaluation.

  • Collect preference data that produces a usable reward model

  • Compare PPO with DPO and know when each is appropriate

The gap alignment closes

Ask a base model “How do I reset my password?” and it may well reply with three more questions in the same style — because that is what FAQ pages look like. It isn't broken. It is doing exactly what it was trained to do.

Alignment changes the goal from “what text usually comes next?” to “what response would a person prefer?”. Three qualities sum up that goal:

🤝

Helpful

Answers the question actually asked, at the right length, in a usable format — and asks for clarification only when it really needs to.

🛡️

Harmless

Declines truly dangerous requests — without refusing the many harmless ones that just sound similar.

🎯

Honest

Says “I don't know”, doesn't make up sources, and doesn't agree with you just because you pushed back.

These three pull against each other, which is why alignment needs judgement and not just a rule. Take “How do I pick a lock?”. A fully “harmless” model refuses, which fails the locksmith student and the person locked out of their own shed. A fully “helpful” model answers anything, including clearly harmful follow-ups. The training data has to show the model where the line sits, example by example — so the people writing and ranking those examples are, in effect, writing the model's values.

The classic three-stage pipeline

From base model to aligned assistantStep-through
  1. Pretrained on trillions of tokens (Chapter 16). It has the knowledge and the language skill. What it lacks is the conversational contract: the habit of treating your text as a request and answering it.
  2. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT): train on tens of thousands of high-quality examples, each a prompt plus the reply a good assistant would give (Chapter 17). This alone gets you most of the way to something usable. It is also the stage with the best payoff for the effort.
  3. Have the SFT model write several responses to each prompt, and ask people to compare them two at a time. Comparisons are far more reliable than scores: people agree on “B is better” much more often than on “that's a 7/10”.
  4. A reward model learns to give each response a single score, so that the preferred response scores higher. Often it is the SFT model with a small extra output that produces one number. Loss: −log σ(r_chosen − r_rejected). This is the Bradley–Terry model — the same maths as Elo ratings in chess.
  5. Now use reinforcement learning (PPO, or simpler versions like GRPO) to update the policy — the model being trained — so its responses score higher. Each step: generate responses, score them with the reward model, and make high-scoring responses more likely.
  6. Subtract a KL penalty — a cost for drifting too far from the original SFT model. Without it, the policy finds weird text that the reward model loves and people hate. The reward model is only accurate on responses similar to the ones it was trained on.
  7. Measure how often it beats the previous model, using human or AI judges. Red-team it (deliberately try to make it misbehave). Check it refuses the right things and only those. Then collect fresh comparisons on the new model's outputs and go round again. Alignment is a loop, not a one-off stage.

So the loss is small when the model already ranks the pair the human's way, and large when it ranks them backwards. Gradient descent pushes r_chosen up and r_rejected down. Notice that only the difference matters: adding 10 to every score changes nothing. That is why reward scores have no absolute meaning — “reward 3.2” only means something compared with another response to the same prompt.

📏
Your annotators define the model's values, literally

The reward model learns whatever your labellers prefer — including biases they don't know they have: longer answers, a confident tone, bullet points, American spelling.

So measure how often labellers agree with each other. Write guidelines that spell out trade-offs (“prefer correct and short over plausible and long”). And check specifically for a bias toward length — it is the most common and the most damaging.

The KL penalty

The RL goal is maximise E[r(x,y)] − β·KL(π ‖ π_ref). The first term is the average reward. The second is a leash. KL divergence measures how different two models' output probabilities are, so this term controls how far the tuned policy may drift from the SFT model. β sets how short the leash is. Slide it and watch the trade-off.

What does a KL number look like? Suppose, for the first word of a reply, the reference model gives “Sure” probability 0.6 and “Here” 0.4. After some RL the policy gives “Sure” 0.9 and “Here” 0.1. KL(π ‖ π_ref) adds up, over each choice, the policy's probability times the log of the ratio: 0.9·ln(0.9/0.6) + 0.1·ln(0.1/0.4) ≈ 0.365 − 0.139 ≈ 0.23. If the policy had not changed, both ratios would be 1, every log would be 0, and the KL would be 0. In real training the same sum is estimated token by token over sampled replies and added up across the reply.

How β plays out: with a large β, even small drift costs a lot, so the policy stays close to the SFT model and gains little. With a tiny β, drift is almost free, so the policy chases the reward model wherever it leads — including into its blind spots. Teams tune β by watching real human (or trusted judge) preference, not the reward score, and stop where the human curve peaks.

DPO: skip the reward model

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) starts from a maths shortcut. The best possible RLHF policy can be written directly in terms of the reward, so you don't need to learn the reward separately.

Instead, you train on the preference pairs directly, with a simple loss that looks like classification: make the preferred answer more likely and the rejected one less likely, relative to a frozen reference model. No separate reward model, no sampling loop, no RL machinery.

L_DPO = −log σ( β·[ log π(y_w|x) − log π_ref(y_w|x)
                   − log π(y_l|x) + log π_ref(y_l|x) ] )
# y_w = preferred, y_l = rejected; π_ref frozen. Two forward passes, ordinary backprop.

How to read that formula. log π(y_w|x) − log π_ref(y_w|x) is “how much more likely the model under training makes the preferred reply than the frozen reference does”. The second pair is the same for the rejected reply. The loss wants the first gap to be bigger than the second. It is the reward-model loss from above, with this log-ratio standing in for the reward — that substitution is the shortcut.

A small trace with β = 0.1. At the start the model is the reference, so both gaps are 0, the inside is 0, σ(0) = 0.5 and the loss is ln 2 ≈ 0.69. After some training, say the preferred reply's log-probability has risen by 0.5 relative to the reference and the rejected one has fallen by 0.5. The inside becomes 0.1 × (0.5 − (−0.5)) = 0.1, σ(0.1) ≈ 0.525 and the loss drops to ≈ 0.64. Because everything is measured relative to the reference, the reference plays the role of the KL leash: pushing far from it is only worth doing if it separates good replies from bad ones.

The practical recipe in 2026

First, SFT on a few thousand excellent examples. Then DPO on preference data — or ORPO/KTO if you only have thumbs-up/thumbs-down rather than pairs. Then repeat: generate from the new model and collect fresh preferences.

Save full PPO-style RL for when you have a reward worth exploring against — a reward you can check automatically, like unit tests passing or a maths checker. That is also where reinforcement learning has made its biggest recent gains.

Scaling preferences: RLAIF and constitutions

Human comparisons cost £1–5 each and take days to collect. Two standard ways to get more for less:

🤖 RLAIF

RL from AI feedback: use a strong model as the judge to produce the preference labels. Far cheaper, and surprisingly competitive. But it copies the judge's biases, so keep a human-labelled subset to check how often they agree and to catch drift.

📜 Constitutional AI

Give the model a written list of principles (a “constitution”). Have it critique and rewrite its own responses against them, then train on the rewrites. This makes the value judgements explicit — a document anyone can read, check and edit, rather than instructions hidden in a labelling guide.

Whichever you pick, check the cheap labels against expensive ones. A common pattern: label most pairs with an AI judge, have humans label a random few hundred of the same pairs, and track how often the two agree. If agreement drops, the judge (or the constitution) needs fixing before its labels go into training.

Failure modes to watch for

Most of these share one root cause. The model is optimised against a stand-in for “what people want” — a reward model, a labeller's quick judgement, a judge model — and it finds the places where the stand-in and the real goal disagree. Each row below is one such gap, and each fix either closes the gap in the data or limits how hard the model can push into it.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Treating the reward model's score as the goal. It is only a stand-in, and it stops being valid exactly where you push it hardest.
  • Collecting preferences on an old model's outputs. The reward model must see samples from the current model (“on-policy”), or it is scoring outputs the model no longer produces.
  • Not measuring false refusals. Refusing too much is as much a product failure as unsafe output — and far easier to ship by accident.
  • Skipping SFT. RL straight on a base model is much harder. Examples get you 80% of the way for a fraction of the effort.
  • Aligning once and stopping. Preferences, jailbreaks and product needs keep changing. This is a loop you run regularly, not a launch step.

Interview drill

Warm-up: Why does RLHF need a KL penalty against the reference policy?

How a strong answer sounds
The reward model is a learned approximation, trained on responses much like the SFT model's. Optimise against it with no limit and the policy drifts into areas where the reward model has never seen data and is simply wrong. The result is text that scores brilliantly and reads terribly. The KL term keeps the policy close to where the reward model can be trusted. It is the standard answer to Goodhart's law (“when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure”): limit the optimiser instead of trusting the measure.

Deeper: Your aligned model is measurably more helpful but 30% worse at code. What do you do?

How a strong answer sounds
That's the alignment tax. It usually comes from preference data made mostly of chatty prompts, so training pulls the model toward chatty behaviour and away from precise code. Diagnose first: is it a formatting problem (wrapping code in prose, refusing to output long files) or a real loss of skill? Formatting can be fixed with targeted preference data. For a real loss: mix code examples and code preference pairs into the tuning set, lower the learning rate and number of epochs, tighten the KL if it's an RL stage, and consider a separate adapter just for code. Above all, add code benchmarks to the standard eval suite so every alignment run reports them. The tax is only invisible if you don't measure it.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • SFT teaches the basic contract of answering. Preference training then sharpens which of several reasonable answers is best.
  • A reward model is trained on pairs of compared answers with a Bradley–Terry loss — the same maths as Elo.
  • The KL penalty keeps the policy where the reward model is still accurate. Without it you get reward hacking.
  • DPO gets most of RLHF's benefit with ordinary supervised-style training and far fewer moving parts.
  • Watch for length bias, sycophancy (agreeing too easily), over-refusal and lost skills — and measure each one.

Check your understanding

Why is preference data collected as pairwise comparisons rather than 1–10 ratings?

One person's 7/10 is another's 5/10, and even the same person drifts from day to day. Picking the better of two is far more reliable, and Bradley–Terry turns those picks into one consistent score.

Removing the KL penalty from RLHF typically causes what?

The policy drifts away from the kind of responses the reward model was trained on, where its scores mean nothing. The classic symptom is weird, repetitive text that gets top marks from the reward model.

What is DPO's main practical advantage over PPO-based RLHF?

DPO trains on preferences directly, with a supervised-style loss measured against a frozen reference model. It is far simpler and more stable. The cost is that it doesn't try out new responses while training.

Your aligned model refuses “how do I kill a process on Linux?”. What's the fix?

The model learned a surface pattern (“kill” = bad) instead of the real concept. More safety data makes that worse. Harmless look-alike examples teach the difference, and tracking the false-refusal rate stops it creeping back.

Preferences were collected on last quarter's model. Why is that a problem now?

Reward models are only reliable on responses like the ones they were trained on. Data from an old model leaves them badly calibrated exactly where the current model's outputs are. That is why you keep looping: generate → label → retrain.
