Fine-Tuning & PEFT

Fine-tuning means taking an already-trained model and training it a bit more on your own examples. It is great at teaching behaviour — an output format, a tone of voice, a way of doing a task. It is surprisingly bad at teaching facts, which is what most people reach for it to do. This chapter covers when to fine-tune, how LoRA makes it cheap, and how to serve a hundred fine-tunes on one GPU.

  • Build an instruction-tuning dataset that doesn't wreck the base model

First: should you fine-tune at all?

Fine-tuning is not free. It adds a training pipeline, a dataset you must maintain, a set of tests, a model file to version, and a plan for serving it. Reach for it only when the cheaper options really fall short.

Why this question comes first: the three tools change different things. A prompt changes what the model sees on one request. Retrieval (RAG) changes what the model sees by fetching documents for each request. Fine-tuning changes the model's weights, so the change is baked in for every request, and undoing it means training again. Pick the tool that changes the thing you actually need changed.

“We'll fine-tune it on our documentation”

This almost never works the way people expect. Fine-tuning nudges how the model writes. It does not build a reliable lookup table of facts.

So the model learns to sound like your docs while still making up specifics. And changing one fact means retraining the whole thing. Put the documents in a retrieval index instead, and fine-tune only for the format and behaviour of the answers.

Full fine-tuning

Full fine-tuning updates every weight. It works exactly like pretraining, but on a small, hand-picked dataset with a low learning rate (1e-5 to 5e-5).

It gives you the most control and costs the most. You need gradients and optimizer state for every parameter (see the memory maths in Chapter 10), and you end up with a full copy of the model for each task.

A worked example makes the cost concrete. Take a 7B-parameter model trained with AdamW in mixed precision. Each parameter needs about 16 bytes: 2 for the bf16 weight, 2 for its gradient, 4 for an fp32 master copy of the weight, and 4 + 4 for Adam's two running averages. So 7 billion × 16 bytes ≈ 112 GB before you count any activations. That is more than one 80 GB GPU holds, just to fine-tune a "small" model.

Why such a low learning rate? The pretrained weights already encode a great deal. A big step would overwrite that knowledge with whatever your few thousand examples happen to say. A small step nudges the model toward your task while keeping most of what it knew.

🧠
Catastrophic forgetting

Train hard on 5,000 customer-support conversations and the model gets better at support — and measurably worse at everything else: coding, other languages, general reasoning. It is like cramming for one exam and forgetting last term's material.

How to limit it: use a low learning rate, run few epochs, mix in 10–30% general instruction data, use PEFT (parameter-efficient fine-tuning) so most weights stay frozen, and always test on general benchmarks, not just your own task.

LoRA: train 0.1% of the weights

Here is the key insight. The change a fine-tune makes to a weight matrix is simple in structure — it is approximately low-rank, meaning the whole d×d change can be rebuilt from a handful of directions.

What does "low-rank" mean? A tiny example. Take the column [1, 2, 3] and the row [4, 5, 6]. Multiply them (column × row) and you get a full 3×3 matrix: [[4,5,6],[8,10,12],[12,15,18]]. That matrix has 9 numbers, but you only needed 6 to build it, because every row is just a multiple of [4, 5, 6]. It has rank 1: one direction explains all of it. A rank-r matrix is a sum of r such pieces. For a big d×d matrix with small r, the saving is enormous.

Why would the fine-tuning change be low-rank? The pretrained model already has the abilities. Adapting it to one narrow task mostly means turning a few existing directions up or down, not building brand-new ones. Experiments in the LoRA paper found that even very small ranks (r = 1 to 8) captured most of the useful change for many tasks.

So don't learn the full change ΔW. Learn two thin matrices whose product is close enough to it:

W' = W + BA        # W frozen (d×d), B is d×r, A is r×d, r ≈ 8–64
                   # trainable params: 2·d·r instead of d²
                   # d=4096, r=16  →  131k instead of 16.8M  (0.8%)

# A is initialised randomly, B to zeros, so the model starts unchanged.
# output scaled by alpha/r, so changing r doesn't change effective strength.

Two details in that block deserve a sentence each. Why is B set to zero? Because then BA is zero at step 0, so W' = W and the model starts out behaving exactly like the base model. Training can only move it away from there gradually. If both were random, the first forward pass would add random noise to every adapted layer. Why scale by alpha/r? A bigger r adds more terms to BA, so its values tend to grow. Dividing by r keeps the update's size roughly steady, which means you can try r = 8 and r = 32 without re-tuning the learning rate.

And where does the memory saving come from? The frozen W still has to sit in memory, but it needs no gradient and no Adam state. Only the small A and B matrices carry those extra 14 bytes per parameter. For a 7B model in bf16 that is about 14 GB of frozen weights plus well under a gigabyte for the adapter and its optimizer state — instead of the ~112 GB from the section above.

LoRA

Learns small low-rank changes to matrices you choose. The resulting adapter file is a few MB, training memory drops hugely, and quality is close to full fine-tuning on most tasks.

🗜️

QLoRA

The base weights are squeezed down to 4-bit and frozen, and LoRA is trained on top in bf16. This lets you fine-tune a 70B model on a single 48 GB GPU. It is the trick that put fine-tuning within reach of everyone.

🎛️

Prefix / prompt tuning

Instead of changing weights, learn a few made-up “virtual tokens” that get stuck on the front of every input. The files are tiny, but it is usually weaker than LoRA and it uses up context length.

The QLoRA arithmetic, step by step: a 70B model in bf16 needs 70 billion × 2 bytes = 140 GB just to hold the weights. At 4 bits (half a byte) per weight it needs about 35 GB. Add the LoRA adapter, its optimizer state and the activations, and it fits in 48 GB. The cost is that each layer's weights are turned back into bf16 on the fly during the forward and backward pass, so training runs slower than plain LoRA. Pick QLoRA when memory is the thing stopping you; pick plain LoRA when the bf16 model already fits and you want speed.

🔀
Merge, or keep the adapter separate?

Merge the adapter into the weights (W ← W + BA) when one task dominates. There is then no extra cost at inference, but you own a full-size model for each task.

Keep it separate when you serve many customers. Modern servers keep one base model in memory and apply a different adapter per request, even within the same batch (multi-LoRA). So 100 customers can share one GPU. That ability is often the whole reason to pick LoRA over full fine-tuning.

Sizing it: a 7B base model is about 14 GB in bf16. A rank-16 adapter on the attention matrices is tens of MB. So 100 adapters add a few GB, while 100 merged copies would need 1.4 TB. The price of keeping them separate is a small extra matrix multiply per adapted layer on every request.

The dataset is the whole job

For instruction tuning, quality beats quantity by a long way. The LIMA paper showed 1,000 excellent examples beating 50,000 mediocre ones.

Aim for examples that are correct, varied, formatted the same way, and look like the inputs you will really get.

Why does quality win so clearly? The model copies whatever it sees, including the mistakes. If 5% of 50,000 examples have wrong answers, that is 2,500 lessons in being wrong. And if half the answers are long and half are one line, the model learns to be unpredictable about length. A small, clean, consistent set gives one clear signal to copy.

✅ Good dataset

500–5,000 examples. Real user phrasing, including messy questions. A consistent answer format. Correctness checked by a human. A held-out test set drawn from the same kind of data. Plus examples where the right answer is to refuse, and awkward edge cases.

❌ Bad dataset

50,000 machine-generated examples nobody read. Every question phrased the same way. Answers of wildly different length and format. No examples of what not to do. Test examples that also appear in training. Everything goes perfectly, every time.

Templates and loss masking

Two small mechanical details quietly decide whether a fine-tune works at all:

<|system|>You are a support assistant for Acme Cloud. <|user|>My deploy is stuck in "pending". What do I check? <|assistant|>Check three things, in order: 1. Quota — `acme quota show` …

The struck-through tokens are masked out of the loss, meaning they don't count toward the score. You want the model to learn to write the assistant's reply, not to reproduce the prompt. Also, the template must match the one used at inference exactly, character for character. A single mismatched special token is the most common silent reason a fine-tune “did nothing”.

A quick example of why masking matters. Say each example has a 200-token system prompt and question, and a 50-token answer. Without masking, 200 of every 250 tokens in the loss — 80% — are the model learning to predict the prompt, which it will never have to write. With masking, 100% of the training signal goes into the answer.

Before the table below, one bit of arithmetic that helps read it. With 800 examples and an effective batch of 16, one epoch is 800 ÷ 16 = 50 optimizer steps. Three epochs is 150 steps, and the model has seen every example three times. That is why epochs stay low: on a small dataset, the model runs out of new things to learn very quickly.

Continued pretraining vs fine-tuning

Sometimes the language of your field is genuinely unusual: legal filings, clinical notes, chip-design netlists, a private codebase. A few thousand instruction examples won't fix that.

What does is continued pretraining (also called domain-adaptive pretraining): keep doing next-token prediction on billions of tokens from your domain, then do instruction tuning afterwards. Budget it like a small pretraining run, and mix in general data so the model doesn't forget everything else.

How do you know your domain is unusual enough? Two cheap checks. First, tokenize some domain text: if common terms of your field split into many small pieces, the model has seen little of that language. Second, measure the base model's loss (or perplexity) on held-out domain text and compare it with its loss on general text. A large gap means the model finds your language hard to predict, and continued pretraining has something to fix. A small gap means instruction tuning alone is probably enough.

🧭
The realistic order of attempts

1) Write a good prompt, with examples in it. 2) Add retrieval. 3) Build an eval set — before you train anything. 4) LoRA fine-tune on a few thousand good examples. 5) Add more data, or move to full fine-tuning, if the evals justify it. 6) Consider continued pretraining only for genuinely unusual domains.

Most teams jump straight to step 4 and skip step 3. Then they cannot tell whether it helped.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Fine-tuning to add facts. Use retrieval for facts and fine-tuning for behaviour.
  • No eval set built before training. Then you can't tell an improvement from a regression, and you ship on gut feeling.
  • Training on the prompt tokens. The model learns to write the question as well as the answer, which wastes capacity and makes outputs worse.
  • Using a different chat template at inference. This quietly throws away most of the gain. Check the exact special tokens.
  • Too many epochs. Three passes over 800 examples will memorise them. You'll see eval loss start rising while training loss keeps falling.
  • Measuring only your target task. Check general ability too, or you will ship a model that forgot how to write code.

Interview drill

Warm-up: Why does LoRA work — what's the assumption?

How a strong answer sounds
The assumption is that the weight change needed to adapt a pretrained model to a narrow task is simple — it has low intrinsic rank. The model already has the abilities; fine-tuning mostly re-weights and re-routes them rather than building new structure. So ΔW ≈ BA with r much smaller than d captures most of the useful change. In practice that also means the adapter is tiny, initialising B to zero makes training start from exactly the base model, and adapters can be swapped in per request. Where the assumption breaks — a genuinely new domain, or long continued pretraining — full-rank training does better.

Deeper: Design fine-tuning for 200 enterprise customers who each want a model in their own voice.

How a strong answer sounds
One shared base model plus 200 LoRA adapters of a few megabytes each — never 200 full model copies. For serving, use an engine that supports multi-LoRA, so requests from different customers batch together on one GPU with their own adapter applied. That keeps the GPU busy. Each customer gets their own dataset, their own eval set, and a versioned adapter you can roll back. Training is a templated job, so onboarding a customer is data collection, not engineering. I'd mix in general instruction data so each adapter doesn't cause forgetting, and keep strict separation between customers for both training data and adapter files (Chapters 37, 39). If one customer has huge volume and tight latency needs, merge their adapter into a dedicated copy of the model.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Fine-tune for behaviour and format. Use retrieval for facts.
  • LoRA learns a small low-rank change: only ~0.1–1% of parameters are trained, adapters are megabytes, and quality is close to full fine-tuning.
  • QLoRA squeezes the frozen base model to 4-bit, so large models fine-tune on a single GPU.
  • A few thousand excellent examples in a consistent template beat tens of thousands of mediocre ones.
  • Always test general ability alongside your target task, so you catch forgetting before shipping.

Check your understanding

Your support bot must answer from a knowledge base that changes weekly. Best approach?

Facts that change belong in an index you can update in seconds, and which can cite its sources. Fine-tuning would mean retraining every week — and the model would still make up specifics.

With d = 4096 and rank 16, how many parameters does one LoRA-adapted matrix train?

Two matrices of 4096×16, which is about 0.8% of the full matrix. That ratio is why adapters are only megabytes and why training memory drops so far.

After fine-tuning on support tickets, your model writes great support replies but its coding ability dropped sharply. What happened?

Training hard on one narrow kind of data pulls the model away from everything else. Mix in general data, use fewer epochs and a lower learning rate, and run general benchmarks before shipping.

Why mask prompt tokens out of the loss during instruction tuning?

If prompts are not masked, the model spends capacity learning to write input text. That is not what you want, and it measurably weakens the answers.

You need to serve 50 customer-specific models on limited GPUs. Best design?

Multi-LoRA serving batches requests from different customers against one shared base model, applying each request's own adapter. It is the standard way to make per-customer models affordable. Merging all 50 together would just average the behaviours into mush.
