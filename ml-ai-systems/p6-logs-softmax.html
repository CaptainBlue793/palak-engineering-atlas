Logs, Exponentials & Softmax

Open any ML paper or training script and you'll find exp and log everywhere: in softmax, in the loss, in learning-rate schedules, in the scaling-law charts. They're not there for decoration. Exponentials turn any number into a positive one and exaggerate differences; logarithms turn multiplication into addition and tame huge ranges. This chapter builds both from scratch and then uses them to explain the three functions at the heart of classification: sigmoid, softmax and cross-entropy loss.

Exponentials: repeated multiplication

An exponential is repeated multiplication: 2³ = 2 × 2 × 2 = 8. The small raised number is the exponent (or power). A few rules follow directly from “count the multiplications”:

  • aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ — three 2s times four 2s is seven 2s.
  • a⁰ = 1 and a⁻ⁿ = 1 / aⁿ — so 10⁻³ = 0.001. Negative exponents give small numbers, never negative ones.

Exponential growth is what happens when something multiplies by the same factor each step. Doubling from 1, thirty times, reaches over a billion (2³⁰ ≈ 1.07 × 10⁹). That's why exponentials feel slow at first and then explode.

The special number e

ML uses one base above all others: e ≈ 2.718. It's the base where the function eˣ has a remarkable property: its slope at every point equals its own value. That makes derivatives of formulas built from eˣ unusually clean, which is why it appears inside sigmoid, softmax and the normal distribution. In code it's np.exp(x).

➕

Always positive

eˣ is above zero for every x: e⁻¹⁰ ≈ 0.0000454, e⁰ = 1, e¹⁰ ≈ 22,026. So exponentiating any score gives a positive number — handy for building probabilities.

🔍

Exaggerates gaps

Scores 2 and 1 differ by 1; e² ≈ 7.39 and e¹ ≈ 2.72 differ by a factor of e. Adding 1 to a score always multiplies its exponential by 2.718.

💥

Overflows fast

e⁷¹⁰ is about the largest number a 64-bit float can hold; e¹⁰⁰⁰ becomes infinity. In 16-bit formats the limit is far lower (P8). This will matter for softmax.

Logarithms: the reverse question

A logarithm answers: “what power do I need?”. log₁₀(1000) = 3 because 10³ = 1000. log₂(8) = 3 because 2³ = 8. The natural log, ln(x) or np.log(x), uses base e: ln(eˣ) = x. In ML papers and code, “log” almost always means the natural log.

Some values worth recognising, because they show up in training logs: ln(0.9) ≈ −0.105, ln(0.5) ≈ −0.693, ln(0.1) ≈ −2.303, ln(0.01) ≈ −4.605. And one more: a model guessing uniformly among 50,000 tokens has loss ln(50,000) ≈ 10.8 — which is roughly where language-model training loss starts before it learns anything.

Log scales tame huge ranges

Model sizes run from millions to trillions of parameters; incomes from hundreds to billions; word frequencies from once to billions of times. On an ordinary axis, everything but the biggest values is squashed against zero. On a log scale, each step is a multiplication (×10), so all of it fits on one chart and equal ratios look like equal distances. Scaling laws (Chapter 16) are straight lines on log–log plots, which is how people noticed them.

The same trick helps models. A feature like “purchase amount” with a long tail (most £5–£50, a few £10,000) is often replaced by log(1 + amount) before training. The huge values stop dominating, and the model sees relative differences — £10 vs £20 matters as much as £1,000 vs £2,000 (Chapter 4).

Why models use log-probabilities

A language model assigns a probability to each token in a sentence. The probability of the whole sentence is the product of them all. Even if every token gets a healthy 0.5, a 1,000-token document has probability 0.5¹⁰⁰⁰ ≈ 10⁻³⁰¹ — right at the edge of what a 64-bit float can store, and far beyond a 32-bit float, which gives up around 10⁻⁴⁵ and just returns 0. This is called underflow.

Logs fix it: the log of the product is the sum of the logs. 1,000 × ln(0.5) = −693 — a perfectly ordinary number. So models store and add log-probabilities, and only exponentiate at the very end if they need to.

Multiplying many probabilities vs adding logsInteractive
float32 can't represent numbers smaller than about 1.4 × 10⁻⁴⁵, so the product becomes exactly 0 — and a 0 can't be compared, trained on, or recovered. The sum of logs never has that problem. Its average, negated, is the per-token loss; exponentiating that gives perplexity, a common language-model metric.

Sigmoid: a score becomes a probability

A binary classifier first computes a raw score — any real number, like a dot product of weights and features. To turn it into a probability between 0 and 1, it applies the sigmoid (or logistic) function:

σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e⁻ᶻ)

Big positive scores give probabilities near 1; big negative scores give probabilities near 0; a score of 0 gives exactly 0.5. The raw score is called a logit, and it has a neat meaning: it's the log-odds, ln(p / (1 − p)). A logit of 2 means odds of e² ≈ 7.4 to 1, or p ≈ 0.88.

The sigmoid curveInteractive
The amber curve is the slope, σ(z)(1 − σ(z)). It peaks at just 0.25 when z = 0 and almost vanishes for large |z| — the reason sigmoid layers deep in a network make gradients vanish (P4), and why modern networks use ReLU inside and keep sigmoid for the final yes/no output.

Logistic regression, the classic classifier, is exactly this: a dot product of weights and features, passed through a sigmoid (Chapter 6). It's also the last step of every binary neural classifier.

Softmax: many scores become a distribution

With more than two classes — cat, dog, fox; or 50,000 possible next tokens — the model produces one logit per class. Softmax turns them into probabilities that are all positive and add up to 1:

softmax(z)ᵢ = e^zᵢ / Σⱼ e^zⱼ

In words: exponentiate each logit (making it positive and exaggerating the gaps), then divide by the total so they sum to 1. Worked example with logits [2.0, 1.0, 0.1]: exponentials are 7.39, 2.72, 1.11, total 11.21, so the probabilities are 0.659, 0.242, 0.099. The biggest logit gets the lion's share, but everyone gets something. (With two classes, softmax reduces to the sigmoid.)

Temperature: how adventurous a model is

When a language model writes text, it picks each next token by sampling from its softmax distribution. Temperature controls how adventurous that sampling is: divide every logit by T before the softmax. With T close to 0, the top token gets nearly all the probability and output becomes predictable (and can get repetitive). With T around 1, you sample the model's real distribution. With T above 1, unlikely tokens get a bigger share and output becomes more varied — and more error-prone. Try it above, and see Chapter 19 for how it's used in practice.

Cross-entropy: the loss you'll use most

Once a model outputs probabilities, how do you score it? For one example, the loss is −ln(p), where p is the probability the model gave to the correct answer. This is cross-entropy, also called log-loss or negative log-likelihood. It's the training loss for nearly every classifier and every language model.

Probability given to the correct answerLoss −ln(p)Reads as
0.990.01Confident and right: almost no penalty
0.90.105Right and fairly sure
0.50.693A coin-flip guess
0.12.303Leaning wrong
0.014.605Confidently wrong: 44× the loss of p = 0.9

That lopsided shape is the point. Being confidently wrong costs far more than being unsure, which pushes models to be calibrated — to say 70% when they're right about 70% of the time. For binary problems the same idea is written as binary cross-entropy: −[y·ln(p) + (1 − y)·ln(1 − p)], where y is 1 or 0. Only one of the two terms is ever active, so it's just “−ln of the probability given to what actually happened”. Over a dataset, you average the per-example losses.

Why logs rather than, say, (1 − p)²? Two reasons. Minimising −ln(p) over a dataset is the same as maximising the probability the model assigns to the whole dataset (the product becomes a sum, as above) — a principled goal called maximum likelihood. And the maths works out beautifully: combined with softmax, the gradient of the loss with respect to each logit is simply predicted probability − target, which trains quickly and stably (Chapter 8).

Numerical stability: softmax without overflow

Softmax on logits like [1000, 999] should give [0.73, 0.27], but np.exp(1000) overflows to infinity and the answer becomes NaN. The fix uses a simple fact: subtracting the same number from every logit doesn't change the softmax (it multiplies top and bottom by the same factor). So subtract the largest logit first — then the biggest exponent is e⁰ = 1 and nothing overflows:

import numpy as np

def softmax(z):
    e = np.exp(z - z.max())                   # subtract the max first: same answer, no overflow
    return e / e.sum()

z = np.array([2.0, 1.0, 0.1])
p = softmax(z)
print(p.round(3))                          # [0.659 0.242 0.099]
print(-np.log(p[0]).round(3))               # 0.417  — log-loss if "cat" is correct
print(softmax(np.array([1000.0, 999.0])))     # [0.731 0.269] — no overflow

The same idea gives the log-sum-exp trick for computing log-probabilities directly without ever forming tiny numbers. You won't implement it — frameworks do, which is exactly why you should use their combined functions (such as PyTorch's cross_entropy, which takes raw logits) rather than applying softmax and log yourself.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Taking the exponential of big logits directly. exp(1000) overflows to infinity. Subtract the largest logit first, or use the framework's built-in softmax and loss.
  • Applying softmax twice. Many loss functions expect raw logits and apply softmax inside. Passing probabilities instead silently weakens training.
  • Taking log of 0. np.log(0) is −∞. Clip probabilities (e.g. to at least 1e-12) or work with logits.
  • Multiplying many probabilities. Add log-probabilities instead to avoid underflow.
  • Reading softmax outputs as guaranteed confidence. Probabilities can be miscalibrated; check calibration before using them for decisions (Ch 7).

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Why is cross-entropy used as the loss for classifiers, rather than accuracy?

How a strong answer sounds
Accuracy only counts right or wrong, so it doesn't change when the model becomes a bit more or less confident — it gives no smooth slope to follow, and gradient descent needs one. Cross-entropy, −ln of the probability given to the correct class, changes smoothly with every prediction, rewards putting more probability on the right answer, and punishes confident mistakes heavily. Minimising it is the same as maximising the likelihood of the data, and with softmax its gradient is just prediction minus target, which trains well.

Numeracy: A language model's average training loss is 2.3 nats per token. Roughly how uncertain is it?

How a strong answer sounds
e^2.3 ≈ 10, so its perplexity is about 10: on average it's as uncertain as if choosing uniformly among about 10 tokens. Equivalently, it gives the correct next token a probability of about e^−2.3 ≈ 0.1 on average (a geometric mean). Compared with the ~10.8 loss of random guessing over a 50,000-token vocabulary, that's a huge amount of learned structure.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • eˣ is always positive, grows explosively, exaggerates gaps, and is its own slope. It overflows for large x.
  • A log asks “what power?”. Logs turn products into sums and powers into multipliers; log scales make equal ratios look equal.
  • Models add log-probabilities instead of multiplying probabilities, avoiding underflow.
  • Sigmoid maps a logit to a probability (logit = log-odds); softmax maps several logits to a distribution that sums to 1.
  • Temperature divides logits before softmax: low T is focused, high T is varied.
  • Cross-entropy (log-loss) = −ln(probability of the correct answer): tiny when confident and right, huge when confident and wrong.
  • Subtract the max logit before exponentiating, and prefer the framework's combined softmax-plus-loss functions.

Check your understanding

What is log₂(32)?

The log asks what power of 2 gives 32. 2⁵ = 32, so log₂(32) = 5.

Softmax is applied to logits [3, 3, 3]. What are the probabilities?

Equal logits give equal exponentials, and dividing by their sum gives equal shares. Adding the same amount to every logit never changes a softmax — only the differences matter.

A classifier gives the correct class a probability of 0.01. Using natural logs, roughly what is its cross-entropy loss on that example?

−ln(0.01) ≈ 4.605. Compare with −ln(0.9) ≈ 0.105: confidently wrong costs about 44 times more than being right and fairly sure.

Why do language models sum log-probabilities instead of multiplying probabilities?

0.5 to the power 1,000 is about 10⁻³⁰¹ — below what float32 can store, so it becomes 0. log(a × b) = log a + log b, so the sum of logs carries the same information safely.

You lower a language model's sampling temperature from 1.0 to 0.2. What happens to its output?

Dividing logits by 0.2 multiplies their gaps by 5, so softmax puts almost all probability on the top token. Output becomes consistent but can be repetitive; higher temperatures do the opposite.
