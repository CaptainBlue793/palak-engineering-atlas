What Training a Model Means

This chapter puts the maths of the previous ones to work. Training has four ingredients — data, a model, a loss and an update rule — and every model in this course, from a two-number line to a trillion-parameter language model, uses the same four. You'll train a tiny model step by step, learn the vocabulary (features, labels, batches, epochs, splits), see overfitting happen with your own eyes, and learn the few numbers that say whether a model is any good.

The four ingredients

Strip away the jargon and every training run is the same recipe:

🗂️

1 · Data

Examples of inputs x, usually with the right answers y. Its quality matters more than anything else.

🧩

2 · A model

A function with adjustable numbers called parameters (or weights): prediction = w × x + b, or a network with billions of them.

📏

3 · A loss

One number saying how wrong the predictions are: mean squared error for numbers, cross-entropy for classes.

🔁

4 · An update rule

Use the gradient of the loss to nudge every parameter downhill. Then repeat — thousands to millions of times.

The model's parameters are learned from data. Other settings — the learning rate, the batch size, how big the model is — are chosen by you before training starts. Those are called hyperparameters, and choosing them well is a large part of practical ML (Chapter 9).

Features, labels and kinds of problems

The inputs are called features: the columns describing each example, such as a house's area and age, or an image's pixels. The answers are called labels or targets. Machine-learning problems are sorted by what the labels look like and where they come from:

KindLabelsExampleTypical loss
RegressionA numberPredict a house price, delivery time or demandMean squared error
Binary classificationOne of two classesSpam or not; will this user churn?Binary cross-entropy
Multi-class classificationOne of many classesWhich of 1,000 objects is in this photo?Cross-entropy over a softmax
RankingAn orderWhich 10 of these 5,000 posts to show first?Ranking losses (Ch 42)

And by where the labels come from:

  • Supervised learning: humans or logs provide the labels (“this email was reported as spam”). Most business ML.
  • Self-supervised learning: the labels come free from the data itself. A language model reads text and learns to predict the next token; every sentence gives thousands of free examples. This is how large language models are pretrained (Chapter 16).
  • Unsupervised learning: no labels at all — find structure, such as clusters of similar customers (Chapter 6).
  • Reinforcement learning: learn from rewards for actions, used for example to align language models with human preferences (Chapter 18).

Train, validation and test splits

A model that memorises its training data can score perfectly on it and still fail on anything new. What you actually care about is generalisation: performance on data the model has never seen. The only honest way to measure that is to hold some data back.

Splitting 10,000 labelled examplesInteractive
The bigger the evaluation sets, the more precise your measurements (P5) — but the less data is left to learn from. With millions of examples, 1% each for validation and test is plenty.
🏋️

Training set

The model learns from these: gradients are computed on them. Usually the large majority.

🧭

Validation set

Used by you during development: to choose hyperparameters, compare models and decide when to stop training. Never used for gradients.

🔒

Test set

Locked away and used once at the end, for the final honest estimate. If you keep tuning against it, it quietly becomes a second validation set and stops being honest.

🚰
Data leakage: the silent killer

Leakage is any way information from the evaluation data sneaks into training, making results look better than they'll be in real use. Classic leaks: the same customer appearing in both train and test; computing feature averages over all the data before splitting; random splits of time-ordered data, so the model “sees the future” (for anything that predicts ahead, split by time instead); or a feature that is only known after the event you're predicting. A model that seems too good to be true usually is. Chapter 3 is devoted to catching these.

Training a line, one step at a time

Now watch the four ingredients work together. We'll train a tiny linear regression. The data is four points: (1, 3), (2, 5), (3, 7), (4, 9). Secretly the rule is y = 2x + 1, but the model doesn't know that. It starts from w = 0, b = 0 and uses a learning rate of 0.05.

  1. Four examples, each an input x and the right answer y. In a real project this is thousands to billions of rows, and collecting it well is most of the work (Ch 3).
  2. The model is ŷ = w·x + b (ŷ, “y-hat”, means “predicted y”). With w = 0 and b = 0 it predicts 0 for everything — a flat line. The red dashes are the errors: −3, −5, −7, −9.
  3. MSE = the average of the squared errors = (9 + 25 + 49 + 81) / 4 = 41. Squaring makes every error positive and punishes big misses more. One number now summarises “how wrong”.
  4. How does the loss change if w or b move? Using the chain rule on MSE: ∂L/∂w = (2/n) Σ error × x = −35 and ∂L/∂b = (2/n) Σ error = −12. Both are negative: increasing w and b will reduce the loss.
  5. Step downhill: w = 0 − 0.05 × (−35) = 1.75 and b = 0 − 0.05 × (−12) = 0.6. The line jumps up towards the points.
  6. Recompute with the new line: the loss drops from 41 to 1.13 after one step, and to 0.043 after two. Each loop of predict → loss → gradient → update is one training step.
  7. After about 500 steps, w ≈ 2.000 and b ≈ 1.000: the model has found the hidden rule from examples alone. A deep network does exactly this — with millions of parameters, a fancier model, and a GPU doing the arithmetic (Ch 5, Ch 8).
import numpy as np
x = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 4.0])
y = np.array([3.0, 5.0, 7.0, 9.0])
w, b, lr = 0.0, 0.0, 0.05

for step in range(500):
    pred = w * x + b                  # 1. model makes predictions
    err = pred - y
    loss = (err ** 2).mean()           # 2. loss: mean squared error
    dw = 2 * (err * x).mean()          # 3. gradient (chain rule on MSE)
    db = 2 * err.mean()
    w -= lr * dw                      # 4. update: step downhill
    b -= lr * db

print(round(w, 3), round(b, 3))          # 2.0 1.0

Batches, epochs and steps

With four examples, we used all of them for every step. With 10 million, computing the gradient on everything before each tiny update would be absurdly slow. Instead, each step uses a random mini-batch — say 256 examples. The gradient from a batch is a noisy but good estimate of the full gradient, and you get to take thousands of steps in the time one full pass would take. This is stochastic gradient descent (SGD); “stochastic” means “with randomness”.

WordMeaningExample: 50,000 examples, batch size 100

The data is shuffled each epoch so batches differ, which stops the model from learning the order. Batch size is a trade-off: bigger batches give smoother gradients and use the GPU more efficiently, but need more memory; smaller batches are noisier, which sometimes even helps generalisation. Very large models often train for just one epoch or less over their huge datasets.

Underfitting and overfitting

A model can go wrong in two opposite ways:

📉 Underfitting — too simple

The model can't capture the pattern at all: a straight line through data that curves. Training error and validation error are both high. Fix: a more flexible model, better features, or more training.

🎢 Overfitting — memorising

The model fits the training data's noise, not just its pattern. Training error is low but validation error is high. Fix: more data, a simpler model, regularisation (penalties that keep weights small), or stopping earlier.

See it for yourself. Twelve noisy training points (pink) come from a smooth curve; eight validation points (purple) come from the same curve but weren't used for fitting. Increase the flexibility of the model — the degree of a polynomial — and watch both errors.

Model flexibility vs errorSimulator

That U-shape — validation error falling, then rising again as flexibility grows — is the most important picture in practical ML. You choose the model (or when to stop training) at the bottom of the validation curve, never by training error. Deep networks add surprising twists to this story, but checking validation error is always the first move (Chapter 7, Chapter 9).

Is the model any good?

A single number rarely tells the whole story. Here are the basics you'll meet everywhere; Chapter 7 goes deep.

For a binary classifier, every prediction lands in one of four boxes — the confusion matrix. Say a spam filter looks at 1,000 emails, 100 of which are spam:

30false positives (false alarms)

  • Accuracy = correct / all = (80 + 870) / 1,000 = 95%. Easy to read, but misleading when classes are imbalanced: “never spam” scores 90% here.
  • Precision = of the emails flagged, how many were really spam = 80 / (80 + 30) = 73%. Matters when false alarms are costly.
  • Recall = of the real spam, how much was caught = 80 / (80 + 20) = 80%. Matters when misses are costly.

Raising the model's threshold usually trades recall for precision; which one matters more depends on the costs, exactly as in the expected-value reasoning of P5. For regression, you'll see MAE (mean absolute error, in the target's units: “off by £12,000 on average”) and RMSE (the square root of MSE, which weighs big misses more).

📐
Always compare with a baseline

A number means nothing without something to compare it to. Before celebrating a model, check the simplest possible alternatives: always predict the most common class; always predict the average; a one-rule heuristic someone already uses. A deep model that beats “always predict the average” by 1% probably isn't worth deploying. Baselines also catch bugs: if your model can't beat them, something is broken.

Training vs inference, and the bigger picture

Training is the loop above: it changes the parameters and is expensive, done once or on a schedule. Inference is using the finished model to make predictions — just the forward calculation, with no loss and no updates — and it happens on every user request. The two have very different engineering needs: training wants throughput on huge batches; inference wants low latency and low cost per request. Most of Levels 3 and 4 of this course are about doing each at scale (Chapter 26).

And training is only one stage of a longer lifecycle: define the problem and the metric, collect and clean data, train and evaluate, deploy, then monitor — because the world changes and models go stale (drift) — and retrain. Chapter 1 opens the main course with exactly that picture.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Judging a model by training error. Memorising is easy. Only validation and test error tell you about new data.
  • Tuning on the test set. Every peek-and-adjust leaks information; keep the test set for the end.
  • Random splits of time-ordered data. The model learns from the future. Split by time for forecasting-like problems.
  • Reporting accuracy on imbalanced data. Use precision, recall and a baseline.
  • Computing preprocessing statistics on all the data. Fit scalers and encoders on the training set only, then apply them to validation and test.
  • Forgetting to shuffle. Batches sorted by label or date teach the model the order, not the pattern.
🎤
Interview drill

Diagnose: Training accuracy is 99% and validation accuracy is 71%. What's going on, and what would you try?

How a strong answer sounds
That big gap is classic overfitting: the model has memorised training examples rather than learning patterns that generalise. First I'd check for bugs and leakage — duplicates, or train and validation drawn differently. Then: get more or augmented data, add regularisation (weight decay, dropout), use a simpler model or fewer features, and use early stopping on validation loss. I'd plot training and validation curves over epochs to see where they start to diverge.

Count: 1.2 million training images, batch size 256, 30 epochs. How many training steps is that?

How a strong answer sounds
Steps per epoch = 1,200,000 ÷ 256 ≈ 4,688 (rounding up for the last partial batch). Times 30 epochs ≈ 140,600 steps. If each step takes 0.3 seconds on the hardware, that's about 42,000 seconds, or roughly 12 hours — the kind of quick estimate that tells you whether an experiment fits in a day.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Training = data → model → loss → gradient → update, repeated. Parameters are learned; hyperparameters are chosen by you.
  • Regression predicts numbers, classification predicts classes. Labels come from people (supervised), from the data itself (self-supervised), or not at all (unsupervised).
  • Train on the training set, tune on the validation set, report once on the test set. Guard against leakage; split by time when predicting the future.
  • SGD uses mini-batches; an epoch is one pass over the data; steps per epoch = examples ÷ batch size.
  • Underfitting: both errors high. Overfitting: training low, validation high. Choose by validation error.
  • Accuracy misleads on imbalanced data; precision and recall trade off. Always compare with a baseline.
  • Training is occasional and expensive; inference happens on every request. Both live inside a longer lifecycle with monitoring and retraining.

Check your understanding

What is the validation set used for?

The validation set guides your decisions during development without being used for gradients. The test set is kept for the final estimate; using it for decisions would make that estimate optimistic.

A dataset has 60,000 training examples and the batch size is 200. How many steps make one epoch?

An epoch is one pass over all the data: 60,000 ÷ 200 = 300 batches, and each batch is one step.

Training error is high and validation error is almost the same, also high. What is most likely?

If the model can't even fit the training data, it's underfitting. Overfitting shows a big gap between low training error and high validation error.

A fraud model flags 200 transactions; 50 of them are fraud. There were 100 fraud cases in total. What are its precision and recall?

Precision = true positives ÷ flagged = 50 ÷ 200 = 25%. Recall = true positives ÷ all real fraud = 50 ÷ 100 = 50%.

You're predicting next week's sales from past weeks. Why is a random train/test split a problem?

When the goal is to predict the future, evaluation must mimic that: train on earlier data, test on later data. A random split leaks future information into training.
