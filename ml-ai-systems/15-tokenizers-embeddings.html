Tokenizers & Embeddings

A model never sees text. It sees numbers. First the text is cut into pieces called tokens, and each token becomes an id number. Then that id is looked up in a big table to get a vector. Tokenization decides your costs, how fairly other languages are treated, and several famous model failures. Embeddings — those vectors — turn meaning into geometry, which is the base of every retrieval system you will build.

Why tokens exist

A model needs a fixed list of symbols it can handle — its vocabulary. Two obvious choices both fail:

Whole words

The list never ends: names, typos, compound words, new slang. And every word you didn't plan for becomes <UNK> (unknown) — a hole in the text the model can never fill in.

Single characters

A tiny vocabulary and nothing unknown. But sequences get about 5× longer, and attention cost grows with the square of length, so 5× longer is ~25× the attention work. Each token also carries almost no meaning on its own.

Subword tokenization takes the middle path. Common words become one token. Rare words split into meaningful pieces — “unbelievably” might become “un” + “believ” + “ably”. Anything left over falls back to raw bytes, so no text is ever impossible to represent.

Why you should care: API prices, rate limits and context windows are all counted in tokens, not words. A handy rule for English is that 1,000 words is about 1,300 tokens. So at a price of, say, $3 per million input tokens, sending one million 1,000-word documents through a model is about 1.3 billion tokens, or roughly $3,900. The same documents in a language the tokenizer handles badly can cost two or three times that.

How BPE learns its vocabulary

Nobody writes the vocabulary by hand. Byte-pair encoding (BPE) learns it from data:

  1. Start with every byte as its own token.
  2. Count every pair of neighbouring tokens across a huge pile of text. Merge the most common pair into one new token.
  3. Repeat until you reach the vocabulary size you want (for example, 100,000 merges).

Common sequences like the, ing and => end up as single tokens, while rare words stay split into pieces.

Here is a tiny dry run. The training text is the word “hug” 3 times, “hugs” 2 times and “bug” once. Start from single letters.

  1. Count pairs. u g appears in every word: 3 + 2 + 1 = 6 times. h u appears 5 times, g s 2 times, b u once. The winner is u g, so add the token ug.
  2. Count again on the merged text: h ug ×3, h ug s ×2, b ug ×1. Now h ug appears 5 times, so add hug.
  3. Once more: hug s appears 2 times — the most common pair left — so add hugs.

The tokenizer now knows ug, hug and hugs. To encode a new word, it replays the merges in the order they were learned. “bugs” becomes b + ug + s: three tokens, and no “unknown” needed, even though “bugs” was never in the training text.

There are variants. WordPiece (used by BERT) picks merges by how much they improve the model's likelihood, not by raw count. Unigram (SentencePiece) starts with a big vocabulary and trims it down. In practice all three behave much the same.

🌍
Tokenization is a fairness and cost issue

A tokenizer trained mostly on English packs ~4 characters into a token for English, but only 1–2 for Hindi, Thai or Amharic. So the same sentence costs 2–4× more tokens. It also fills the context window faster and costs more to serve — and this hits the languages least likely to get attention.

When you pick or train a tokenizer, measure its fertility (tokens per word) in every language you support.

From token id to vector

The embedding table is just a big lookup table: one row per vocabulary entry, each row a vector of d numbers. Token id 15,231 means “take row 15,231”.

Those rows are learned during training. For a 100k vocabulary with d=4096, the table holds ~410M parameters — and there is one at the input and one at the output. That is why the two are often tied, meaning they share the same weights.

Why can they share? At the input, a row answers “what vector stands for this token?”. At the output, the model scores each possible next token by the dot product of its final hidden vector with that token's row: a high score means “my output points the same way as this token”. Both jobs want similar tokens to have similar rows, so one table can serve both. (Looking up a row is also the same as multiplying a one-hot vector by the table, which is why the lookup can be trained like any other layer.)

ids   = tokenizer("hello world")      # [15496, 1917]
x     = embedding_table[ids]          # (2, 4096)
h     = transformer(x)                # (2, 4096) contextual representations
logits = h @ embedding_table.T        # tied weights: (2, vocab)
📐
Static vs contextual embeddings

The embedding table gives one fixed vector per token. In it, “bank” is the same vector everywhere.

After a few transformer blocks, the vector becomes contextual: the “bank” in river bank and the “bank” in bank account have drifted apart. Modern embedding models are transformers whose contextual outputs get combined (“pooled”) into one vector per sentence or document.

Embedding geometry

In an embedding space, meaning becomes direction. Similar items point the same way. The standard score is cosine similarity: the cosine of the angle between two vectors, where 1 means same direction and 0 means unrelated (Chapter 2). Click a word to see its neighbours in this toy space.

The formula is cos = (a·b) ÷ (|a| × |b|): the dot product divided by both lengths. Three quick cases:

  • a = [1, 2], b = [2, 4]: a·b = 2 + 8 = 10, |a| = √5, |b| = √20, and √5 × √20 = 10. So cos = 1. b is just a longer copy of a — same direction, same meaning.
  • a = [1, 2], c = [2, −1]: a·c = 2 − 2 = 0, so cos = 0. At right angles: unrelated.
  • Why divide by length? A long document may get a longer vector just from having more words. Cosine ignores length and compares only direction, so a long and a short text on the same topic still score high.

Real embedding spaces have 384–4,096 dimensions. In so many dimensions, almost every pair of random vectors points in a near-unrelated direction (they are close to orthogonal, at right angles). The space is mostly empty — which is exactly what makes “find the nearest vectors” meaningful (Chapter 20).

How embedding models are trained

They are trained by contrastive learning: pull matching pairs closer together in the space, and push everything else apart.

The matching pairs come from data that is already paired up: a question and its answer, a title and its article, a search query and the document the user clicked, an image and its caption.

How one training step works, with a batch of 4 question–answer pairs. Embed all 4 questions and all 4 answers, then compute every question-to-answer similarity: a 4×4 grid. The diagonal cells (question 1 with answer 1, and so on) are the true pairs; the other 12 cells are wrong pairs. For each row, apply softmax and use cross-entropy to push the diagonal cell's probability toward 1. Training raises the true pairs' similarity and lowers the rest, and nobody had to label a single “wrong” example.

🧲

In-batch negatives

Every other item in the same batch counts as a “wrong” pairing to push away. It costs nothing extra, and bigger batches give more of them — which is why embedding training likes very large batches.

🎯

Hard negatives

Use a quick first-pass search to find documents that look almost right, then train the model to tell them apart from the true match. This is usually the single biggest quality jump.

🪆

Matryoshka

Named after nesting dolls: train so the first 64, 128 or 256 numbers of each vector are already useful on their own. You can then cut vectors short to save 8× the storage, and re-rank the top hits with the full vector.

🔢
Pooling and normalization matter

You have to turn many token vectors into one. Averaging them, taking the special [CLS] position, or taking the last token all give different answers. Use whatever the model's documentation says, or your similarity scores will be quietly wrong.

Then L2-normalize: scale each vector to length 1. Once vectors have length 1, the dot product is the cosine similarity, which makes vector databases faster and results consistent.

Example: [3, 4] has length √(9 + 16) = 5, so it normalizes to [0.6, 0.8], whose length is exactly 1. Since the lengths in the cosine formula are now both 1, the division drops out.

The systems view: storage and re-embedding

Embeddings are data. You have to store them, index them, back them up and one day regenerate them all. Work out the bill before you pick a dimension.

The arithmetic is simple: number of vectors × dimensions × bytes per number. For 10 million chunks at 768 dimensions in float32 (4 bytes): 10,000,000 × 768 × 4 ≈ 31 GB. The same vectors in int8 (1 byte) are about 8 GB, which can fit in one server's RAM. Cutting to 256 dimensions with a Matryoshka model divides it by 3 again. Each choice trades a little retrieval quality for a lot of memory, so measure the quality loss on your own queries before committing.

Pin the embedding model version

Vectors from two versions of a model are not comparable — not even a little. Each model builds its own space, so comparing across them is meaningless.

Upgrading the embedder means re-embedding the whole corpus and rebuilding the index. Normally you do it behind a shadow index, writing to both old and new, so search never breaks. Store the model name and version next to every vector, and treat an upgrade as a migration with a rollback plan (Chapter 21).

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Comparing vectors from different models or versions. You still get numbers out, and they mean nothing.
  • Forgetting to normalize when the index expects cosine — or normalizing twice, then wondering why all the scores look the same.
  • Using the wrong pooling for the model. It silently costs 5–15 points of retrieval quality.
  • Counting characters instead of tokens when budgeting cost and context. Non-English text and code wreck that estimate.
  • Chunking text without thinking about tokens. “500 words” is about 700 tokens in English and 2,000 in Japanese. Chunk by tokens, with some overlap (Chapter 21).
  • Asking a model to play with letters and deciding it can't reason. It cannot see letters at all — that is a tokenization artefact.

Interview drill

Warm-up: Why do LLMs fail at counting letters in a word?

How a strong answer sounds
The model never sees characters, only token ids. “strawberry” might be two or three tokens, and none of them show their spelling. So the model has to recall the spelling from memory instead of looking at it. That is a limit of how the input is represented, not of reasoning — which is why the same model can write a correct program that counts the letters. Workarounds: have it call a tool or write code, or use character-level or digit-split tokenization where it matters.

Deeper: You're choosing an embedding dimension for 200M chunks. Walk through the decision.

How a strong answer sounds
Start with retrieval quality on my data, not a public leaderboard. I'd build a small set of queries with known right answers and measure recall@k for a few models and dimensions. Then I'd price it out: 200M × 1024 dims × 4 bytes is ~820 GB before the index, versus ~205 GB at int8 or ~100 GB at 384 dimensions. That decides whether it fits in RAM, and RAM is the main cost of a vector index. I'd prefer a Matryoshka-capable model, so I can store short vectors for the first pass and re-rank the top few hundred with full vectors, plus quantization on top. Finally, I'd pin the model version and plan the re-embedding migration before launch, because that cost comes back with every upgrade.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Subword tokenization balances vocabulary size against sequence length. Falling back to bytes means no text is ever unrepresentable.
  • Tokens — not characters — decide context limits and cost, and token counts vary hugely across languages and code.
  • Embeddings turn meaning into direction. Normalize your vectors, and use the pooling the model asks for.
  • Embedding models are trained by pulling matching pairs together. Hard negatives usually give the biggest gains.
  • Vectors are infrastructure. Budget the storage, and treat an embedder upgrade as a full re-index migration.

Check your understanding

Why do most LLMs use subword tokenization rather than words?

A fixed vocabulary plus a byte fallback means you never get an <UNK>, and sequences stay far shorter than they would be one character at a time.

The same paragraph costs 180 tokens in English and 520 in Thai. Why?

BPE merges are learned from whatever text trained the tokenizer. Scripts that barely appeared stay close to one token per byte: more tokens, more cost, less usable context.

You upgrade your embedding model to a new version. What must happen?

Different models build different spaces, so mixing their vectors makes similarity meaningless. You need a full re-index, usually done behind a shadow index while writing to both.

What are “hard negatives” in contrastive embedding training?

Random wrong documents are too easy to tell apart, so the model learns little. Near-misses, found with a quick first-pass search, force it to learn the fine distinctions that actually matter at search time.

You store 50M embeddings at 1536 dimensions in float32. Roughly how much raw vector storage is that?

50e6 × 1536 × 4 bytes ≈ 307 GB, and that is before the index adds its own overhead. This arithmetic is why int8 quantization and shortening vectors are standard practice.
