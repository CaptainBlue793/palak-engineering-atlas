Case Study: Real-Time Fraud Detection

Score every card payment in 50 milliseconds. Catch fraud that makes up just 0.15% of transactions. And do it against attackers who are actively probing your defences and will switch tactics the week after you launch. The hardest parts of this problem are not the modelling.

  • Turn fraud losses and wrongly declined payments into one clear decision rule
  • Build live counting (velocity) and network (graph) features within 50 ms
  • Train when labels arrive late, are biased, and keep shifting because attackers adapt
  • Design the human review process as part of the system, not bolted on afterwards
  • Keep the system honest when the attacker adapts
📚
What you need before this chapter
  • Read precision, recall and cost-based thresholds.
  • Build event streams and time-windowed features.
  • Monitor changing data when labels arrive late.

1 · Requirements

At a 0.15% fraud rate, one million transactions contain about 1,500 fraudulent ones. A system approving everything would be 99.85% accurate, yet catch none of them. That is why you measure fraud recall, genuine-customer declines and money lost together. Treat the stated 12,000 transactions/second as a peak; multiplying it by a whole day would overestimate daily volume unless that peak is sustained.

The first design question: what happens if the model is down?

Fail open means approving everything, so you lose money to fraud until the model is back. Fail closed means declining everything, which stops all revenue and hurts far more.

The real answer is a ladder of fallbacks: the model → a cached score for this card → a small set of rules → approve, but only up to a cautious amount.

Decide this in advance, write it down, and practise it. It's a business decision, not an engineering one.

🎯
Metrics

Primary (online): net fraud loss per £1,000 processed, plus the false-decline rate (genuine customers wrongly refused). Always report the two together, or someone will improve one by quietly ruining the other. Offline: PR-AUC (a precision-recall score that suits rare events), and recall at the precision level the review team can keep up with. Guardrails: approval rate by customer segment, p99 latency, how deep the review queue gets, and how often appeals overturn a decline.
Baseline: the rules engine you already have. It will be strong on known fraud patterns and useless on new ones — which is exactly the argument for adding a model.

😤
False declines cost more than fraud

Experience across the industry is consistent: wrongly declining genuine customers costs several times more than the fraud you prevent. A declined customer often never comes back — and tells other people.

A model that cuts fraud by 30% but raises false declines by 1 percentage point is usually a net loss.

That's why the decision threshold must come from the expected cost of each mistake (Chapter 7), not from a default setting — and why the false-decline rate belongs right next to the loss number in every report.

2 · Features inside 50 ms

Make the latency budget explicit: for example, 5 ms routing, 15 ms feature reads, 10 ms scoring and 5 ms policy evaluation. That leaves 15 ms for queueing and variation within the 50 ms target. These are illustrative allowances; verify the end-to-end p99 under load. At 12,000 requests/second and a measured mean service time of 25 ms, about 300 requests are in service concurrently.

A velocity counter needs a definition for retries. Count distinct payment attempts by an attempt ID, while preserving genuinely new attempts from the same card. Otherwise a network retry can make one customer look like an attack burst. Store the feature timestamp too: zero recent attempts and a counter that has not refreshed for an hour are different inputs.

Graph features precomputed, refreshed near-line

“This device has been used with 14 different cards in the last hour” is one of the strongest fraud signals there is — and one of the most expensive to compute.

The trick: keep the network of links between cards, devices, emails and addresses up to date in a streaming job. For each entity, store simple summary numbers — how many links it has, how many distinct cards, how risky its cluster looks — in the online store. At scoring time you just read them like any other feature.

The latency budget is met, the signal survives, and the graph job is where the interesting engineering happens.

3 · Defence in depth

Return a decision record containing the model version, policy version, feature times and reason codes. The same score can produce a different action after a policy change, so storing only the score cannot explain a decline. Give temporary incident rules an owner and expiry. Test their effect on recent legitimate traffic before rollout whenever the incident permits.

RulesOutright blocks for known-bad cards, devices and accounts, sanctions lists, and hard limits on how fast an entity can transact. You can update them instantly — which matters during a live attack, when waiting for a retrain isn't an option.
ModelGradient-boosted trees over the features above. They run fast on ordinary CPUs, cope with mixed data types and missing values out of the box, and retrain in minutes (Chapter 6).
PolicyA separate threshold for each amount range and customer segment, worked out from the expected cost of each mistake. Asking for extra proof of identity (3-D Secure, a one-time code) sits between approve and decline — and it's often the most valuable option you have.
Human reviewA limited-size queue holding the most valuable uncertain cases. It's also your fastest source of labels and your best view of new attack patterns.
Post-hocBatch jobs that spot patterns only visible across many transactions — networks of money-mule accounts, bursts of card testing, merchants working with fraudsters — and feed what they find back into the rules and the graph.

4 · Deep dive: thresholds by amount band

The threshold follows from comparing two expected costs. Let p be fraud probability, A the loss if fraud is approved, and C the cost of declining a genuine payment. Approval costs p × A on average; decline costs (1 − p) × C. Decline when p × A exceeds (1 − p) × C, which rearranges to p > C ÷ (A + C). With C = £18, the threshold is 60% for A = £12, but about 0.45% for A = £4,000.

This calculation assumes calibrated probabilities and those two costs only. Recoveries, chargeback fees, customer effects and authentication costs change the comparison. The simulator uses illustrative detection curves, so it demonstrates a decision rule rather than proving an optimal production threshold. Estimate costs from your own outcomes before applying the rule.

5 · Deep dive: labels are late, biased and adversarial

Keep event time and label time separately. A payment made on 1 June and disputed on 20 July must retain its 1 June feature snapshot. Treat it as unresolved until the relevant observation window closes, rather than quietly calling it legitimate. Evaluate mature cohorts by transaction date so a recent drop in observed chargebacks is not mistaken for improved detection.

A random approval sample only estimates outcomes for the population eligible for that sample. A low-amount cap cannot tell you how blocked high-value payments would have behaved. Record selection probabilities and report that coverage limit. Human review and appeals add evidence, but they also have selection bias and do not automatically produce an unbiased test set.

⏳

Late

A chargeback (the customer disputing the payment) is confirmed 30–90 days later. So any performance number you compute today describes a model from three months ago (Chapter 34).

🎭

Biased

You only find out what happens to payments you approved. Declined ones never get a label — the classic selection-bias trap in fraud.

🥷

Adversarial

The patterns change because of your model. Whatever gave fraudsters away yesterday is what they carefully avoid today.

🔍
Recovering labels for what you declined

Three practical routes:

  • Approve a small random sample of payments you would have declined, capped at a low amount. It's a deliberate, budgeted cost that buys you unbiased labels.
  • Use the outcomes of human review as labels — while remembering reviewers only see what the model flagged.
  • Use later signals on suspicious payments you approved: customer complaints, refund requests, reports of account takeover.

Without at least one of these, the model's view of the world shrinks to the shape of its own past decisions.

6 · Deep dive: the adversary adapts

Monitor both traffic and feature freshness. A sudden rise in small payments could be an attack; a sudden fall in all velocity counters could be a broken stream job. Check ingestion lag, missing features and scores by merchant before deciding which response is appropriate. A feature outage should trigger the documented fallback, not an automatic retrain on corrupted inputs.

🧪
Attackers probe your thresholds

Card testing means running thousands of tiny payments to find out which stolen cards still work and which amounts get through. It's both an attack and a way of mapping your defences.

Signs of it: a burst of small payments across many cards from one device or IP range; a merchant with an unusually high decline rate; amounts suddenly clustering just under a known limit.

Defences: limits on how fast any one card, device or merchant can transact; thresholds with a little randomness in them, so the exact boundary can't be learned; rate limits per device and per merchant; and alerts based on the pattern of declines, not only approvals.

7 · The review loop

Review capacity is a separate bottleneck. Four thousand cases per day averages about 167 per hour across 24 hours. A burst of 1,000 cases/hour grows the backlog by roughly 833/hour at that service rate. Measure the age of the oldest case, reserve capacity for urgent cases, and define the action at each decision deadline. A queue without a timeout policy merely postpones an uncontrolled approval or decline.

Expected loss is a useful starting priority: 40% × £4,000 = £1,600, versus 90% × £30 = £27. Then consider whether a reviewer can change the outcome and how long the review takes. A case already stopped by a reliable rule may have little additional review value. Log overturned decisions and audit a sample of ordinary approvals to improve the next training set.

Interview drill

Follow-up: “Fraud losses dropped 40% after launch. Is that success?”

How a strong answer sounds
Not on its own. I'd want the false-decline number right next to it. Declining more payments always reduces fraud; the question is how many genuine customers it cost, and each of those costs more than the fraud you saved. So I'd look at: the approval rate before and after, broken down by segment and amount band; how often appeals overturn a decline; and ideally a holdout group whose payments were scored but not acted on, which gives an unbiased view of both sides. I'd also ask where the drop came from — if it's concentrated in one merchant or one country, it may be a quirk in the data rather than better detection. And I'd check the review queue: if it grew past capacity, the "gain" may just be cases quietly timing out into a default decision.

Follow-up: “A new attack pattern appears on Saturday night. What happens?”

How a strong answer sounds
The model won't catch it. It has never seen this pattern, and the labels that would teach it are 90 days away. That's exactly what the rules layer is for: an on-call analyst can push a speed limit on the offending device or account, or an outright block, within minutes — with a documented review and an expiry date, so it doesn't turn into permanent clutter. At the same time, the case goes into the review queue with a priority flag. The graph job finds connected cards, devices and accounts, so the block covers the whole network rather than one card. The labelled cases then feed the next retrain. The principle: the fast layer is rules that humans can edit, and the slow layer is the model. Trying to fix a Saturday-night attack by retraining is how people lose a weekend and a lot of money.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Always report fraud loss and false declines together; set the threshold from the expected cost in each amount band.
  • Counting features and network features carry most of the signal — compute the network ones in advance to fit the latency budget.
  • Labels arrive late, are biased by your own decisions, and shift as attackers adapt; budget for approving a random sample.
  • Rules let you react in minutes; the model spots patterns it wasn't explicitly told about. You need both.
  • The review queue is part of the system: order it by money at risk, and collect structured labels from it.

Check your understanding

Why is a single global decision threshold a poor choice here?

Working from expected cost gives a different best threshold for each amount band: strict on £4,000, relaxed on £8.

You only observe fraud outcomes for approved transactions. What problem does this create?

The model's blind spots keep themselves alive, because it never learns what would have happened to the payments it blocked. Approving a small random sample under a low amount cap buys unbiased labels.

A new attack starts tonight. Which layer responds fastest?

Retraining needs labels that are months away. Rules exist precisely to cover the gap between an attack appearing and the model learning about it.

Why precompute graph features rather than traversing the graph at request time?

Keep the network of links updated in a streaming job and store simple summary numbers per entity. Then scoring just reads ordinary features.

How should the review queue be prioritised?

Reviewer time is the scarce resource. A fairly suspicious large payment deserves more attention than a very suspicious tiny one.
