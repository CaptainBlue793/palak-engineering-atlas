Case Study: Self-Driving Perception

Your job is to turn eight cameras, a lidar (a laser scanner that measures distance) and five radars into one clear picture of the world, forty times a second. It all runs on a computer bolted into a car, with a time budget of a few tens of milliseconds. And when this system is wrong, the result isn't a bad recommendation. Everything you've learned about shipping fast meets a field where "wrong now and then" is not an acceptable place to operate.

  • Design a perception system that combines many sensors and many tasks, under a hard real-time deadline
  • Build the data engine that digs rare situations out of a whole fleet of cars
  • Think about computing on the vehicle itself, determinism and backup systems
  • Put together a safety argument that stands up to statistical scrutiny
  • Understand why the last 1% of situations takes 99% of the effort
📚
What you need before this chapter
  • Build labels and independent evaluation sets.
  • Understand detection, segmentation and visual features.
  • Trade model size and precision against quality.

Specify the operational design domain before interpreting any benchmark: roads, weather, speeds and sensor conditions covered by the design. A miss rate from clear daylight cannot establish performance in fog. The fleet, power and timing numbers below are illustrative design assumptions, not a validated vehicle specification. 🎯
Metrics

Safety-critical: how often the system fails to spot a pedestrian or cyclist, per million miles, broken down by lighting, weather and how much of them was hidden from view. Plus how often the car brakes for nothing, per thousand miles.

Perception quality: how accurately objects are detected and followed, by distance band and type of object; how wrong the measured speeds are; and how far off the predicted position is three seconds ahead.

System: total latency at p99.9 — not p95, because the rare slow cases are the whole point — plus dropped frames and how often the computer overheats and slows itself down.

Programme: how many miles between times a safety driver has to take over, and how quickly newly found failures get fixed.

⚖️
Why every metric in this chapter is a tail metric

A recommender that is 95% accurate is excellent. A perception system that misses 5% of pedestrians is lethal.

Everything you care about lives in the rare cases: the slowest 0.1% of frames, the rarest 0.1% of scenes, the one lighting condition your training data barely covers. That turns normal engineering economics upside down. Improving the average case is nearly worthless here. Almost all the effort goes into finding and fixing situations that happen once every few hundred thousand miles.

2 · The perception stack

Use a common time as well as a common coordinate system. At 30 metres/second, a measurement that is 0.01 seconds old corresponds to 0.3 metres of vehicle motion. Transform each sensor reading using the vehicle pose at its capture time, then account for motion when combining readings. A spatially correct transform with the wrong timestamp can still place an object in the wrong lane.

One frame, sensors to plannerStep-through
  1. The sensors run at different rates, and none of them capture at exactly the same instant. Triggering capture in hardware from one shared clock gives every measurement a timestamp you can trust. At 30 m/s (about 67 mph), a 10 ms timing error means a 30 cm position error — enough to change whether a pedestrian is in your lane.
  2. The system needs to know exactly where each sensor sits and which way it points (its "extrinsics"). Those shift with temperature, potholes, and a technician leaning on a mirror. So calibration is checked continuously while driving. A camera that slowly rotates makes sensor fusion worse in a way that looks exactly like the model getting worse — but isn't.
  3. Each camera runs the same "backbone" network, which produces features (rich numeric summaries of what's in the image) rather than finished boxes. Pulling out features first and combining them later is what lets the system reason across cameras. An object moving from one camera's view into another's must not turn into two objects.
  4. Using the calibrated geometry, project all those features into one shared top-down map of the area around the car, as if seen from above. Camera, lidar and radar are all combined there. This is the heart of a modern stack: one consistent space where every sensor and every task agrees, instead of separate per-camera detections glued together afterwards with ad-hoc rules.
  5. Several "heads" read the same features to answer different questions: what objects are there, where the lanes go, which space is drivable, what the traffic signals say, what space is occupied. Sharing one trunk is necessary to fit the compute budget, and the shared features also help accuracy. The downside is coupling: a change that helps one head can hurt another, so every head is a release gate.
  6. Detections in single frames become lasting objects with a speed and an identity. The stack then predicts several seconds of likely futures, each with a probability. What a cyclist intends to do matters far more to the planner than a perfectly tight box. Tracking is also where flicker — an object detected, lost, then detected again as new — turns into braking for no reason.
  7. The planner needs honest confidence levels and explicit reasoning about what's hidden. "There may be something behind that van" is a real output, not a blank space. A perception stack that reports only what it saw forces the planner to assume it can see everything, which is never true.
  8. The car produces terabytes per hour and can upload only a tiny slice. Triggers running on the vehicle pick out the interesting seconds: sensors disagreeing, low-confidence detections, the driver taking over, near-misses, scenes unlike anything seen before. Those clips are the fuel for everything in the next section.

3 · Deep dive: the data engine

Preserve some representative random sampling alongside rare-event triggers. Triggered clips efficiently find failures, but their event frequency does not represent ordinary driving. Keep related frames, trips and near-duplicate scenes in the same train/test partition so the test set cannot reward memorising the same encounter. Record which trigger selected each clip and which teacher supplied its labels.

An offline teacher may use future frames to establish a target, such as an object's true trajectory. The deployed student's inputs must still contain only information available at its decision time. Audit that boundary in data preparation. Auto-label confidence is not proof of correctness; send disagreements and difficult slices for independent review and keep a separate checked evaluation set.

Everyone has roughly the same models. What separates a demo from a product is the loop that finds the scenes your model gets wrong and turns them into training data. And how you spend a limited labelling budget decides how fast that loop goes round.

Auto-labelling: the offline model is allowed to cheat

The model in the car has two hard limits: it can only use what has happened so far, and it must finish in 45 ms. The labelling model, running later in a data centre, has neither. It can watch the rest of the clip, take a hundred times longer, use a much bigger network, and use lidar data the car's stack may not even have.

Run that model over a clip and you get labels better than a human could draw by hand — especially for speeds, for objects partly hidden behind things, and for distant objects. Humans then check and correct a sample, instead of drawing every single box.

This is knowledge distillation, with the extra advantage of seeing into the future of the clip (Chapter 27). It's the biggest single lever on labelling cost in the industry. The trap is a feedback loop: if the auto-labels come from a teacher trained on the same data, you just reinforce the blind spots they share. Protect against it with a human-checked holdout set that never goes through the auto-labelling loop.

4 · Deep dive: the on-vehicle budget

Frame rate and latency are different limits. At 36 Hz a new frame arrives every 1,000 ÷ 36 = 27.8 ms. A 45 ms perception path can sustain that input only if pipeline stages overlap sufficiently; a single serial 45 ms worker manages about 22 frames/second. Verify both the completion deadline for each frame and the rate of completed frames. Bound queues so old observations do not accumulate.

The listed perception stages total 8 + 18 + 12 + 7 = 45 ms. Adding 25 ms for planning and control gives 70 ms, leaving 30 ms within the stated 100 ms total. Sensor age, transport, scheduling and actuation must fit that remainder. A low p99.9 is useful evidence, but a hard deadline also needs a defined response when the deadline is missed.

StageBudgetConstraint
Capture and image processing~8 msFixed by the hardware; sets the floor for everything after it
Backbones (8 cameras)~18 msThe biggest cost. Uses int8 numbers, merged GPU operations, and fixed tensor sizes
Bird's-eye fusion + heads~12 msSharing one trunk keeps this affordable
Tracking + prediction~7 msMostly CPU work; must give identical results every time, so clips can be replayed
Planner + control~25 msOutside this chapter, but it shares the 100 ms from sensing to acting
Thermal headroom~15%A stack that only fits in winter doesn't fit

Three constraints here have no equivalent in cloud serving. Determinism: the same inputs must always give the same outputs. Otherwise you can't replay an incident to reproduce it, and you can't prove you fixed it. No batching across requests: there's one car, so you can't trade latency for throughput the way a server does. Degrade, don't fail: if a camera dies, the stack must keep running with reduced ability, and tell the planner that its field of view has shrunk.

5 · Deep dive: the safety argument

Zero observed failures does not imply a zero failure rate. Under a simple constant-rate Poisson model, the approximate one-sided 95% upper bound after M failure-free miles is 3 ÷ M. After 10 million miles, that is about three failures per 10 million miles. Correlated routes, changing software and different conditions weaken the assumptions; report those limits instead of extrapolating a clean average everywhere.

Replay keeps recorded sensor inputs fixed. Once a new planner would choose a different path, those recordings no longer describe the resulting world. Closed-loop simulation explores that interaction but introduces simulator assumptions. Shadow execution avoids giving the candidate control authority, yet still needs resource isolation so it cannot delay the active stack. These forms of evidence answer different questions. Waymo's safety methodology describes an approach spanning hardware, driving behaviour and operations within a defined domain.

The statisticsIn human driving, a serious injury happens roughly once every few tens of millions of miles. To show you're better than that by observation alone, you'd need billions of miles — decades of fleet driving. No programme can prove safety from road miles alone, so the argument has to be assembled from several weaker pieces.
SimulationMillions of scenario variations per release, changing the things that matter: the pedestrian stepping out one moment later, the van parked half a metre further out. The known weakness is that simulation mostly tests situations someone thought to write down.
ResimulationReplay real recorded clips through the new stack and compare the outputs with before. This catches anything that got worse on the exact scenes that caused trouble in the past. It only works if the stack is deterministic.
Shadow modeRun the new stack on fleet cars without letting it control anything, and compare what it would have done with what the human did. Huge amounts of evidence with zero risk — the closest thing to free data in this field.
Operational design domainState exactly where you claim the system works: which roads, speeds, weather and lighting. Most of the safety case is a limited claim, plus reliably noticing when the car is leaving those limits.
Regression disciplineEvery failure you find becomes a permanent test case. The real measure of a programme's speed is how quickly newly found failures get closed out — not how good the headline benchmark looks (Chapter 32).

6 · Fleet, training and cost

At the illustrative 1.5 TB per vehicle-hour, 25,000 vehicles each recording one hour produce 37,500 TB, or 37.5 PB. If the retained 0.02% of frames represented the same fraction of bytes, uploads would be about 7.5 TB. In practice clip lengths and sensor selection change that ratio. Budget actual retained bytes and upload windows, not only the percentage of frames.

A training run of 2,000 GPUs for four days uses 192,000 GPU-hours. That excludes auto-labelling, failed runs and validation. Keep each dataset, calibration package, model and runtime version together so a reported improvement can be reproduced on the target hardware.

7 · What breaks

A failed sensor must produce an explicit health state, not a convincing empty scene. Pass freshness, uncertainty and reduced coverage to the planner, which owns the validated response for that operating condition. Test shared failures too: two models using the same miscalibrated camera are not independent protection against that camera's error.

FailureSymptomResponse
One car's calibration driftsSensor fusion errors on that car only; looks like a model bugCheck calibration continuously while driving; track health per vehicle
Computer overheats in summerDropped frames and latency spikes in hot climatesBudget with spare headroom; test at temperature extremes, not average conditions
A rare object type is barely in the dataThe system confidently misreads a rare but critical objectMine for it with fleet triggers, collect more on purpose, and gate releases per object class

Object identities flicker between framesThe car brakes for nothing when an object is lost and re-foundSmooth tracks over time and measure identity stability, not just per-frame accuracy
🎤
Interview drill

Follow-up: “Your detection benchmark improved 3% but the fleet sees more phantom braking. How is that possible?”

How a strong answer sounds
Because braking for nothing isn't caused by per-frame accuracy. It's caused by instability over time. A model can be slightly better at spotting an object on average while flickering more: detected, lost for two frames, then detected again as a brand new track. Further down the pipeline, that looks like an object suddenly appearing right in front of the car, so the planner brakes. The benchmark has no concept of keeping the same identity across frames, so it can't see this at all. I'd look at track-level numbers — identity switches, tracks breaking into pieces, how steady the measured speeds are — and at the whole distribution rather than the average. The overall gain probably comes from easy objects at medium range, while the regression is at the edges of the view or on partly hidden objects. To fix it, I'd replay the clips that caused the braking events, then add stability-over-time terms to both the training objective and the release gates. The general lesson: the metric that moves the product is rarely the one the model was trained on. Here that gap is dangerous, not just annoying.

Follow-up: “How would you prove the new release is safer than the old one?”

How a strong answer sounds
Not by driving miles. The events are far too rare; showing a real difference by observation would take billions of miles. So I'd build the argument in layers. First, replay the full library of past problem clips, to show every previously known failure is still fixed and to see every behaviour change on real recorded scenes. Second, simulate millions of varied scenarios, and report coverage by scenario family so the gaps are stated openly rather than hidden. Third, run shadow mode across the fleet: the new stack runs without controlling anything, and I compare its decisions with the old stack's and with the human driver's. That gives real statistical power at zero risk. Fourth, set gates per object class, distance band and weather condition, because an overall improvement can hide things getting worse for pedestrians at night. Finally, roll out in stages, with a clearly stated operating domain and a defined trigger for pulling it back. No single one of these proves safety. Together they make an argument that a regulator, or an engineer, can actually pick apart and examine.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Combine sensor features in one shared top-down space, instead of gluing together separate per-camera detections afterwards.
  • The data engine — triggers, mining, auto-labelling, targeted collection — is the real product. The model architecture is widely available.
  • An offline labelling model can see the rest of the clip and use unlimited compute, so its labels beat human ones and cost less.
  • Running on the vehicle adds determinism, heat limits and graceful degradation on top of the usual latency constraints.
  • You can't prove safety by driving miles. You argue it from replayed clips, simulation, shadow mode and a clearly bounded operating domain.

Check your understanding

Why fuse sensors in a shared bird's-eye-view space rather than merging per-camera detections?

Merging per-camera boxes at the end needs hand-written rules to settle disagreements, and those rules are exactly where the rare, dangerous failures hide.

Why can an offline auto-labelling model produce better labels than the on-vehicle model?

The model in the car can only use the past, and must finish in 45 ms. The labelling model has neither limit. That's why its labels for speed and for partly hidden objects beat what a human would draw by hand.

Why is determinism a hard requirement on the vehicle?

Replaying recorded clips is the backbone of the safety argument, and it only works if identical inputs always give identical outputs.

Why can safety not be validated by driving enough miles?

That's why the argument comes in layers: replayed clips, simulation, shadow mode, per-component gates, and a clearly bounded operating domain.

Detection accuracy improved but phantom braking increased. Most likely cause?

Per-frame metrics can't see whether an object keeps the same identity over time. Broken tracks and identity switches are what the planner actually reacts to.
