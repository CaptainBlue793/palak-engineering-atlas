Probability & Statistics

A classifier doesn't answer “spam”. It says “spam, with probability 0.93”. Deciding what to do with that number, knowing whether a model is really better than another, and reading an A/B test all need probability and statistics. This chapter teaches the part you'll use: probabilities and how they combine, mean and spread, the common distributions, why bigger samples give steadier answers, conditional probability and Bayes' rule, and how to turn a probability into a decision.

  • Combine probabilities with AND, OR and NOT, and recognise independence
  • Compute mean, variance, standard deviation and the median, and know when each misleads
  • Recognise uniform, Bernoulli, binomial and normal distributions, and use the 68–95–99.7 rule
  • Explain why estimates get steadier with more data, and roughly by how much
  • Work Bayes problems by counting, and explain the base-rate trap
  • Use expected value to turn a model's probability into a decision

Why probability runs through ML

Machine learning is learning from examples, and examples are noisy: the same features can go with different outcomes, data has errors, and the future never looks exactly like the past. Probability is the language for being precisely uncertain. You'll meet it in four places throughout the course:

🎯

Model outputs

Classifiers output probabilities; language models output a probability for every possible next token (P6).

📉

Losses

The most common losses measure how much probability the model gave to the right answer (P6, P7).

🧪

Evaluation

“Is 91.2% really better than 90.8%?” is a statistics question about samples and noise (Ch 7).

⚖️

Decisions

Blocking a payment, showing an ad or flagging a post means trading off the costs of different mistakes (Ch 44).

Probability basics

A probability is a number from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain) that says how likely an event is. P(heads) = 0.5 for a fair coin. You can read it as a long-run frequency: flip the coin many times and about half the flips are heads. Probabilities of all the possible outcomes add up to 1.

RuleFormulaWorked example (one fair die)
NOT (complement)P(not A) = 1 − P(A)P(not a 6) = 1 − 1/6 = 5/6
OR (can't both happen)P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B)P(1 or 2) = 1/6 + 1/6 = 1/3
OR (might overlap)P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B)P(even or > 3) = 3/6 + 3/6 − 2/6 = 4/6 (4 and 6 counted once)
AND (independent)P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B)Two dice both 6: 1/6 × 1/6 = 1/36

Two events are independent when knowing one tells you nothing about the other, like two separate coin flips. Independence is what lets you multiply. A useful consequence: the chance that at least one of many independent things goes wrong is 1 minus the chance that none do. If each of 20 independent checks has a 5% false-alarm rate, the chance of at least one false alarm is 1 − 0.95²⁰ ≈ 64%. That's why testing many ideas at once finds “significant” results by luck (Chapter 33).

🔗
Real data is rarely independent

Many ML methods assume examples are independent. But clicks from the same user, frames from the same video and sentences from the same document are related. Treat them as independent and you'll overestimate how much evidence you have — and leak information between training and test sets (Chapter 3).

Mean, variance and standard deviation

Before any modelling, you summarise data with a centre and a spread. Take eight numbers: 2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 9.

  • The mean (average) is the sum divided by the count: 40 / 8 = 5.
  • The variance measures spread: the average squared distance from the mean. The distances are −3, −1, −1, −1, 0, 0, 2, 4; squared they are 9, 1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 4, 16; their average is 32 / 8 = 4.
  • The standard deviation (std, σ “sigma”) is the square root of the variance: √4 = 2. It's in the same units as the data, so it's easier to read: “values are typically about 2 away from 5”.
import numpy as np
data = np.array([2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 9])
print(data.mean(), data.var(), data.std())   # 5.0 4.0 2.0
print(np.median(data), np.percentile(data, 90))   # 4.5 7.6

Small print: when you estimate variance from a sample, statisticians often divide by n − 1 instead of n (here 32 / 7 ≈ 4.57). NumPy divides by n unless you pass ddof=1; pandas' .var() uses n − 1 by default. With big datasets the difference is tiny.

When the mean misleads: medians and outliers

The mean is pulled hard by extreme values. Nine people earn £30,000 and one earns £3,000,000: the mean income is £327,000, which describes nobody. The median — the middle value when sorted — is £30,000, which describes almost everybody. Percentiles generalise this: the 90th percentile is the value 90% of the data lies below.

ML data is full of such long-tailed quantities: purchase amounts, session lengths, the number of followers, request latencies. For them, report medians and percentiles, not just means, and consider transforming with a log before modelling (P6, Chapter 4).

Distributions

A distribution describes how likely each possible value is. You'll meet four constantly:

🎲

Uniform

Every outcome equally likely: a fair die (1/6 each), or rng.random(), which picks any number between 0 and 1 evenly. Used for random initialisation and shuffling.

🪙

Bernoulli

One yes/no trial with probability p of “yes”: did the user click? Every binary classifier is really predicting the p of a Bernoulli outcome.

📊

Binomial

The number of yeses in n independent Bernoulli trials. 1,000 ad views with a 2% click rate: on average 20 clicks, but 14 or 27 wouldn't be surprising.

🔔

Normal (Gaussian)

The bell curve, described by its mean μ and standard deviation σ. Heights, measurement errors, and — importantly — averages of many random things all look normal.

That last point has a name, the central limit theorem: the average of many independent random values tends towards a normal distribution, whatever the individual values look like. It's why the normal curve shows up everywhere, including in the error bars on model accuracy.

A handy rule for normal data: about 68% of values fall within 1σ of the mean, 95% within 2σ, and 99.7% within 3σ. So if adult heights have mean 170 cm and σ = 7 cm, about 68% of people are between 163 and 177 cm, and someone at 191 cm is 3σ above average — rarer than 1 in 700.

The normal curveInteractive

A related everyday tool is the z-score: how many standard deviations a value is from the mean, z = (x − μ) / σ. The 191 cm person has z = (191 − 170) / 7 = 3. Standardising features (P2) is exactly converting every value to its z-score, and a very large |z| is a quick way to flag possible outliers or data errors.

Samples, noise and sample size

You almost never see the whole “population” — every user, every possible email. You see a sample, and statistics computed from it wobble from sample to sample. The key fact: the wobble shrinks as the sample grows, but only with the square root of its size.

Concretely, if you measure a rate p (like accuracy or click rate) on n examples, its typical error — the standard error — is about √(p(1 − p) / n). Worked example: a model gets 90% accuracy on a test set of 1,000 examples. The standard error is √(0.9 × 0.1 / 1000) ≈ 0.0095, so the true accuracy is plausibly anywhere from about 88% to 92% (±2 standard errors). To halve that uncertainty you need four times as many test examples. Flip coins and watch the estimate settle:

Early on, the running estimate swings wildly; after thousands of samples it hugs the true rate. The shaded band is ±2 standard errors around the truth — notice how slowly it narrows. This is why a “1% improvement” measured on 200 test examples means very little (Ch 7, Ch 33).

Conditional probability and Bayes' rule

P(A | B) means “the probability of A given that B is true” — a conditional probability. It can be wildly different from P(B | A). P(wet ground | it rained) is high; P(it rained | wet ground) is lower, because sprinklers exist. Confusing the two is one of the most common reasoning errors in all of data work.

Bayes' rule tells you how to flip one into the other:

P(A | B) = P(B | A) × P(A) / P(B)

The easiest way to use it is to count instead of juggling formulas. Say 20% of email is spam. The word “prize” appears in 30% of spam and in 1% of normal mail. You see “prize”. How likely is spam? Picture 1,000 emails: 200 are spam, and 60 of those contain “prize”. 800 are normal, and 8 of those contain “prize”. So 68 emails contain “prize”, and 60 of them are spam: 60 / 68 ≈ 88%. Drop the spam share to 0.5% and watch the answer collapse. When the thing you're looking for is rare, even a small false-alarm rate swamps the true hits. That's the base-rate effect behind fraud and moderation systems (Ch 7, Ch 44).

In Bayes' vocabulary, P(spam) = 20% is the prior (what you believed before looking), P(“prize” | spam) is the likelihood (how well the evidence fits), and P(spam | “prize”) is the posterior (what you believe after). A whole family of models — naive Bayes spam filters — is just this calculation repeated over every word.

Expectation: turning probabilities into decisions

The expected value of something random is the average you'd get over many tries: each outcome times its probability, added up. A bet that pays £10 with probability 0.3 and £0 otherwise has expected value 0.3 × 10 + 0.7 × 0 = £3.

This is where a model's output meets the real world. Suppose a model says a payment is fraud with probability 0.1, fraud would cost £200, and wrongly blocking a good customer costs about £5 in goodwill. If you allow it, you expect to lose 0.1 × £200 = £20. If you block it, you expect to lose 0.9 × £5 = £4.50. Block. Notice that a probability of just 10% led to blocking, because the two kinds of mistake cost very different amounts. That is why “use a threshold of 0.5” is rarely the right rule; the right threshold comes from the costs.

Expectation is also what a loss really is during training: the expected (average) error over the data. When you minimise the average loss over a training set, you're estimating the expected loss on data like it (P7).

Correlation is not causation

Correlation measures how strongly two quantities move together, on a scale from −1 (perfectly opposite) through 0 (no straight-line relationship) to +1 (perfectly together). Ice-cream sales and sunburns are strongly correlated. Neither causes the other; hot weather drives both. That hidden third factor is called a confounder.

ML models learn correlations — that's all they can see in the data. Usually that's fine for prediction. It goes wrong when you act on a correlation as if it were a cause (“users who see this feature spend more, so show it to everyone”), or when a model latches onto a spurious signal that disappears later: a pneumonia model that learned “hospital X's scanner” rather than “sick lungs”. Randomised experiments — A/B tests — are the standard tool for finding causes (Chapter 33).

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Reading P(A | B) as P(B | A). “Most spam contains links” does not mean “most emails with links are spam”. Count it out with a base rate.
  • Ignoring base rates. A 99%-accurate test for something that affects 1 in 10,000 people is wrong most of the time it says “positive”.
  • Trusting small samples. 90% accuracy on 50 examples could easily be 80% or 97% in reality. Report uncertainty.
  • Summarising long-tailed data with the mean alone. Use the median and percentiles too.
  • Multiplying probabilities of dependent events. Two alerts triggered by the same outage aren't independent evidence.
  • Treating correlation as causation. Look for confounders, and use experiments to test causes.
🎤
Interview drill

Reason it out: A test for a disease that affects 1 in 1,000 people catches 99% of cases and has a 1% false-positive rate. Someone tests positive. How worried should they be?

How a strong answer sounds
Count 100,000 people: 100 have the disease and 99 test positive. Of the 99,900 healthy people, 1% — about 999 — also test positive. So of roughly 1,098 positives, only 99 are real: about 9%. The test is good, but the disease is rare, so false positives dominate. The right next step is a second, independent test, not panic — and the same logic applies to any rare-event classifier like fraud or abuse detection.

Evaluate: Model A scores 91.0% and model B 91.6% accuracy on a 2,000-example test set. Is B better?

How a strong answer sounds
Not clearly. The standard error at about 91% on 2,000 examples is √(0.91 × 0.09 / 2000) ≈ 0.64 percentage points, so a 0.6-point gap is about one standard error — well within noise. Because both models are scored on the same examples, I'd use a paired comparison on the examples where they disagree, or a bootstrap confidence interval, and ideally a larger test set, before claiming B is better.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Probabilities run from 0 to 1. NOT: 1 − P. OR: add (minus any overlap). AND for independent events: multiply.
  • Mean = centre, variance = average squared distance from it, standard deviation = its square root. Use medians and percentiles for long-tailed data.
  • Key distributions: uniform, Bernoulli, binomial, normal. For normal data, 68–95–99.7% lie within 1, 2 and 3σ; z = (x − μ)/σ.
  • Sample estimates wobble; the standard error of a rate is √(p(1−p)/n). Four times the data halves the uncertainty.
  • P(A|B) ≠ P(B|A). Work Bayes by counting; rare base rates produce many false alarms.
  • Expected value = Σ outcome × probability. Compare expected costs to turn probabilities into decisions.
  • Correlation measures moving together, not cause and effect; confounders lurk.

Check your understanding

1% of transactions are fraud. A detector flags 90% of fraud and 5% of legitimate transactions. Roughly what share of flagged transactions are really fraud?

Count 10,000 transactions: 100 are fraud and 90 get flagged; 9,900 are legitimate and 495 get flagged. So 90 of 585 flags are fraud, about 15%. The rare base rate dominates.

What is the standard deviation of the numbers 1, 3, 5, 7 (dividing by n)?

The mean is 4. Squared distances: 9, 1, 1, 9, averaging 20 / 4 = 5 — that's the variance. The standard deviation is √5 ≈ 2.24.

Exam scores are roughly normal with mean 60 and standard deviation 10. About what share of students score between 40 and 80?

40 and 80 are 2 standard deviations below and above the mean, and about 95% of a normal distribution lies within ±2σ.

You want to halve the uncertainty (standard error) of your test-set accuracy. How much bigger must the test set be?

The standard error shrinks with √n. Halving it needs √n to double, which means n must be four times larger.

A model says a loan has a 20% chance of default. Approving a loan that defaults costs £1,000; rejecting a good borrower loses £150 of profit. What does expected cost suggest?

Approve: 0.2 × £1,000 = £200 expected loss. Reject: 0.8 × £150 = £120 expected loss. Rejecting is cheaper, even though default is less likely than not — the costs are lopsided.
