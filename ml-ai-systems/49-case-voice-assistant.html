Case Study: Voice Assistant

You're building a spoken assistant that has to feel like a real conversation. People start replying about 200 ms after the other person stops talking, and they interrupt each other constantly. That one fact — not model quality — decides the whole architecture. Everything must stream. Everything must be cancellable. And the hardest part of the system is the piece that works out when you've finished your sentence.

  • Set a time budget for a whole turn, based on how fast humans take turns
  • Stream speech recognition, generation and speech synthesis so the stages overlap
  • Solve endpointing — deciding when the user has stopped, which makes or breaks the feel
  • Support barge-in (the user talking over the assistant), with echo cancellation and full cancellation
  • Evaluate a system where word error rate hides the failures that actually matter

🎯
Metrics

Primary: task completion per conversation — did the user get what they asked for without having to repeat themselves?

Conversational quality: response latency at p50 and p95; false-endpoint rate (cutting the user off); late-endpoint rate (an awkward pause); barge-in success; and the repeat rate, which is the best single sign of "it misheard me".

Component quality: word error rate overall and on entities (names, numbers, addresses); how natural the synthesised voice sounds; and how long after the wake word the first word is spoken.

Guardrails: cost per minute of conversation, and how often audio is dropped.

🗣️
The number that sets the architecture

In human conversation, the gap between one person finishing and the next starting averages about 200 ms. A gap longer than roughly a second reads as hesitation, or as the system being broken.

That tiny budget has to cover four things: deciding the user has stopped, finishing the transcription, getting the model's first words, and turning them into audio. A simple pipeline that does these one after another takes several seconds. Every technique in this chapter exists to overlap stages that a basic design would run in sequence.

2 · One turn, end to end

Partial transcripts are revisions, not append-only text. “Call Ann” might become “Call Andrew” after more audio arrives. Attach a revision number to speculative work and discard it when its transcript changes. You can prefill or prepare a lookup early, but do not commit an external action from an unstable transcript. Confirm the final intent and permissions before execution.

From microphone to spoken replyStep-through
  1. Acoustic echo cancellation comes first. Without it, the assistant hears its own voice through the microphone and interrupts itself. Then voice activity detection decides when someone is actually speaking, so silence isn't sent over the network, and a low-bitrate codec shrinks the audio. Audio leaves in 20 ms frames over a connection that stays open. A normal request-and-response API can't express a conversation.
  2. A streaming ASR model produces a partial guess at the transcript every ~120 ms, and corrects it as more audio arrives. These partial transcripts make everything else possible: the dialogue model can start reading the sentence before the user has finished saying it.
  3. This is the hardest part of the system. A pause isn't the same as an ending. People pause mid-sentence to think, and sometimes stop abruptly when they're done. So combine two signals: how long the silence has lasted, and whether the transcript so far reads as a complete request. Then adjust the threshold for the situation and the speaker.
  4. This is the speculative start. As soon as the end looks likely, begin reading the partial transcript into the model (prefill). If the user keeps talking, cancel and start again. Wasted compute is much cheaper than a slow reply. Once the ending is confirmed, only the last few tokens still need prefilling, so the first output arrives in tens of milliseconds instead of hundreds.
  5. Don't wait for the whole answer. Cut the stream of words at clause boundaries and synthesise each piece as soon as it's complete. The first word is spoken while the model is still deciding how the sentence ends. This is the biggest single latency win in the pipeline, and it's purely about overlapping work.
  6. Audio must never cut out mid-word, so a small buffer smooths out uneven network delivery. Make it too big and you've just moved the delay to the start. Make it too small and the reply stutters, which users hate far more than a slightly later start.
  7. When the user talks over the reply, stop playback within ~150 ms and cancel both generation and synthesis. Crucially, record how much of the reply the user actually heard. The conversation history must match what the user experienced, not what the model produced.
  8. Keep the transcript, the entities you resolved, tool results, device context and what was actually spoken. Voice sessions are long and full of references back — "that one", "the second thing" — so session state does more work here than in a text product.

3 · Deep dive: spending the 700 ms

Read the timeline relative to the red line at 800 ms, when speech ends. The batch example finishes at 2,950 ms, so its response gap is 2,150 ms. Streaming finishes at 1,180 ms, leaving 380 ms; speculative start finishes at 960 ms, leaving 160 ms. Those are illustrative component timings. Network transit, queueing and playback buffering must also fit the measured product target.

Keep a bounded audio queue. If TTS creates speech faster than the client can play it, stop generating far ahead of the listener. A ten-second playback backlog makes an interruption expensive and can leave stale audio ready to play after cancellation. Label every chunk with its turn ID and sequence number so the client can discard late chunks from cancelled turns.

4 · Deep dive: endpointing

Cancellation cannot undo a completed tool action. If a booking finished just before the interruption, record that outcome even if its spoken confirmation was never heard. Keep action state separate from the delivered transcript, and explain the completed action on the next turn. Otherwise shortening the transcript would incorrectly make the system behave as if the booking never happened.

Every other component has a benchmark to score it. This one is judged purely by how it feels. It can fail in two opposite directions, and the fixes pull against each other.

Too eagerThe assistant cuts in mid-sentence. Users notice immediately, start talking faster and more defensively, and things get worse. This happens most with fixed silence thresholds under 500 ms, and with people who pause to think. How much someone pauses varies with age, accent and how hard they're concentrating, so one global threshold is quietly unfair.
Too slowAn awkward gap after every request. Less alarming than being cut off, but it's the difference between a conversation and a ticket machine — and it adds up over a long session.
Acoustic signalHow long the silence has lasted, how loud the speech was, and the melody of the voice. A falling pitch at the end of a phrase strongly suggests the person has finished; a rising one usually doesn't. Cheap and fast, but it varies by language.
Semantic signalDoes the transcript so far read as a complete request? "Set a timer for" is clearly unfinished. "Set a timer for ten minutes" is clearly done. A small classifier reads the running partial transcript — which is why partial transcripts matter for more than just showing text on screen.
ContextRight after the assistant asks a question, wait longer: the user is thinking of an answer. In dictation mode, wait much longer. In the middle of a list ("add milk, eggs…"), the way the voice hangs on the comma says more is coming.
The escape hatchThe speculative start makes it cheap to guess the end too early: begin generating, and cancel if the user keeps going. That turns an accuracy problem into a compute problem, and compute is far easier to buy.
🔊
Barge-in is a distributed cancellation problem

When the user speaks over the assistant, four things must stop almost at once: playback, speech synthesis, token generation, and any tool call already running. Playback is on the device, so that part is easy. The rest are on a server that may be several network hops away. So the cancel signal has to travel the same fast path as the audio, and the generation scheduler has to act on it. A stream that keeps generating after everyone has moved on costs money — and worse, can play audio the user no longer expects.

Then there's the subtle part. The conversation history must record what the user heard, not what was generated. If the assistant produced three sentences and the user cut in after four words, the model's next turn must be based on those four words only.

Get this wrong and the assistant confidently refers to information it never actually said out loud. It's one of the strangest-feeling bugs in a voice product, and no test of a single component will catch it.

5 · Evaluating a voice system

Build complete conversation tests with pauses, corrections and interruptions. For example, “Set a timer for ten … no, fifteen minutes” should create one fifteen-minute timer. Check both the tool record and the audio actually played. Include lost packets and a delayed cancellation signal so tests cover interactions that clean prerecorded utterances cannot expose.

Test in real-world sound conditions: a kitchen with the radio on, a car at speed, a phone on speaker across a room. Voice systems look finished on clean recordings, and turn out not to be on recordings from the field.

6 · Capacity and cost

The audio estimate is 90 million turns × 10 seconds ÷ 3,600 = 250,000 hours/day. Dividing by 24 gives about 10,417 simultaneous audio streams on average. If all 60,000 peak calls send 20 ms frames, the ingress layer receives 60,000 × 50 = 3 million frames/second. Batch processing can help, but waiting too long to assemble batches spends the latency budget.

Measure throughput in audio seconds processed per wall-clock second at the target delay. A fleet-size estimate without that benchmark cannot establish capacity. Wasted-prefill percentage is also different from wasted total compute: cancelled starts may be very short. Charge cancellation work, ASR, dialogue and TTS separately before comparing cost per conversation minute.

7 · What breaks

On reconnection, agree which audio sequence was last received and which response was last played. Avoid replaying an old spoken instruction as if it were new. If recognition becomes uncertain, ask for the affected name or number again rather than repeating a whole turn. This makes recovery observable to the user and keeps retries from becoming duplicate actions.

🎤
Interview drill

Follow-up: “Users say it interrupts them. Your endpointer's false-cut rate is 2% and hasn't changed. Explain.”

How a strong answer sounds
Two percent is an average, and it almost certainly hides big differences between groups. I'd break it down four ways. By speaker: some people pause mid-sentence far more, which goes with age, accent and speaking a second language, so one global silence threshold cuts off some groups several times more often. By situation: right after the assistant asks a question, the user is still thinking, so the same threshold is too aggressive. By sound conditions, because background noise changes what the voice activity detector sees. And by type of utterance, since lists and dictation need much longer waits. I'd also check whether "it interrupts me" is really about endpointing at all. Failing echo cancellation makes the assistant interrupt itself, and a badly tuned barge-in threshold lets background speech stop playback. Users describe both the same way. The fix is thresholds that vary by group and situation, with a part that adapts per user, plus the speculative start so guessing early is cheap. And I'd replace the single global rate with a dashboard broken down by group, because the average is exactly what hid this.

Follow-up: “Would a single speech-to-speech model remove all of this complexity?”

How a strong answer sounds
It removes some of the complexity and moves the rest somewhere else. Going straight from audio to audio cuts out two points where one stage had to wait for another. It can also keep tone, emotion and overlapping speech that a transcript throws away, and the latency really is better. What it doesn't remove: endpointing and barge-in, because those belong to conversation itself, not to the pipeline. You still need cancellation. And you lose the transcript, which is where you debug, log and evaluate. That's a real operational cost: with separate stages I can tell whether a failure was mishearing or misunderstanding, and with one end-to-end model I often can't. It's also harder to attach tools, to check the text against policy before it's spoken, and to support twelve languages with uneven amounts of training data. In practice I'd use it where its strengths pay off — casual conversational turns and quick chit-chat — and keep the multi-stage pipeline for anything involving tool calls, entities or compliance. That's the same tiering answer as in the other case studies.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • The speed of human turn-taking sets the budget. Every stage must stream, so stages overlap instead of queueing up.
  • Speaking the first clause while the model is still writing the rest is the biggest single latency win available.
  • Endpointing is the hardest component. It needs both sound-based and meaning-based signals, and must be measured per group of speakers — averages hide unfairness.
  • The speculative start turns an accuracy problem into a compute problem, and compute is the easier one to buy.
  • Barge-in means cancelling work across several machines at once, and the history must record only what the user actually heard.

Check your understanding

Why synthesise speech clause by clause instead of waiting for the full response?

This is pure overlapping of work. The answer itself doesn't change; the user just hears it seconds earlier. That's the difference between a conversation and a ticket machine.

Why is a fixed silence threshold a poor endpointer?

Combine the length of the silence with a judgement about whether the request sounds complete, then adapt to the situation and the speaker. One global threshold is quietly unfair to people who speak more slowly.

What does speculative start buy you?

About 18% of prefills are thrown away, and that's a good deal. Compute is far easier to buy than a more accurate endpointer.

After a barge-in, what must the conversation history contain?

Otherwise the assistant confidently refers to things it never said out loud. That feels eerie to users, and no test of a single component will catch it.

Why track entity error rate separately from word error rate?

Whether a task succeeds depends on names, numbers and addresses. Entity error rate tracks that. Overall word error rate can improve while task completion actually falls.
