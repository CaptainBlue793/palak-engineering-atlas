Case Study: Training a Frontier LLM

Ten thousand GPUs, ninety days, and a budget of over a hundred million pounds, all spent on one computation. At the end, you either have a frontier model (one at the cutting edge) or you don't. There's no A/B test and no gradual rollout. You make the big design decisions once, watch a loss curve for three months, and find out at the end. This chapter is the capstone: every earlier chapter shows up here, and the hardest problems turn out to be about operations, not maths.

  • Split a fixed compute budget between model size, training data and the later cost of running the model
  • Build the data pipeline that sets the model's upper limit before training even starts
  • Choose how to split the work across GPUs so it fits in memory and keeps the network busy
  • Keep a 90-day run going through hardware failures, slow machines and sudden loss jumps
  • Decide, from evidence, whether the result is worth launching

What you need before this chapter

1 · The commitment

Check that compute, time and hardware assumptions agree. An 88-day run lasts 7,603,200 seconds. Delivering 3 × 10²⁵ model FLOPs on 10,240 GPUs in that time requires about 3.85 × 10¹⁴ useful FLOPs/second per GPU. At 45% MFU, the corresponding peak is about 8.56 × 10¹⁴ FLOPs/second. These are implied requirements, not a guarantee that a chosen cluster reaches them.

🎲
What makes this different from every other chapter

Everywhere else in this course, you ship, measure and improve. Here, one round of feedback takes ninety days, and you get one attempt per budget cycle.

The decisions that matter — the model's shape, the data mixture, the tokenizer, the optimizer, the learning-rate schedule — are all made before a single full-scale training step runs. By week two, they're effectively locked in. So the whole discipline is about reducing risk in advance: scaling laws fitted from small runs, ablations (controlled experiments that change one thing at a time) at 1% scale, and monitoring good enough that you spot a problem on day three instead of day forty.

2 · Deep dive: allocating the budget

Apply the same formula to each proposed configuration before comparing it. A 400B dense model on 1.2T tokens uses about 2.88 × 10²⁴ FLOPs; 140B on 3.6T uses 3.024 × 10²⁴. A 70B model on 14T uses 5.88 × 10²⁴. These examples fit below the 3 × 10²⁵ ceiling but do not spend the same compute. Treat the interactive losses, times and prices as schematic scenarios, not a controlled equal-budget experiment.

For a genuinely fixed budget C, choose N and solve D = C ÷ (6N). With C = 3 × 10²⁵ and N = 140 billion, D is about 35.7 trillion tokens. Check that enough suitable data exists and benchmark the resulting sequence lengths and batch sizes. For MoE, active parameters better approximate token compute, while all experts still affect storage and communication.

Training compute is roughly 6 × parameters × tokens. With the total fixed, every choice of model size decides how many tokens you can train on. A bigger model means less data, and a smaller model means more. The trade-off also affects costs long after training ends.

💡
Compute-optimal is not deployment-optimal

Scaling laws tell you which model size gives the lowest training loss for a given training budget. That's the right answer only if you train once and never serve the model.

If the model will produce a trillion tokens a month for two years, running it (inference) costs far more than training it. So you can deliberately "over-train" a smaller model: feed it more data than the compute-optimal point suggests. Its training loss ends up a bit worse than a bigger model's would be, but it's cheaper and faster to run, so the total cost comes out better. Treat the compute-optimal point as a reference, not a target (Chapter 16).

3 · Data: the decision made before training starts

Freeze a manifest listing shard hashes, source versions, filters, tokenizer and mixture weights. Reconstructing a run requires the same processed examples, not merely the same source URLs. Track how often each source is sampled: five passes over one corpus are not five times as many unique examples. Keep evaluation data outside both training and repeated mixture selection.

Small runs reduce uncertainty; they do not remove it. An ablation using 1% of full-run compute costs 1% per trial, so ten such trials can consume 10% before overhead. Test the most uncertain choices first and fit error bars around extrapolations. Longer contexts and communication-heavy architectures also need full-scale performance trials because small-cluster timings may not transfer.

From raw crawl to training shardsStep-through
  1. Start from web crawls, books, code, research papers, hand-picked reference material and licensed collections. That might be 100 trillion tokens raw, of which you'll keep about a seventh. Having a big surplus is what lets you filter hard — you buy quality by throwing data away.
  2. Detect the language. Score quality with a trained classifier. Use rules to remove boilerplate and machine-generated spam. Remove unsafe material, including CSAM (child sexual abuse material), and scrub PII (personal information like names, emails and phone numbers). Filtering gives you most of the measurable quality, and every filter is a judgement call about what the model will be good at (Chapter 16).
  3. Remove exact and near-duplicate documents across the whole collection. Duplicates waste compute, push the model to memorise, and ruin evaluation if benchmark questions sneak into the training data. Checking the data against every test set you plan to use is mandatory, not optional. Skipping it is one of the most common reasons frontier results turn out to be wrong.
  4. Decide how much code, how much maths, how many languages and how much conversation to include. Code improves reasoning even on tasks that aren't about code. Giving one area too much weight quietly costs you in another. Mixture weights come from 1%-scale ablations. A curriculum — general data first, the highest-quality and long-document material near the end — reliably beats one fixed blend.
  5. The tokenizer (which splits text into tokens) can never change for the life of the model. A bigger vocabulary means more parameters for the embedding table but shorter token sequences. Poor coverage of other languages becomes a permanent extra cost for every non-English user (Chapter 15). Split the data into shards (chunks) so any worker can read any shard, and make the order reproducible from a random seed.
  6. Mixture weights, learning rate, batch-size schedule, architecture details — choose all of them from small runs, then extend the results along a fitted scaling law. This is the most valuable activity in the whole programme. A week of ablations regularly saves a month of full-scale compute, and nothing else can rescue a wrong decision made on day zero.
  7. Save checkpoints often, keep running a fixed set of evaluations, and compare the loss curve against the predicted curve every day. A run that's 3% off the prediction on day five is telling you something. Investigating costs two days; ignoring it could cost eighty.

4 · Parallelism and the memory arithmetic

For 140 billion parameters, weights, optimizer state and gradients total 280 + 1,680 + 280 = 2,240 GB before activations and buffers. Dividing by aggregate GPU memory is only a first check: each rank must fit its own largest live tensors, temporary gathers and communication buffers. Profile the peak training step, including checkpoint preparation, rather than average memory use.

Parameters140B parameters in bf16 (2 bytes each) = 280 GB. That's already far more than one 80 GB GPU holds, before anything else is added.
Optimizer stateThe Adam optimizer keeps two running averages per parameter, plus a full-precision (fp32) master copy of the weights. That's about 12 bytes per parameter, or roughly 1.7 TB. This, not the weights, takes up most of the training memory (Chapter 23).
GradientsAnother 280 GB in bf16, plus buffers used while GPUs combine their gradients.
ActivationsThe intermediate results saved for the backward pass. They grow with batch size × sequence length × layers. Recomputing some of them instead of storing them costs about 30% extra compute but saves a lot of memory, and it's nearly always worth it.
The planSplit each layer 8 ways across the GPUs inside one machine (tensor parallelism, over fast NVLink). Split the layers into 16 stages across machines (pipeline parallelism). Run 80 copies of that setup on different data (data parallelism), with the optimizer state spread across them. Add sequence parallelism for long inputs. The rule of thumb: the dimension that talks most gets the fastest link (Chapter 24).
The constraintEvery kind of splitting saves memory but adds communication. Tensor parallelism beyond one machine chokes on network bandwidth. Pipeline parallelism leaves GPUs idle while waiting (called "bubbles"), and smarter schedules only partly hide them. Data parallelism needs a batch big enough to split up, yet small enough that the model still learns well.

Sanity-check the plan with MFU before committing: MFU = achieved FLOPs ÷ peak FLOPs. Above 45% is good at this scale. Below 30% means the network or the pipeline schedule is wasting a third of a budget of over a hundred million pounds. That's worth a week of profiling before day one, not after.

5 · Eighty-eight days

A background checkpoint is usable only after all shards and its manifest are durably complete. Publish the completion marker last and verify checksums on restore. Keep the previous checkpoint until the new one passes that check. Rehearse restoring with a failed rank, and verify that the data cursor and optimizer step agree so resumed training does not silently repeat or skip examples.

If a checkpoint takes two minutes and is saved every 30 minutes, blocking saves alone would consume about 6.7% of time. With failures occurring uniformly between saves, expected lost work is about 15 minutes per failure, plus restart time. Asynchronous saves reduce blocking but still need bandwidth and memory. Choose the interval from measured save cost, failure frequency and recovery time.

Day 0Burn-in: a 6-hour run at full scale with the real settings. Confirm MFU, that checkpoints save and restore, that the evaluation code works, and that the dashboards work. Teams that skip this find a broken restore on day nine, and lose everything since the last checkpoint they had actually tested.
Day 1–3Loss falls quickly and as predicted. Compare it against the scaling-law projection every few hours. Drifting away from the prediction early is the cheapest warning you'll ever get — and the last point where restarting is just embarrassing rather than a disaster.
Day 4First hardware failure. With 10,240 GPUs, a machine dies every few hours: memory errors, overheating, a broken optical cable. The run must spot the failure, remove the machine and resume from the last checkpoint automatically. Otherwise engineers spend ninety days restarting jobs by hand.
Day 11A straggler. One machine runs 8% slow — a GPU slowing itself down to stay cool, or a weak network link. Every step waits for all machines, so the whole cluster runs 8% slow. Timing each machine's steps finds it. Without that monitoring, it can hide for weeks and cost millions.
Day 23A loss spike: loss suddenly jumps up. Rewind to a checkpoint from before it, skip the data batches that caused it, and resume — sometimes with a lower learning rate. Spikes tend to come from bad data, so inspect those batches. Often it's a repeated document or a text-encoding glitch that deduplication missed.
Day 40One capability stops improving on the evaluations, while loss keeps falling. It's usually a data-mixture problem, and this is the most painful moment of the run. You can change the rest of the curriculum, but you can't undo the training that's already happened. This is exactly the situation the 1%-scale ablations were meant to prevent.
Day 66Silent data corruption. A shard starts serving garbled tokens, and loss gets worse without any error being raised. Your only defences are checksums on shards and automatic sanity checks on batch statistics. Hardware doesn't always tell you when it's wrong.
Day 88The learning rate winds down to its minimum and the final checkpoints are written. Pretraining is done, but the model isn't a product yet. Post-training is a separate programme with its own risks (Chapters 17 and 18).
🔁
Determinism, checkpointing and the ability to rewind

A 90-day run will be interrupted, so being able to resume exactly where you left off is a hard requirement. That means three things:

  • Save the model, optimizer state, data-loader position and random-number-generator state together, as one all-or-nothing checkpoint.
  • Write checkpoints in the background, so the cluster isn't sitting idle during a save of several terabytes.
  • Keep enough old checkpoints to rewind past a spike that's only noticed hours later.

Teams that keep only the last two checkpoints find out on day 23 that both are already contaminated (Chapter 25).

6 · Evaluation and the launch decision

Separate training diagnosis from product release. Compare loss at the same number of processed tokens to detect learning changes; compare tokens per second to detect throughput changes. A slow node can shift loss against wall-clock time without changing learning per token. During post-training, reassess capabilities and serving costs because the final deployed configuration may differ from the pretrained checkpoint.

The launch decision isn't "did the loss go down?". It's four questions. Is the capability gain big enough to justify the serving cost? Are the safety results acceptable? Is the contamination audit clean? And do people prefer it, in a blind comparison, over the model you already ship? A model can win every benchmark and still be the wrong thing to launch.

7 · Where the money goes

For a fixed amount of work, raising MFU from 45% to 49.5% is a 10% relative throughput gain. Runtime falls to 45 ÷ 49.5 = 90.9% of its former value, a 9.1% saving on the affected compute. On the illustrative £86.8M pretraining line, that is about £7.9M if charges fall with runtime. Reserved capacity, fixed deadlines and other programme costs change the realized saving.

The run consumes 10,240 × 88 × 24 = 21,626,880 GPU-hours. Dividing the £86.8M pretraining allocation by that total implies roughly £4.01 per GPU-hour for that budget line. This is a consistency check on the example, not a current hardware quote. State which networking, storage and operations costs sit inside the line before using it elsewhere.

That's why two weeks of profiling before day one is one of the best-value engineering decisions you can make. It's also why MFU is watched like a production SLO (a service-level target) for three months.

8 · What breaks

Do not automatically blame every loss spike on bad data. Preserve the suspect batches and check numerical overflow, learning-rate changes, gradient norms and hardware health. Reproduce the event on an isolated copy before deciding to skip data or rewind the main run. Record the intervention and its dataset version so later evaluations can explain what the model actually saw.

Interview drill

Follow-up: “Day 30 of 88. Loss is 4% above the projected curve. What do you do?”

How a strong answer sounds
First, work out whether the problem is the model or the measurement. I'd check the evaluation code and how loss is computed before believing anything. A tokenizer or masking change in the evaluation code produces exactly this pattern, and it costs nothing to rule out. If the gap is real, I'd break it down. Is it the same across all data areas, or concentrated in one? One area would point at the mixture or a corrupted shard. Are batch statistics normal, are shard checksums clean, did anything change in the data loader? Are throughput and MFU normal? A straggler makes the run slower, not worse, but the two get confused. Is it linked to gradient size or the learning rate — a schedule bug, or the after-effects of a spike that only partly recovered? Then comes the decision, which really is hard. A 4% gap at day 30, with a smooth curve and normal internals, is often something you ride out. Rewinding costs a third of the budget, and the projection itself has error bars, since it was fitted from small runs. A 4% gap that's growing, or that lines up with one specific data area, means rewinding to the last clean checkpoint. What makes this answerable at all is having set the thresholds in advance — before the run, when nobody has sunk £50M into the current path and everyone can still think clearly.

Follow-up: “The model beats your current production model on every benchmark. Do you launch it?”

How a strong answer sounds
Not on that evidence alone. I'd want four more things. First, a contamination audit. Benchmark gains are exactly what you'd expect if test data leaked into a 14-trillion-token training set, and that's the most common reason a frontier result turns out not to be real. Second, human preference in blind side-by-side comparisons on our real mix of traffic, because benchmark gains often don't show up in anything a user can feel. Third, safety and red-team results: a more capable model is also more capable of harm, so that evaluation isn't optional. Fourth, serving economics. If it's 5% better and costs three times as much per token, the right move may be to distil it into a smaller model, or send only hard queries to it, rather than replacing the production model entirely. Then I'd ship it like anything else: shadow mode, a canary, a staged rollout with guardrails on latency, cost and safety, and a rollback plan someone has actually tested. Finishing the training run doesn't make the model a product. It makes it a candidate.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Compute-optimal gives the lowest training loss. If you're going to serve the model, deliberately over-training a smaller one usually wins on total cost.
  • Data filtering, deduplication and the mixture set the model's upper limit, and they're fixed before the first full-scale step runs.
  • Ablations and scaling-law fits at 1% scale give the best return in the programme. Nothing else can rescue a wrong day-zero decision.
  • At 10,000 GPUs, failures, slow machines and silent corruption are routine. Automatic resume, per-machine monitoring and a ladder of checkpoints are essential.
  • At this scale, MFU is a money metric — ten percentage points is millions of pounds.
  • The launch decision weighs capability, contamination, human preference, safety and serving cost together. Winning every benchmark decides nothing on its own.

Check your understanding

Why might you deliberately train a smaller model past its compute-optimal point?

Compute-optimal answers "what gives the lowest training loss for this training budget?". If you'll serve a trillion tokens a month for two years, that's the wrong question to ask.

What dominates memory during training?

140B parameters is 280 GB of bf16 weights, but about 1.7 TB of optimizer state. That's why spreading the optimizer state across the data-parallel GPUs is the first thing you do.

One node out of 10,240 runs 8% slower. What is the effect?

In synchronous training, every step waits for all workers, so the whole run moves at the pace of the slowest one. Timing each worker's steps turns an invisible multi-million-pound leak into a ten-minute fix: remove the slow machine.

Why are ablations at 1% scale the highest-value activity in the programme?

A week of ablations regularly saves a month of full-scale compute. Once the run is two weeks in, nothing can undo a wrong data-mixture or learning-rate decision.

Your model wins on every public benchmark. What must be verified first?

Contamination produces exactly this symptom. With 14 trillion tokens of training data, it's what happens by default unless you actively checked. Human preference, safety and serving cost are the other three gates.
