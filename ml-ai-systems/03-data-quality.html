Data: Sourcing, Labeling & Quality

A model is a compressed summary of its training data. Whatever the data gets wrong — missing groups of people, wrong labels, information from the future that slipped in — the model will copy, and then make worse. This chapter will save you more pain later than any other.

  • Compare where training data comes from, and what each source costs you
  • Get labels that are good enough, and measure how good they are
  • Split data so your offline score predicts what really happens live
  • Spot data leakage — the most common cause of results that are “too good”
  • Handle rare classes (imbalance) and check data automatically

Where does training data come from?

There are five realistic sources, and most real systems use several. The question is never “which is best?”. It's “what does each one cost, and how does it skew the model?”.

Provenance is an engineering requirement, not paperwork

For every dataset, write down where it came from, when, under what licence or consent, and whose data can be deleted from it. One day someone will ask for their data to be deleted, or legal will ask whether a dataset may be used commercially. “We scraped it in 2023” won't be an answer. Chapter 37 covers the rules side. The engineering side is simple: version your datasets and track where each one came from (its lineage).

Labels: the expensive part

A label is the answer you're teaching the model to give. Getting labels is usually the slowest part. So teams trade quality against quantity in four standard ways.

👩‍⚖️

Expert labels

Radiologists, lawyers, senior support agents. Best quality, fewest labels, highest cost. Save them for your evaluation set (the data you judge the model on).

👥

Crowd labels

Many cheap annotators, 3–5 per item, and the majority vote wins. Needs clear guidelines, plus “gold” questions with known answers to catch careless workers.

🔖

Weak supervision

Rules of thumb, regexes and existing systems each vote on a label. A model learns how to combine their votes. Noisy, but almost free at scale.

🖱️

Implicit / natural

The world labels it for you: the payment was reversed (charged back), the user re-ran the search, the ticket was reopened.

📏
Measure your labellers before you measure your model

Have two annotators label the same 300 items and measure how often they agree (Cohen's κ is the standard score). If humans only agree 70% of the time, no model will do much better than about 70% on that task. Your “model error analysis” will mostly be arguments about labels. Low agreement usually means the guidelines are unclear, not that the annotators are bad. Fix the definition first.

Why κ instead of plain "percent agreed"? Because two people can agree a lot by luck. Suppose 90% of comments are not toxic, and two annotators each say "not toxic" 90% of the time, fairly randomly. They would agree on about 0.9 × 0.9 + 0.1 × 0.1 = 82% of items without reading anything carefully. If their real agreement is 90%, κ asks how much of the possible improvement over chance they achieved: (0.90 − 0.82) / (1 − 0.82) ≈ 0.44. That is only moderate, even though "90% agreement" sounds excellent. As a rough guide, κ above about 0.8 is strong, and below about 0.6 means the task needs a clearer definition.

A rough model: accuracy grows with the log of dataset size (each 10× more data adds a fixed amount), but label quality puts a cap on it. The curve isn't exact, but the lesson holds: past a certain point, cleaning labels beats collecting more.

Splits: the contract with your future self

You split data into three parts. Train is for fitting the parameters. Validation is for choosing settings (hyperparameters) and picking between models. Test gives one honest final score, so touch it as rarely as possible. How you split matters more than the exact percentages.

Why three sets and not two? Every time you look at a score and make a choice because of it, some of that data's quirks leak into your decisions. Try 50 learning rates and keep the one with the best validation score, and part of that winning score is luck: the setting that happened to suit those particular rows. The validation score is now a little optimistic. The test set, untouched during all that choosing, is what tells you the honest number. If you check the test set after every experiment, it quietly becomes a second validation set, and you no longer have an honest number at all.

On size: the test set needs to be big enough that its score is stable, not a fixed share. With 5,000 rows, 15% is 750 examples, which is about the minimum for a usable score. With 50 million rows, 1% is still 500,000 examples, far more than enough, so the rest can go to training.

⏱️
If time matters, split by time. Always.

Any system that predicts the future — churn, demand, fraud, recommendations — must be tested on data from after the training period. A random split lets the model learn from Tuesday to predict Monday. It can never do that in real life. The offline score will look wonderful, and the launch will disappoint.

A concrete time split, for a model trained in July: train on January to April, validate on May, test on June. Every validation and test row is later than every training row, just as live traffic will be later than everything the model saw. Two more cases need care. When the same user appears many times, also make sure a test user's earlier rows do not sit in training if you are testing on new users (a grouped split). When one class is rare, a stratified split keeps the same share of positives in each part, so the test set is not left with, say, 3 fraud cases instead of 30.

Leakage: why your model looks brilliant

Data leakage is any information in training that won't be available — or won't be honest — at the moment you make a prediction. It's like a student who saw the answer sheet: great exam score, no real skill. Leakage is the most common reason an offline result doesn't hold up live, and it's embarrassingly easy to cause.

It comes in two main kinds, and it helps to name them:

  • Target leakage: a feature is really a consequence of the answer. Predicting churn with a "cancellation reason" column is the classic case: it is only filled in once the customer has already left. The model learns "reason is not empty means churn", scores almost perfectly offline, and at prediction time the column is always empty.
  • Train–test contamination: information from the validation or test rows reaches training. Examples are fitting a scaler on all rows (so the training data "knows" the test set's average), duplicates landing on both sides of the split, or a random split of time-ordered data.

Both kinds make the offline score describe a situation that will never happen live.

🔍
The leak-detector heuristics
  • Suspiciously good. 0.999 AUC on a hard problem is a bug until proven otherwise.
  • One feature dominates. Check feature importance. If a single column does all the work, ask when that column gets filled in compared with the label.
  • Ask “when is this known?” for every feature. Write the time next to each one. Anything recorded after the moment of prediction must go.
  • Prepare data inside the split. Scalers, missing-value fillers (imputers), target encoders and vocabularies must be fitted on training data only. Then apply them to validation.

Imbalance: when the interesting class is 0.2%

Fraud, disease, defects, ad clicks — the thing you're looking for (the positive class) is rare. Accuracy stops meaning anything: always predict “no” and you score 99.8%. And during training, the gradients are dominated by the “no” examples.

⬆️ Resample

Copy the rare examples more often (oversampling, or SMOTE, which creates synthetic ones), or drop some common ones (undersampling). Cheap, but it changes how common positives look — so you must recalibrate the probabilities afterwards (Ch 7).

⚖️ Reweight

Make mistakes on rare examples count more in the loss. You keep all the data, and it usually beats resampling. Watch out for the model memorising the few positives you have (overfitting).

🎯 Change the metric

Often the real fix. Optimise and report PR-AUC, recall at a precision your review team can handle, or expected money lost — not accuracy.

The two numbers that replace accuracy. Take the 1,000 transactions above, with 4 frauds. A model flags 10 transactions, and 3 of them are real fraud.

  • Precision = of the ones you flagged, how many were fraud: 3 / 10 = 30%. This is how much of your review team's time is well spent.
  • Recall = of the real frauds, how many you caught: 3 / 4 = 75%. This is how much fraud you stopped.

Accuracy for this model is 992 / 1,000 = 99.2%, lower than the useless "always legit" model, even though it catches most of the fraud. That is exactly why accuracy misleads here. PR-AUC summarises the trade-off between precision and recall across every threshold; Chapter 7 covers it properly.

Choosing a fix. Reweighting is the usual first choice: with 4 positives and 996 negatives, a weight of about 249 on each positive makes both classes count equally in the loss. Resample when the data is too big to train on in full: keeping 1 in 10 negatives makes training ten times cheaper. But the model then sees positives ten times more often than in real life, so its predicted odds come out roughly ten times too high. Divide the odds by 10 (or recalibrate on untouched data) before treating the outputs as probabilities.

Data quality, automated

You can't eyeball a table that grows by 40 million rows a day. Treat data like code: write down what you expect, test it in the pipeline, and fail loudly when it's wrong.

CheckExample expectationAction on failure
SchemaColumn amount exists, is float, non-nullStop the pipeline completely — never train on it
Range / validity0 < age < 120, currency is one of the allowed valuesSet bad rows aside, alert, carry on

🗂️
Version datasets like code

You can only reproduce a model if you can get back the exact same data. Use snapshots that never change (a table version, a partition date, a content hash). Record that ID next to every training run (Ch 31). “We trained on the users table” can't be reproduced. “users@2026-04-02T00:00Z, sha 9f3c…” can.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Splitting time-based data at random. Instant leakage, wildly optimistic offline scores, disappointing launch.
  • Fitting the scaler on all the data before splitting. The validation set's averages have now leaked into training. Fit it on the training part only.
  • Removing duplicates after splitting. Near-identical rows end up in both train and test, so your test partly checks memory. Remove duplicates first, split second.
  • Cleaning the test set selectively. If you only fix labels where the model disagrees, you've tuned your evaluation, not your model. Fix labels everywhere, by the same rules, or not at all.
  • Not looking at 50 random rows. Ten minutes reading raw examples finds text-encoding bugs, cut-off fields and wrong labels that no summary statistic will show you.
🎤
Interview drill

Warm-up: Your churn model hits 0.97 AUC offline and does nothing in the A/B test. Walk through your debugging.

How a strong answer sounds
First, suspect leakage. List every feature with the time it gets written. Look for anything that comes from the cancellation itself: support contacts about closing, a downgrade flag, the refund record. Second, check the split. If it was random over time-ordered data, redo it by time and see how much of the AUC survives. Third, check that training and serving match: are live feature values computed the same way as offline? Only after those three would I look at the model. And separately: even a perfect predictor changes nothing if the action you take doesn't work. So I'd check whether the intervention, not the prediction, is the weak link.

Design: You need 100,000 labelled examples of “is this support reply helpful?” with a budget for 5,000 human labels. What do you do?

How a strong answer sounds
Spend the human budget on a clean, expert-labelled evaluation set, plus enough to measure agreement. For example: 2,000 for evaluation, 500 labelled twice to compute κ, and 2,500 as starter training data. Get the bulk through weak supervision, using signals like thread reopened, thumbs-down, escalation, or the tone of the customer's reply. Combine those noisy signals into labels with a probability attached. Then train on the weak labels and evaluate only on the clean set. If humans agree poorly on the clean set, stop and rewrite the guidelines: the task definition is the bug.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Every data source skews the model in a predictable way. Name that skew when you choose the source.
  • How well humans agree is your accuracy ceiling. Measure it before blaming the model.
  • Split by time for anything that predicts the future, and by group when many rows belong to the same user, patient or item.
  • Leakage is the first thing to suspect when results look surprisingly good. Check when each feature becomes known.
  • Automate checks on schema, volume, distribution and freshness. Version datasets so every run can be reproduced.

Check your understanding

You're predicting whether a loan will default. Which feature is leakage?

Collections calls only happen after payments are missed, so this feature is a result of the label. At prediction time (when someone applies) it is always zero, so the model learns nothing it can actually use.

Two annotators agree on only 65% of your toxicity labels. What should you do first?

65% agreement caps how well any model can do, and it means the task definition is unclear. Dropping disputed examples leaves only the easy cases. And a bigger model can't learn a rule that humans can't state.

Your dataset has 1,000 images per patient from 200 patients. What split is correct?

A random split puts images of the same patient in both train and test. The model can then memorise the patient instead of learning the disease. Group by whatever repeats — here, the patient.

Fraud is 0.3% of transactions. Your model reports 99.7% accuracy. What has it most likely learned?

Just saying “legitimate” every time already scores 99.7%. Report PR-AUC, or recall at a precision you can work with, instead. Never make accuracy the headline metric when one class is rare.

Which pipeline step order avoids leakage?

Remove duplicates before splitting, so near-copies can't land on both sides. Split by time. Fit every transform on training data only, then apply it to validation and test.
