Evaluation, Metrics & Validation

Training is easy to get working, and when it's broken you usually notice. Evaluation is the opposite. It looks trivial, yet almost every serious ML failure comes from measuring the wrong thing, on the wrong data, at the wrong threshold.

  • Choose a metric based on the decision the model drives
  • Read confusion matrices, ROC and PR curves — and know when each one misleads
  • Set a threshold from costs, not from 0.5
  • Check calibration before using probabilities for money decisions
  • Evaluate slices, measure uncertainty, and do honest error analysis

Validation: the discipline of not fooling yourself

Why so much ceremony? Because a model's score on the data it trained on says almost nothing about how it will do on tomorrow's data. A deep tree can score 100% on its training rows and 70% on new ones. The only way to estimate real-world performance is to measure on rows the model — and you, while making choices — never learned from.

Three datasets, three jobs. Train fits the parameters. Validation chooses everything else: settings, architectures, thresholds, whether to ship. Test gives one honest number. But every time you look at the test score and change something, the test set becomes a little more like a validation set.

A typical split for 100,000 rows is 80,000 train, 10,000 validation, 10,000 test. With millions of rows you can keep the validation and test shares smaller (1–2% is often plenty), because what matters is having enough examples of each outcome to measure reliably, not a fixed percentage. With only a few thousand rows, a single 10% slice is too small and noisy — that's where cross-validation comes in.

📚

k-fold CV

Cross-validation: split the data into k parts, train k times (each time holding out a different part), and average the scores. Every row gets used for both training and testing. Best when data is scarce and rows are independent.

📅

Rolling-origin CV

For time series: train on weeks 1–4, test on week 5; train on 1–5, test on 6; and so on. The only honest option when you're predicting the future.

👥

Grouped CV

Keep all rows for one user, patient or seller inside the same part. Otherwise you're measuring memory, not skill.

A concrete k-fold run: 2,000 rows and k = 5 gives five folds of 400. Round 1 trains on folds 2–5 (1,600 rows) and scores fold 1; round 2 trains on folds 1, 3, 4, 5 and scores fold 2; and so on. You might get scores of 0.81, 0.84, 0.79, 0.83 and 0.82. Report the mean, 0.818, and the spread — here roughly ±0.02 — because that spread is a free warning of how much the score would wobble on different data. The cost is training five models instead of one, which is why big deep-learning projects usually skip it and rely on one large validation set.

🎰
Multiple comparisons: how teams overfit the validation set

Try 200 model variants and pick the best validation score, and part of what you picked is luck. Expect it to drop on the test set. To limit this: keep a test set nobody touches, prefer the simpler model when scores are within noise, and report a range (a confidence interval) instead of a single number.

Classification: it all starts with four numbers

Every classification metric is built from the same four counts in the confusion matrix: true positives, false positives, false negatives and true negatives. Move the threshold below and watch every metric shift together. No setting improves them all.

First, where the threshold comes from. A classifier like logistic regression doesn't output “fraud” or “legit”; it outputs a score such as 0.73. You turn that into a decision by picking a cut-off: flag everything above it. The four counts then compare each decision with the truth. “Positive” just means the class you're looking for (fraud), and the words true/false say whether the model was right.

Worked example. 10,000 transactions, 200 of them fraud. At some threshold the model flags 250, and 150 of those really are fraud.

  • TP = 150 (fraud, flagged). FP = 100 (legit, flagged). FN = 50 (fraud we missed). TN = 9,700 (legit, left alone).
  • Precision = 150 / 250 = 0.60. Six in ten flags are real.
  • Recall = 150 / 200 = 0.75. We caught three quarters of the fraud.
  • Accuracy = (150 + 9,700) / 10,000 = 98.5%. Sounds superb — until you notice that flagging nothing scores 98.0%.

Why a harmonic mean for F1? F1 = 2·P·R / (P + R). Unlike a plain average, it stays low if either number is low. In the example above, P = 0.60 and R = 0.75 give F1 ≈ 0.67. But a model that flags a single case and gets it right has P = 1.0 and R = 0.005: the plain average would be about 0.5, while F1 is about 0.01 — correctly calling it useless.

What the two curves are. Each threshold gives one confusion matrix, and so one point. Sweep the threshold from 1 down to 0 and join the points up. The ROC curve plots recall (the true-positive rate, TPR) against the false-positive rate, FPR = FP / (FP + TN) — the share of legit cases wrongly flagged. In the example, FPR = 100 / 9,800 ≈ 1%. The PR curve plots precision against recall. A perfect model hugs the top-left of the ROC chart (AUC = 1.0); random guessing sits on the diagonal (AUC = 0.5). “AUC” is simply the area under a curve, a single number that sums up all thresholds at once — handy for comparing models, but it hides the one threshold you will actually use.

📉
Why ROC-AUC flatters imbalanced models

The false-positive rate (FPR) divides by the huge number of negatives. So 5,000 false alarms out of 980,000 legitimate transactions is an FPR of just 0.5% — invisible on an ROC curve. Precision divides by the number of flags instead. Those same 5,000 false alarms next to 300 real catches give a precision of 6%: your review team drowns. When the positive class is rare, report PR-AUC, and precision at the recall you actually run at.

Thresholds come from costs, not conventions

0.5 is the default only because it's the middle. The right threshold is the one with the lowest expected cost:

flag if  P(positive) × cost_of_miss  >  (1 − P(positive)) × cost_of_false_alarm

# fraud: £500 miss, £8 false alarm  →  flag above p ≈ 0.016
# spam:  £0.05 miss, £5 false alarm (lost email) →  flag above p ≈ 0.99

Where do those two numbers come from? The rule says: flag when the expected cost of letting it through is bigger than the expected cost of stopping it. Solve the inequality for p and the threshold is cost_of_false_alarm / (cost_of_false_alarm + cost_of_miss). For fraud that's 8 / (8 + 500) ≈ 0.016: a miss costs so much more that you should flag anything with even a 1.6% chance of fraud. For spam it's 5 / (5 + 0.05) ≈ 0.99: losing a real email is so painful that you only filter when you're nearly certain. Note that this only works if the model's probabilities are honest — which is what the calibration section below is about.

In practice you often flip the question around: “we can review 2,000 cases a day — which threshold catches the most at that volume?” That's recall at fixed review capacity, and it's what operations teams actually care about.

Calibration: are the probabilities real?

A model is calibrated if its probabilities can be taken at face value: of all the cases it scored 0.30, about 30% really are positive. Ranking metrics don't care about this. But any decision involving money, thresholds or further calculations cares a great deal.

For example, an ad system bids P(click) × value_per_click. If the model says 0.04 but the true click rate for those ads is 0.02, every bid is double what it should be, and the company overpays on every auction — even though the ranking of ads might be perfect.

How do you check it? Group the validation predictions into buckets by score (0–0.1, 0.1–0.2, and so on). In each bucket, compare the average predicted probability with the fraction that really were positive. Suppose the 0.7–0.8 bucket holds 500 cases with an average score of 0.75, but only 300 of them (0.60) were positive. That bucket is overconfident by 0.15. The reliability diagram below plots exactly this, one bar per bucket. Expected calibration error (ECE) is the average of those gaps, weighted by how many cases sit in each bucket. Zero means perfectly calibrated.

Fixes: Platt scaling (fit a logistic regression on the validation scores), isotonic regression (a flexible step-shaped mapping that needs more data), or temperature scaling for neural nets (divide the logits by one learned number T). Always fit these on held-out data, never on training data.

⚖️
Resampling breaks calibration

Say you oversampled positives from 1% to 50% to help training. The model now believes the world is 50% positive, and its outputs are far too high. Either undo the shift with maths (adjust the intercept by log(w)), or recalibrate on held-out data with the true base rate. This catches people out all the time in fraud and ads.

What is w in that fix? It's how much you inflated the odds of the positive class. Going from 1% to 50% positive moves the odds from 1:99 to 1:1, so w = 99. Logistic models add up evidence on the log-odds scale, so subtracting log(99) ≈ 4.6 from the model's raw score (the logit, before the sigmoid) undoes the shift. Check: a case the resampled model scores 0.5 (logit 0) becomes logit −4.6, which is a probability of about 0.01 — back to the true base rate.

MAE versus RMSE, with numbers. Four forecasts miss by 2, 2, 2 and 10 units. MAE, the mean absolute error, is (2+2+2+10)/4 = 4. RMSE squares first, averages, then takes the square root: √((4+4+4+100)/4) = √28 ≈ 5.3. The one big miss pulls RMSE up much more. Pick RMSE when big misses are disproportionately bad (running out of stock on a busy day), and MAE when every unit of error costs about the same. R² answers “how much better than always predicting the average?” — 1.0 is perfect, 0 is no better than the average.

Averages hide everything interesting

A 4-point gain overall can hide a 20-point drop for new users. Slice your evaluation — break it down by every group that matters for the business or for fairness. Then set alerts on the worst slice, not the average.

How can the overall number rise while two slices fall? Because the overall score behaves roughly like a weighted average of the slices, and the big slice dominates. Returning users are 78% of traffic, so their +0.046 contributes about 0.78 × 0.046 ≈ 0.036 to the total all by itself — nearly the whole overall gain — which easily outweighs the losses on the two small slices. One practical caution: very small slices give noisy scores. Before you panic about a drop, check how many examples the slice holds (the next section shows how).

Is the difference real?

Model A scores 0.843 and model B scores 0.847. Before you celebrate, ask whether that gap would survive a slightly different test set. Two cheap tools help:

🔁 Bootstrap CI

Build 1,000 new test sets by drawing rows from yours at random, with repeats allowed. Recompute the metric on each, and report the middle 95% range (2.5th–97.5th percentiles). If the two models' ranges overlap a lot, they're tied.

🪙 Paired test

Compare both models on the same examples and test the differences (McNemar for classification, paired bootstrap otherwise). Pairing removes “some examples are just harder” as a source of noise, so you need far less data.

To get a feel for the size of this noise: on a test set of 1,000 examples, an accuracy of 85% has a 95% range of roughly ±2.2 points (about 82.8% to 87.2%). Even with 10,000 examples it's still about ±0.7 points. So a gap of 0.4 points between two models, measured on an ordinary test set, is well inside the noise unless a paired test says otherwise. The range shrinks with the square root of the test-set size: four times the data halves it.

🔬
Error analysis beats one more metric

Take 100 mistakes, read them, and sort each into a bucket. Typically you'll find about 40% are wrong labels, 25% are one repeated failure pattern, and 35% are genuinely hard. That tells you exactly what to do next — relabel, add a feature, or accept the limit. No overall number will ever tell you that.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Reporting accuracy when one class is rare. Always guessing the common class already scores 99%, so the number tells you nothing.
  • Tuning the threshold on the test set. A threshold is a setting like any other — choose it on validation data.
  • Comparing models at different thresholds. Compare whole curves, or compare at a matched point (same recall, or same number of reviews).
  • Ignoring calibration when the score drives a decision rule. A badly calibrated 0.9 and a well calibrated 0.9 lead to different, expensive actions.
  • Evaluating only the average. Slice by how long users have been around, locale, device and time — or the drop will find you in production.
  • Letting the test set go stale. Reuse it fifty times and it's no longer a real test set. Refresh it now and then with new data.
🎤
Interview drill

Warm-up: Your model has 0.98 ROC-AUC on fraud detection. The ops team says it's useless. Explain how both can be true.

How a strong answer sounds
With only 0.2% fraud, ROC-AUC is dominated by the huge number of legitimate transactions. A tiny false-positive rate still means thousands of alerts a day. At the threshold the team uses, precision might be 4%. So reviewers see 24 legitimate cases for every real fraud, and they stop trusting the queue. The right framing is precision at their review capacity — “with 500 reviews a day, we catch X% of fraud at Y% precision” — with PR-AUC as the main offline metric. Then set the threshold by expected cost, not at 0.5.

Deeper: How would you decide whether a 0.4% offline improvement is worth shipping?

How a strong answer sounds
First, is it real? Run a paired bootstrap on the same test examples and get a confidence interval. If the interval includes zero, it's noise. Second, is it real where it matters? Check the slices and the threshold you'll actually use, not just the overall curve. Third, what does it cost? A 0.4% gain from a model that triples serving cost or adds 40 ms of delay is usually a loss. Finally, the offline number only predicts the online one. So the real decision is whether to spend an A/B test slot on it — and the size of the expected effect decides how long that test must run to detect it.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Choose metrics from the decision and its costs. Accuracy is almost never the right headline.
  • When one class is rare, use PR-AUC and precision at a workable recall, not ROC-AUC.
  • Choose thresholds from expected cost or review capacity, using validation data.
  • Calibrate before any probability drives a money decision, and recalibrate after resampling.
  • Slice everything, measure uncertainty, and read 100 errors before tuning anything else.

Check your understanding

Disease affects 1% of patients. Your classifier reports 99% accuracy. What is the most useful next question?

Always answering “healthy” already scores 99%. Only metrics on the positive class show whether the model detects anything at all.

You lower the decision threshold from 0.5 to 0.2. What happens?

A lower bar flags more cases. You catch more real positives (higher recall) and more false ones (lower precision). The model itself hasn't changed — only the point you operate at.

A model's scores rank perfectly but every probability is roughly double the true rate. Which metric is unaffected?

AUC only depends on the order of the scores. Any distortion that keeps the order leaves it unchanged. That's exactly why AUC alone can't tell you the probabilities are safe to act on.

You oversampled positives from 1% to 50% during training. What must you do before using the probabilities for a money decision?

The model learned a world where positives are 50% likely, so its outputs are hugely inflated. Adjust the intercept with maths, or fit a calibration mapping on held-out data that has the real positive rate.

Model B beats model A by 0.003 AUC on a 5,000-row test set. What's the right response?

On 5,000 rows, a 0.003 gap is well within random noise. Compare the two on the same examples and look at the interval before spending an experiment slot on it.
