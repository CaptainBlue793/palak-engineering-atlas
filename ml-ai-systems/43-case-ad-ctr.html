Case Study: Ad Click Prediction

Predict how likely an ad is to be clicked — its click-through rate (CTR) — one million times a second, in under ten milliseconds, and accurately enough that an auction can charge real money based on it. Few ML systems face tighter time limits, need more trustworthy probabilities, or give more people a reason to game them.

  • Understand why the numbers must be right (calibration), not just in the right order
  • Handle billions of possible feature values (sparse features) within a fixed amount of memory
  • Answer in 10 ms while handling a million requests a second
  • Train with delayed conversions and position bias
  • Spread each advertiser's budget through the day, explore, and give new advertisers a chance
📚
What you need before this chapter
  • Represent sparse inputs with features and embeddings.
  • Understand calibration, class imbalance and thresholds.
  • Separate candidate retrieval from ranking.

1 · Requirements

Separate requests from predictions before sizing the system. One million requests each containing 500 candidates require 500 million candidate scores per second. Batching those candidates shares request-level features, but does not remove their model work. If a replica sustains 2,000 complete requests/second within the deadline, the illustrative minimum is 1,000,000 ÷ 2,000 = 500 replicas, before headroom and failures.

This is a pricing model, not a ranking model

The auction estimates expected earnings per impression as bid times P(click). Multiply by 1,000 for earnings per thousand impressions, or eCPM. For campaigns that pay per sale, also multiply by P(convert | click). These estimates influence allocation, pricing and budget decisions.

Calibration means a predicted 1% really happens about 1% of the time. Incorrect probabilities can distort those decisions even when the ranking looks good. The exact price effect depends on the auction: a uniform multiplier cancels from the simplified second-price CPC calculation below.

Calibration is the product requirement here — getting the order right is not enough (Chapter 7).

🎯
Metrics

Offline: log loss (a scoring rule that can only be improved by giving honest probabilities), calibration error overall and for each group (advertiser, placement, device, country), and AUC as a secondary check. Online: revenue per thousand requests, the return advertisers get on their spend (ROI/ROAS), fill rate (how often an ad slot gets filled), and whether campaigns spend their budget as planned. Guardrails: p99 latency, advertisers getting much more or less delivery than promised, and exposure for new advertisers. Baseline: the past CTR for each (ad, placement) pair, pulled toward the average when there's little data (Bayesian shrinkage). Genuinely strong for big advertisers, useless for new ones.

2 · Features: billions of sparse values

A sparse feature space has many possible values, while one request uses only a few. A request might contain one device type, one country and a few recent categories. You look up those rows instead of building a vector with billions of mostly empty positions. For 100 million IDs, 16 numbers per embedding and 2 bytes per number, the raw table needs 3.2 GB. Optimizer state, replicas and lookup metadata need additional memory.

Hashing plus embeddings, with a bounded table

Combined (cross) features have no real limit on how many values they can take. So hash them into a fixed number of slots (Chapter 4). If two rare values land in the same slot, it costs very little.

Frequent ids get their own learned embedding. Rare ones share hashed buckets and rely on descriptive features instead.

The embedding table is the model: tens of gigabytes, split across parameter servers. An id only gets its own row after being seen N times, and ids that stop appearing are dropped. Fixed memory is a hard design constraint, not a nice-to-have.

3 · The 10 ms budget

Set one deadline for the complete request and pass its remaining time to each dependency. If the feature service stalls, waiting for its normal timeout can consume the entire auction budget. Use a tested missing-feature path and record that the fallback was used. A stale campaign-budget record needs separate treatment: a valid prediction must not make an ineligible campaign eligible.

🖥️
This runs on CPUs, and that's deliberate

The model is shallow, and most of the work is looking up embeddings rather than multiplying big matrices. GPUs are good at the second thing, so they don't help much here. And putting CPUs on the same machines as the ad server avoids a network trip that would eat a quarter of the 10 ms budget.

You scale out, not up: thousands of small copies, each holding a slice of the embedding table or reading from a store next door. Serving costs a fraction of a penny per thousand requests — but at 500 million scorings a second, a 5% efficiency gain is a lot of money.

4 · Deep dive: what miscalibration costs

Work through two bidders. A £2 bid with 1% CTR has £20 eCPM; a £1 bid with 3% CTR has £30 eCPM. The second ad wins despite its lower bid. In the simplified second-price auction, its CPC is £20 ÷ (1,000 × 0.03), about £0.67. Double both predicted CTRs and the price is £40 ÷ (1,000 × 0.06), still £0.67. Change only one advertiser's prediction and the winner or price can change.

The simulator's ROI field compares actual with predicted click probability; it is not a complete profit calculation. There are no reserve prices or live pacing decisions in this auction model. Uniform bias can affect those additional components in a real system, but this simulation cannot demonstrate that effect. Keep calibration checks by advertiser and placement so opposite errors do not cancel in the overall average.

5 · Deep dive: delayed conversions

Suppose 100 eventual purchases follow a cohort of clicks, but only 40 have appeared by day one. Calling all remaining clicks negative teaches the model that this cohort converts less than it really does. A delay model estimates how much of the outcome is still unobserved at each age. Check its estimates against older, fully observed cohorts, and separate campaigns whose customers buy at different speeds.

You see a click within seconds. A conversion (the purchase or sign-up that follows) may take 30 days — or never happen. If you train on “has it converted yet?”, every recent conversion that hasn't landed yet gets labelled as a failure. That biases the model.

⏳

Wait for the window

The labels are right, but the model is always 30 days out of date. On its own, that's useless in a market that changes every week.

📐

Delay modelling

Model how long conversions usually take, then adjust the training for examples whose outcome isn't known yet (they're "censored"). This is the standard approach, and it lets you train honestly on recent data.

🔁

Importance weighting

Train on fresh data, weighting each example by how likely it is that its conversion simply hasn't arrived yet. Then feed conversions back in as they land.

6 · Deep dive: budget pacing and exploration

For a £10,000 daily budget, even pacing targets about £416.67 per hour. If only £300 has been spent after the first hour, the controller can increase auction participation. Limit how quickly it changes: delayed spend reports can otherwise cause repeated overcorrection. Allocate bounded budget shares to serving regions so they cannot each spend the full global balance during a network interruption.

🥶
Cold-start advertisers and the exploration tax

A new campaign has no views, so its predicted CTR is just the default guess, so it loses auctions, so it never gets views. Left alone, the marketplace freezes around the advertisers already winning.

Fixes:

  • Start with a hopeful estimate based on similar ads and advertisers — a layered model that borrows information from the campaign, advertiser and category levels.
  • Set aside a budget for exploration, choosing ads by sampling from what their CTR might plausibly be (Thompson sampling).
  • Guarantee every new campaign a minimum number of views.

This costs measurable revenue today. It's the price of a working market, exactly as in Chapter 41.

7 · Training

Negative sampling changes the class balance. In 100,000 impressions at 0.8% CTR, there are 800 clicks and 99,200 non-clicks. Keeping every click but only one tenth of non-clicks leaves 800 ÷ (800 + 9,920) = 7.46% clicks in training. The odds, rather than the probability itself, increased tenfold. Undo that sampling with appropriate weights or a probability correction, then validate calibration on an unsampled holdout.

Deploy the feature schema, embedding table, model and calibrator as a compatible versioned set. A new embedding row order with an old model can return plausible but meaningless predictions. Shadow-score a sample first, inspect missing-feature rates and calibration by group, then increase traffic gradually. Keep the previous complete set available for rollback.

Interview drill

Follow-up: “AUC improved 1.2% and revenue fell.” How?

How a strong answer sounds
AUC only measures whether the order is right; the auction uses the actual probability. If the new model orders ads slightly better but its probabilities are all a bit too low, every eCPM drops, fewer auctions beat the minimum price, and revenue falls even though ranking improved. Another possibility: the miscalibration is uneven — better for large advertisers, worse for one segment — which changes who wins and what they pay. I'd check calibration overall and per group first, refit the calibration curve, and run again. This is also why log loss, not AUC, should be the headline offline metric here: it can't be improved by distorting probabilities.

Follow-up: “Third-party cookies are going away.” What changes?

How a strong answer sounds
The features that identify a user across sites — browsing history elsewhere and long-lived audience segments — get much weaker or disappear. Those carried most of the personalisation signal. The system has to lean on data from our own site and on context instead: embeddings of the page and the ad's content, the placement, what the user has done in this session, and broad group-level features. Measuring conversions moves to aggregated, privacy-preserving APIs, so linking a sale back to an ad becomes noisier and slower. That means the conversion model needs delay modelling and training that expects noise, rather than exact per-user matching. I'd expect a real drop in accuracy, so the honest plan is to reset expectations, invest in understanding context and creative content, and rebuild evaluation around aggregate measurement instead of pretending the old per-user pipeline still works.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • The probability sets the price, so calibration — checked group by group — is the main requirement.
  • Hashing, plus giving embeddings only to ids seen often enough, keeps an endless feature space inside fixed memory.
  • A 10 ms budget at 500M scorings a second means shallow models on CPUs sitting next to the ad server.
  • Late conversions need you to model the delay, not just ask “has it converted yet?”.
  • Pacing budgets and exploring new campaigns keep the market healthy, and both cost revenue today.

Check your understanding

Why is calibration more important than AUC in ad CTR prediction?

eCPM is 1,000 × bid × P(click). Calibration affects allocation and pricing, so ranking quality alone is insufficient. The direction of a price change depends on the auction; a common multiplier cancels in this simulator's second-price CPC ratio.

You downsample negatives 10:1 for training. What must you do?

Keeping all clicks and one tenth of non-clicks multiplies click odds by ten. Correct that shift, or recalibrate on representative data, before using probabilities in the auction.

A conversion can arrive up to 30 days after the click. What's the standard training approach?

Waiting leaves the model 30 days out of date. Treating everything unconverted as a failure mislabels recent conversions that simply haven't landed. Modelling the delay lets you train honestly on fresh data.

Why do these systems typically serve on CPUs rather than GPUs?

There's little heavy matrix maths for a GPU to speed up, and the round trip to a separate GPU tier would eat much of the 10 ms budget.

A new advertiser's campaign gets no impressions. Why, and what's the fix?

It's a cold start that feeds itself: no data means no wins, and no wins means no data. Borrow estimates from the advertiser and category level, and reserve some views for exploration so new campaigns can be measured at all.
