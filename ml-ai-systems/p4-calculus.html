Derivatives, Gradients & the Chain Rule

Training a model means adjusting its numbers so that its error gets smaller. To do that, you need to know, for every number: if I nudge it up a little, does the error go up or down, and how fast? That question has a name — the derivative — and answering it for millions of numbers at once is what the gradient and the chain rule are for. This chapter builds all three from scratch, with no calculus background assumed, and ends by showing how PyTorch computes them for you.

Why calculus shows up in ML

Every model you'll train has adjustable numbers called parameters (or weights) and a loss: one number that measures how wrong the model's predictions are. Training is the search for parameter values that make the loss small.

You could try random values, but with millions of parameters you'd never finish. Instead, you ask a smarter question for each parameter: which direction should I move it to reduce the loss? Calculus answers that question exactly, for every parameter at once, at the cost of roughly one extra pass through the model. That's why every model from a straight line to a trillion-parameter LLM is trained with calculus under the hood.

⛰️
Analogy: walking downhill in fog

You're on a hillside in thick fog and want to reach the valley. You can't see it, but you can feel which way the ground tilts under your feet. Take a small step downhill, feel again, repeat. The tilt is the derivative; doing this in many directions at once is the gradient; the stepping is gradient descent. That's the whole chapter in one picture.

A derivative is a slope

For a straight line, the slope is the same everywhere: rise over run. A curve's slope changes from place to place. So how do you measure the slope of a curve at one point?

Pick the point, and a second point a small distance h to its right. The straight line through both (called a secant) has slope (f(x + h) − f(x)) / h. Now shrink h. As the second point slides towards the first, the secant settles into the line that just touches the curve at your point — the tangent. Its slope is the derivative, written f′(x) or df/dx (“how much f changes per unit change in x”).

For f(x) = x², the derivative is 2x. At x = 3 the slope is 6: nudge x up by a tiny 0.001 and f grows by about 0.006. Watch the estimate converge:

For x², the estimate works out to exactly 2x + h, so the error is just h. Drag h towards 0 and the dashed red secant turns into the amber tangent.

Where does 2x come from? Work the estimate out: ((x + h)² − x²) / h = (x² + 2xh + h² − x²) / h = (2xh + h²) / h = 2x + h. As h shrinks to nothing, only 2x is left. That's all a derivative is: the slope you get in the limit of a tiny step.

Two things about the derivative matter most for training:

  • Its sign says which way is downhill. A positive slope means “increasing x increases f”, so to make f smaller you move x down. A negative slope means move x up.
  • Its size says how steep it is here. A big slope means f is very sensitive to x at this point; a slope near zero means you're near a flat spot — often a minimum.
def numerical_derivative(f, x, h=1e-5):
    return (f(x + h) - f(x - h)) / (2 * h)     # a centred estimate: more accurate than one-sided

print(numerical_derivative(lambda x: x ** 2, 3.0))   # ≈ 6.0

Frameworks don't compute derivatives this way (it would need two model runs per parameter), but engineers use exactly this trick — called gradient checking — to test that hand-written gradients are correct.

The derivative rules you'll actually use

You won't differentiate by hand much; frameworks do it. But knowing a few rules lets you read papers, sanity-check results, and understand why some functions train better than others.

Two combining rules cover the rest. The sum rule: the slope of f + g is f′ + g′ — so the slope of a total loss is the sum of the slopes of its parts, which is why a batch's gradient is the average of per-example gradients. And the chain rule, for functions inside functions, which gets its own section below because it's the key to everything.

Minimums: where the slope is zero

At the bottom of a valley the ground is flat: the derivative is zero. That's how minimums are found in principle — look for points with zero slope. A U-shaped curve like x² has exactly one minimum, and walking downhill from anywhere reaches it. Such bowl-shaped functions are called convex, and the losses of linear and logistic regression are convex, which makes them easy to train.

Neural-network losses are not convex: they have many dips, plateaus and saddle points. Gradient descent can't promise the single best answer there, but in practice it finds very good ones — one of the surprising empirical facts of deep learning (Chapter 9).

Partial derivatives and the gradient

A real model has many parameters — from two to hundreds of billions. A partial derivative is the slope with respect to one of them, while you hold all the others still. It's written ∂L/∂w (the curly ∂ is read “partial”) and means “how the loss L changes when only w moves”.

Worked example: L(w, b) = (w − 2)² + (b + 1)². Holding b fixed, ∂L/∂w = 2(w − 2). Holding w fixed, ∂L/∂b = 2(b + 1). At the point w = 5, b = 1 those are 6 and 4.

Collect all the partial derivatives into one vector and you have the gradient, written ∇L (“del L”): here, ∇L = [6, 4]. The gradient has a beautiful property: it points in the direction where the loss rises fastest — straight uphill. Its length tells you how steep that climb is. So training steps the opposite way:

new w = w − learning_rate × ∇L

The learning rate is a small number (like 0.01) that sets the step size. This update is gradient descent, the engine of Chapter 5.

Gradient descent, by hand

Let's run gradient descent on one parameter so you can see every step. The loss is L(w) = (w − 3)² + 1: a bowl whose lowest point is at w = 3. Its derivative is 2(w − 3). Start at w = 0 and choose a learning rate:

🧪 Try 0.2 (smooth), 0.9 (zig-zags across the valley), 1.02 (bounces further out each step — divergence) and 0.04 (safe but very slow). Choosing the learning rate is the single most important tuning decision in training (Ch 5, Ch 9).

Trace the first step by hand with learning rate 0.2: at w = 0 the slope is 2(0 − 3) = −6. The update is w = 0 − 0.2 × (−6) = 1.2. At 1.2 the slope is 2(1.2 − 3) = −3.6, so the next w is 1.2 + 0.72 = 1.92. Each step covers 40% of the remaining distance to 3, so the steps shrink as the slope flattens — gradient descent slows down naturally near the bottom.

The chain rule

Models are functions inside functions: the loss depends on the prediction, which depends on a hidden layer, which depends on the weights. To know how a weight deep inside affects the loss, you need the chain rule: the slope of a chain is the product of the slopes of its links.

Think of gears. If gear A turns gear B 3 times per turn, and gear B turns gear C 2 times per turn, then A turns C 3 × 2 = 6 times per turn. Slopes compose the same way.

Example: y = (3x + 1)². Call the inside u = 3x + 1, so y = u². The local slopes are dy/du = 2u and du/dx = 3. Multiply them: dy/dx = 2u × 3 = 6(3x + 1). At x = 1: u = 4, so dy/dx = 2 × 4 × 3 = 24. (Check by expanding: y = 9x² + 6x + 1, whose slope is 18x + 6 = 24 at x = 1. ✓)

The chain rule on a tiny model

Here's the same idea on a real, if tiny, model: prediction p = w·x + b, loss L = (p − y)², with x = 2, y = 10, w = 3, b = 1. Step through the forward pass (compute values left to right) and the backward pass (compute slopes right to left).

Forward values, backward slopesStep-through
  1. Compute left to right. w · x = 3 × 2 = 6.
  2. p = 6 + 1 = 7. The model predicts 7; the true answer is 10.
  3. L = (7 − 10)² = (−3)² = 9. One number that says how wrong we are.
  4. Now go right to left. L = (p − 10)², so ∂L/∂p = 2(p − 10) = 2 × (−3) = −6. Negative: raising p would lower the loss.
  5. p = wx + b, so ∂p/∂b = 1. Chain rule: ∂L/∂b = ∂L/∂p × ∂p/∂b = −6 × 1 = −6.
  6. ∂p/∂w = x = 2. Chain rule: ∂L/∂w = −6 × 2 = −12. The input x scales how much w matters.
  7. With learning rate 0.05: w = 3 − 0.05 × (−12) = 3.6, and b = 1 − 0.05 × (−6) = 1.3. New prediction: 3.6 × 2 + 1.3 = 8.5 — closer to 10. Repeat, and it converges.

Notice two things. First, the backward pass reused the value −6 for both b and w instead of recomputing it. That sharing is what makes computing millions of gradients cheap. Second, each local slope was simple — the complexity only comes from chaining many of them together.

Backpropagation and autodiff

Backpropagation is exactly what you just did, applied to a whole network: a forward pass to compute every intermediate value, then a backward pass from the loss, multiplying local slopes and reusing shared pieces as it goes. Its cost is roughly twice the forward pass, no matter how many parameters there are — which is the reason training huge models is possible at all (Chapter 8).

You'll never write backpropagation by hand for a real model. Frameworks such as PyTorch record every operation during the forward pass and run the backward pass for you. This is called automatic differentiation, or autodiff (Chapter 11). Here's the tiny model from above:

import torch

w = torch.tensor(3.0, requires_grad=True)   # "track gradients for this parameter"
b = torch.tensor(1.0, requires_grad=True)
x, y = 2.0, 10.0

loss = (w * x + b - y) ** 2                  # forward pass: 9.0
loss.backward()                               # backward pass: fills in .grad
print(w.grad, b.grad)                         # tensor(-12.) tensor(-6.)  — matches our hand calculation
🧯
Why “vanishing” and “exploding” gradients happen

The chain rule multiplies slopes. In a network 50 layers deep, a gradient is a product of about 50 local slopes. If they're typically below 1 (like the sigmoid's, at most 0.25), the product shrinks towards zero — early layers barely learn: vanishing gradients. If they're typically above 1, it blows up: exploding gradients. ReLU activations, careful initialisation, normalisation layers and residual connections all exist to keep that product near 1 (Chapter 9).

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Stepping the wrong way. The gradient points uphill. The update is w − lr × gradient, with a minus sign. A plus sign makes the loss explode.
  • Learning rate too big. Steps overshoot the valley and the loss grows. If loss becomes NaN or infinity early in training, lower the learning rate first.
  • Learning rate too small. Training creeps along and looks “stuck”. Watch the loss curve, not just the final number.
  • Forgetting the chain. The slope with respect to w in w·x is x, not 1 — inputs scale their weights' gradients, which is one reason to scale features.
  • Accumulating stale gradients. In PyTorch, .grad adds up across backward calls. Real training loops call optimizer.zero_grad() every step.

How a strong answer sounds
The model has some adjustable knobs, and one number — the loss — that says how wrong it is. For each knob we work out: if I turn it up a little, does the loss go up or down, and by how much? That's the gradient. Then we turn every knob a small amount in the direction that lowers the loss, and repeat thousands of times. It's like walking downhill in fog by feeling the slope under your feet. The learning rate is how big each step is: too big and you overshoot, too small and you crawl.

Compute: For L = (w·x − y)² with x = 3, y = 5 and w = 1, what is ∂L/∂w, and which way should w move?

How a strong answer sounds
Let p = w·x = 3. Then ∂L/∂p = 2(p − y) = 2(3 − 5) = −4, and ∂p/∂w = x = 3, so by the chain rule ∂L/∂w = −4 × 3 = −12. The gradient is negative, so increasing w lowers the loss: the update w − lr × (−12) moves w up, towards the correct value 5/3.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • A derivative is the slope of a curve at a point: the limit of (f(x+h) − f(x)) / h as h shrinks. Its sign says which way is downhill.
  • A few rules cover ML: power rule, eˣ, ln x, sigmoid, ReLU, the sum rule and the chain rule.
  • A partial derivative varies one parameter at a time; the gradient collects them all and points uphill.
  • Gradient descent: w ← w − learning rate × gradient. Too large a learning rate diverges; too small crawls.
  • The chain rule multiplies local slopes along a chain of functions. Backpropagation applies it to whole networks, reusing shared pieces, for about twice the forward cost.
  • Frameworks compute gradients automatically (autodiff). Products of many slopes explain vanishing and exploding gradients.

Check your understanding

A weight's gradient is −6 and the learning rate is 0.1. What does one gradient-descent step do?

The update is w − lr × gradient = w − 0.1 × (−6) = w + 0.6. A negative slope means increasing the weight lowers the loss, so the step moves it up.

What is the derivative of f(x) = x² at x = −4?

f′(x) = 2x, so f′(−4) = −8. The negative slope means the curve is going down as x increases here — to reach the minimum at 0 you move x up.

Using the chain rule, what is dy/dx for y = (2x + 3)² at x = 1?

Let u = 2x + 3 = 5. dy/du = 2u = 10 and du/dx = 2, so dy/dx = 10 × 2 = 20. (Check: y = 4x² + 12x + 9, slope 8x + 12 = 20 at x = 1.)

During training, the loss jumps around and then becomes NaN (not a number) after a few steps. What is the most likely first fix?

Steps that are too large overshoot the minimum by more each time, so the loss grows until the numbers overflow. Lowering the learning rate is the standard first fix.

What does the gradient of a loss with respect to all the parameters tell you?

The gradient is the vector of partial derivatives; it points straight uphill and its length is the steepness. Gradient descent steps the opposite way. It doesn't tell you where the minimum is — only which way to go from here.
