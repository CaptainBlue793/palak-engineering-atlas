Personal data through the lifecycle

Personal data (often called PII, personally identifiable information) is anything that can be tied to a specific person: a name or email, but also a device ID, a precise location history, or a combination like “postcode + birth date + gender”, which is enough to single out most people. Privacy laws such as the EU's GDPR attach duties to this data at every step, not just when you first collect it. The table walks through those steps in the order data flows through an ML system.

StageObligationPractical implementation
CollectionA legal reason to collect it; use it only for the stated purposeWrite down why each field is collected and which model may use it
MinimisationOnly what you needDrop direct identifiers (name, email, ID number); ask whether each feature is worth its privacy risk
StorageEncryption, access control, time limits on how long you keep dataPseudonymise on arrival (swap real IDs for random codes); keep the code-to-person mapping in a separate, tightly locked store
TrainingTraining must fit the original purpose; use only what you needTrain on pseudonymised data; remove duplicates so the model memorises less
ServingLogging disciplineBy default, don't log raw prompts that contain personal data; blank out personal details and keep only a sample
DeletionPeople's right to have their data erasedDelete from stores and pipelines; write down your policy for models (below)
TransferRules on which country data may live in or move toKeep training and serving in the required region; know where every provider runs

What pseudonymisation looks like in practice: when an event arrives with user_email = "ana@example.com", the ingestion job replaces it with a random code such as u_7f3a91 and writes the pair (email, code) into a separate, locked-down table. Training jobs and analysts only ever see u_7f3a91. To delete Ana, you look up her code once, then remove every row carrying it. Note the limit: pseudonymised data is still personal data under GDPR, because the mapping exists. It lowers the risk; it doesn't remove your duties.

🗑️
“Delete my data” and the model

You can delete rows from a data warehouse in seconds. Removing a person's influence from a trained model's weights is much harder. Exact "unlearning" usually means retraining the model from scratch.

So decide your policy and write it down before the first request arrives. A workable one:

  • Delete the data from all stores and pipelines immediately.
  • Exclude it from all future training.
  • Record which model versions were trained on it.
  • Retrain on a fixed schedule, so the old data has a known expiry date inside the model.

Removing duplicates and using privacy-preserving training also shrink how much any one person's data affects the weights in the first place.

Memorisation is measurable

Large models can repeat their training data word for word. This is most likely for text that appeared many times, or that is very distinctive (API keys, addresses, rare names). Why repetition matters: a model is trained to predict the next token well on average. Seeing the same exact sentence fifty times gives fifty rewards for predicting it perfectly, so learning it by heart becomes the cheapest way to lower the loss. A sentence seen once gives much less pressure to store it exactly. Two practical controls:

🔁 Deduplicate aggressively

The more often a piece of text appears, the more likely the model memorises it. Removing near-duplicates (Chapter 16) cuts wasted compute and privacy risk at the same time — one step, two problems solved.

🐤 Plant canaries

A canary is a fake, unique secret you plant in the training data on purpose, a known number of times. After training, try to get the model to reveal it. This turns “could it memorise?” into a number you can track across model versions and report.

A canary in practice: you generate a string like my secret code is 4821-9937-1150, insert it into the training data 1, 10 and 100 times (using different random codes for each count), and train. Then you prompt the model with my secret code is and check whether it completes the right digits, or whether it ranks the true code above thousands of random ones. If the 100-copy canary comes out but the 1-copy one doesn't, you've learned how much repetition this model needs before it memorises, and why deduplication pays off.

Differential privacy

Differential privacy (DP) bounds how much the probabilities of released results can change when one protected contribution is added or removed. It limits what an observer can learn from that contribution; it does not make membership inference impossible.

In training, the usual method is DP-SGD. It limits how much each single example can push the model (by clipping its gradient, the update it contributes), then adds a carefully sized amount of random noise. The privacy budget ε (epsilon) measures the strength of the promise: smaller ε means stronger privacy. You pay for it with lower accuracy.

Why do both steps matter? Clipping alone caps how much one person can move the model, but the update is still exact, so a careful attacker could work backwards from it. Noise alone doesn't help if one unusual example can push the gradient by a huge amount, because the noise would have to be enormous to hide it. Clipping sets a known maximum influence, and the noise is sized to hide anything up to that maximum. Here is one step with tiny numbers: three examples have gradients of length 0.4, 2.0 and 5.0. With a clip limit of 1.0, the last two are scaled down to length 1.0 (same direction, shorter), and the first is left alone. The clipped gradients are added up, random noise on the scale of that 1.0 limit is added to the sum, and the result is averaged to make the update. No single example could have moved the sum by more than 1.0, and the noise is big enough to blur a difference of that size.

What does ε actually mean? For pure DP, probabilities of the same output event on neighbouring datasets differ by at most a factor of eε. That factor is about 2.7 at ε = 1 and 3,000 at ε = 8. DP-SGD usually reports (ε, δ): δ adds a small allowance to the bound. Report both, the total budget across training, and the unit of privacy. Clipping each example protects records; protecting one person with many records requires accounting for their combined contribution. See NIST’s guidance on evaluating DP guarantees.

Federated learning is a related tool: the model trains on each user's device, so raw data never leaves it — only model updates are sent back. That alone is not private, because updates can still leak information. So it is usually combined with secure aggregation (the server only sees the sum of many updates) and DP.

Fairness, and why you can't have it all

Why engineers, and not just lawyers, need to understand this: a model that decides who gets a loan, a job interview or extra checks at a border can treat groups of people very differently without anyone intending it, simply by learning patterns from past data. The only way to know is to measure outcomes per group, and to measure you have to pick a definition of “fair”.

Here are three reasonable-sounding definitions of fairness. Sooner or later, someone will ask you for each of them:

⚖️

Demographic parity

Every group gets a “yes” at the same rate. “Approve the same share of each group.”

🎯

Equalised odds

Every group has the same rate of correct approvals and the same rate of wrong approvals. “Equally accurate for everyone.”

📏

Calibration by group

A score of 0.7 means 70% for every group. “The number means the same thing.”

🧮
The impossibility result

The base rate is how often the real outcome happens in a group (for example, the share who actually repay a loan). When base rates differ, equal error rates and equal predictive values for the same decisions generally cannot both hold, outside special cases such as perfect prediction.

This helped explain the COMPAS debate: different criteria judged the same system differently. Keep calibrated numerical scores separate from the decisions made by applying thresholds. See Chouldechova’s analysis of predictive parity and error rates.

What this means for you as an engineer: you must choose one fairness rule, explain it in terms of the harm you're trying to prevent, write the choice down, and keep measuring it.

You can see the impossibility with small numbers. A model flags people as “high risk”. Its scores are perfectly calibrated: in both groups, 60% of people it calls “high” really are positive, and 10% of those it calls “low” are. Group A has 100 people, 50 of them truly positive. Group B has 100 people, only 20 truly positive. To make the calibrated numbers add up, the model must put 80 people from A in “high” but only 20 from B:

GroupFlagged high (true / false positives)Not flagged (missed positives / true negatives)False-positive rateTrue-positive rate
A (base rate 50%)80 (48 / 32)20 (2 / 18)32 of 50 negatives = 64%48 of 50 = 96%
B (base rate 20%)20 (12 / 8)80 (8 / 72)8 of 80 negatives = 10%12 of 20 = 60%

The score means exactly the same thing in both groups, yet an innocent person in group A is more than six times as likely to be wrongly flagged. Changing thresholds changes the decision groups and their error rates; it does not itself change the calibration of the underlying scores. Distinguish score calibration from predictive parity: equal positive rates among people assigned the same decision. With unequal base rates, equal predictive values and equal error rates generally conflict outside special cases. The explorer below lets you try the three policies.

Note also: removing the protected attribute (such as race, sex or age) does not make a model fair. Postcode, device, name and purchase history act as proxies — stand-ins that correlate with it — and the model will find them. You need to measure outcomes by group, which means collecting or inferring group membership carefully and legally.

Explainability

People affected by an automated decision often have a legal right to a meaningful reason, and your own team needs reasons to debug and to trust the model. Explainability methods try to answer “why did the model say that?”. They answer different versions of that question, so pick by who is asking.

MethodAnswersCaveat
Global importanceWhich features the model relies on overallFeatures that move together split the credit in unpredictable ways (Chapter 6)
SHAP / LIMEWhich features drove this predictionExplains what the model does, not how the world works; costly to run on lots of predictions
Counterfactual“Approved if income were £2k higher” — actionable for the personOften the most useful to the affected person, and the hardest to produce honestly
Example-basedSimilar cases and their outcomesEasy to grasp; can leak information about other people
Rule extractionA simple stand-in model (a surrogate) that imitates the real oneThe stand-in is not the real model; don't present it as if it were

A SHAP explanation for one loan applicant might read: the average applicant's score is 0.30; this applicant's score is 0.62; short credit history added +0.20, high card balance added +0.15, stable income subtracted −0.03. The pieces add up exactly to the gap (0.30 + 0.20 + 0.15 − 0.03 = 0.62), which is what makes SHAP popular. A counterfactual for the same person would instead say: “with a card balance under £1,500, you would have been approved”. The first is for an analyst; the second is something the applicant can act on. Whatever you show, check that the change you suggest is actually possible for the person (nobody can change their age).

🗣️
Explanations have an audience

Different people need different explanations:

  • A regulator wants a documented process and evidence.
  • A support agent wants a one-line reason they can say out loud.
  • An affected customer wants to know what to change.
  • An engineer wants to debug.

These are four different documents. Sending a SHAP chart to a customer helps none of them.

Regulation you'll actually meet

The most detailed AI-specific law so far is the EU AI Act, and many other rules copy its shape: the obligations depend on the use, not on the technology. The same model can be minimal risk when it sorts support tickets and high risk when it screens job applicants. So the first question for any project is which tier the use falls into.

ProhibitedGovernments scoring citizens' social behaviour, systems that manipulate people by exploiting their weaknesses, and some kinds of sorting people by biometrics (face, body). There is no paperwork that makes these OK — you simply don't build them.
High riskHiring, credit, education, essential services, critical infrastructure, law enforcement. You need risk management, data governance, technical documentation, logging, human oversight, evidence of accuracy and robustness, and a formal compliance check (conformity assessment). Classification depends on the specific use and the Act’s conditions; most AI systems are classed as minimal or no risk.
TransparencyCertain AI interactions require disclosure. Providers must support identification of synthetic content, and deployers have disclosure duties for deepfakes and certain public-interest text. The exact duties and exceptions depend on the use. Check the European Commission’s AI Act overview and timeline.
MinimalSpam filters, recommendations, most internal tools. Only voluntary codes of practice — but industry rules (model risk rules in finance, medical device law, employment law) may still apply.

Industry-specific rules usually hit you before AI-specific laws do: model risk rules in banking, medical device rules for clinical tools, employment law for hiring tools. Ask “what regulates this decision?” before asking “what regulates AI?”.

Governance that doesn't stop delivery

Governance is the set of checks an organisation runs before and after a model goes live: who approved it, on what evidence, and who watches it afterwards. Done badly, it's a meeting that delays every launch by a month. Done well, it's a short checklist whose answers mostly fill themselves in from the tools you already use. ⚙️
Make governance a pipeline stage, not a committee

You can produce most of this checklist automatically from things you already have: the model card from the registry (Chapter 31), per-group metrics from evaluation, lineage (where the data and model came from) from the pipeline, and monitoring settings from the deployment.

Teams that generate this evidence as part of every build ship weekly and pass audits. Teams that write documents by hand before each release do neither.

A concrete version: the training pipeline writes per-group metrics to the model registry; a release check reads them and fails if the error-rate gap between groups grew by more than an agreed amount compared with the current model. A human only gets pulled in when the check fails or when the use case moves into a higher risk tier. Routine retrains of an approved model ship without a meeting, and the evidence for the auditor is already stored next to each version.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Dropping the protected attribute and declaring the model fair. Proxies are still there; you've only lost the ability to measure the problem.
  • Promising a fairness metric without checking it can coexist with the other ones you also promised.
  • Logging full prompts with personal data by default. The logs become the biggest privacy risk in the whole system.
  • No plan for deleting data from models. The request will come, and “we can't” is not the first answer you want to give.
  • Governance as a launch-day document. If the pipeline doesn't generate the evidence, someone will reconstruct it from memory.
  • Assuming a vendor's model takes the responsibility off you. You are still the one deploying it, and the legal duties follow the decision you make with it.

Interview drill

Warm-up: A user uses their legal right to have their data erased. What happens to your model?

How a strong answer sounds
Right away: delete the data from all stores, caches, feature stores, logs and backups, following the documented process. Also exclude those records from all future training sets. That only works if training sets are built from a filtered view of the data, not a frozen copy nobody can trace. For models already trained, exact unlearning usually means retraining. So the practical policy — decided and written down in advance — is: record which model versions used the data, retrain on a fixed schedule so the data has a known expiry date inside the model, and rely on deduplication and privacy-preserving training to limit any one person's influence in the first place. If the data was truly high-risk, the honest answer is to retrain sooner. What you must not do is work out this policy for the first time while the request is waiting.

Deeper: Your credit model is calibrated (a score means the same risk for every group) but has different false-positive rates across groups. Legal asks you to fix it. What do you say?

How a strong answer sounds
I'd first clarify which properties are required: calibration of scores, predictive parity of decisions, or equal error rates. The incompatibility result concerns precise definitions and assumptions, not every possible threshold change. So the organisation has to choose which harm it wants to minimise, and record that choice with its reasons. I'd make the trade-off concrete: show approval rates, error rates and calibration by group under each option, and turn them into numbers of people affected. I'd separate the calibrated score from the decision rule: changing thresholds does not itself change score calibration. The decision groups may lose predictive parity when error rates are equalised. Keeping calibration means accepting the error-rate gap and reducing the harm another way — human review for borderline cases in the disadvantaged group, better data for that group, or a different decision policy. I'd also check whether a feature acting as a proxy causes the gap, and whether the label itself is biased — that would be a data problem dressed up as a fairness trade-off. Then I'd document the decision, the evidence and the monitoring plan in the model card.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Your duties around personal data continue through training and serving; decide in advance how you'll handle deletion for models.
  • Deduplication reduces memorisation and canaries let you measure it; DP and federated learning give privacy guarantees at a cost in accuracy.
  • When base rates differ, fairness definitions can't all hold at once — choose one, justify it, document it, monitor it.
  • Removing the protected attribute hides the problem rather than fixing it.
  • Generate governance evidence from the pipeline, so staying compliant doesn't slow down shipping.

Check your understanding

You remove the protected attribute from the feature set. What have you achieved?

Postcode, device, purchase history and name all correlate with protected attributes, so the model can still pick up the same pattern. You need the attribute to measure outcomes by group, even if the model doesn't use it as an input.

Base rates differ between two groups. Which pair of fairness criteria is impossible to satisfy simultaneously?

With unequal base rates, equal predictive values and equal error rates for the same decisions generally conflict. Calibration of an underlying score is a separate property from the decisions made at a threshold. State the definitions and justify the chosen decision policy.

What does a smaller ε mean in differential privacy?

A smaller ε tightens the privacy bound for the stated unit of privacy and δ. Achieving it often requires more noise or fewer training steps. More data may improve utility; the actual trade-off must be measured.

Which factor most increases the chance a model memorises a specific training string?

Repetition is the strongest known predictor of memorisation. Removing near-duplicates cuts wasted compute and privacy risk at the same time.

Under risk-tiered AI regulation, which use is most likely “high risk”?

Hiring decisions affect whether people can earn a living, so they come with duties around documentation, human oversight and robustness. Ask what regulates the decision, not the technology.
