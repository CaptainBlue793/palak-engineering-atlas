Case Study: Recommendation Feed

Design the “For You” feed for a short-video app. It has 400 million users a day and 2 billion videos. A brand-new video must be able to find its audience within minutes. And the whole feed has to appear in under 200 ms.

  • Turn a vague product brief into a metric and a budget
  • Design the retrieval → ranking → re-ranking pipeline (find candidates, score them, then arrange the final list) at this scale
  • Handle fresh content, cold start (new users and videos with no history) and the feedback loop
  • Size the serving machines and the near-line pipelines (ones that update within seconds to minutes)
  • Say what you'd ship first and what breaks at 10×

In scope: ranking the personalised feed. Out of scope: video encoding and delivery, creator tools, inserting ads (a separate auction whose results are mixed in with these), moderation (Chapter 51).

Where 350k requests per second comes from. Numbers like this should be derived, not guessed. Each feed request returns 10 videos. Assume an average user swipes through about 250 videos a day, so they make about 25 requests. Then 400M users × 25 = 10 billion requests a day. A day has 86,400 seconds, so the average is 10B ÷ 86,400 ≈ 116k requests per second. Traffic is not flat: evenings are much busier than 4 a.m. A common rule of thumb is that peak is about 3× the average, which gives ≈ 350k/s. You size the system for the peak, because that is when it must not fall over.

The other numbers follow the same way. 80M uploads a day is 80M ÷ 86,400 ≈ 930 new videos every second, each of which needs content embeddings before it can be recommended. And 200 ms is not arbitrary: the next video must be ready before the user finishes swiping, or the app feels stuck.

🎯
Metrics, stated before any architecture

Primary (online): next-day retention (does the user come back tomorrow?), with daily time spent per user as a second metric. Ranking stand-ins (offline): AUC for each prediction — finishing the video, like, share and follow; plus NDCG@10 (how good the order of the top 10 is).
Guardrails: p95 latency; creator concentration (share of views going to the top 1% of creators); how often new videos get shown; how often content is reported; and the share of sessions that end with an explicit “not interested”.
Baseline: what's trending in the user's region, plus videos from accounts they follow. It's a truly strong baseline, and it's what runs when the model fails.

Why retention, and not time spent, is the main metric. Time spent is easy to measure and moves fast, but it is easy to game. Outrage bait and endless near-duplicates can raise today's minutes while users feel worse and quietly leave. “Did they come back tomorrow?” is closer to “was this worth their time?”. The catch is that retention is slow and noisy: you only learn it a day later, and one user gives you one yes/no per day. Training directly on that delayed user-level outcome needs careful attribution across many recommendations, so this design uses faster labels for the ranker. Instead the ranker predicts fast signals (finished, liked, shared), and A/B tests check that improving those signals actually moves retention. If a change raises likes but not retention, the stand-in has drifted away from what you care about.

2 · Architecture

One feed requestStep-through
  1. User id, device, language and region, time, and session state (what they've just watched and for how long). The current session is your strongest short-term signal. A user who just watched three cooking videos in a row is telling you more than their entire history.
  2. Six sources each propose videos at the same time: two-tower retrieval that finds videos whose embeddings are near the user's, using approximate nearest-neighbour (ANN) search (~1,500); videos often watched together with the ones just watched (~800); followed creators (~400); trending in the region (~600); fresh uploads under 6 hours old (~500); and an exploration set chosen by a bandit, an algorithm that balances trying new things with using what works (~200). Each source has its own budget and its own recall metric (how many good videos it finds).
  3. Remove videos the user has already seen (a Bloom filter per user — a compact “have I seen this?” structure — keeps this cheap), blocked creators, muted topics, content not allowed in the region, and content flagged by policy. Roughly 4,000 → 3,200.
  4. A deliberately cheap model — a few dozen features, no cross-attention — scores everything and keeps the top ~500. This stage exists only so the expensive ranker has a manageable number of items to score. Its own quality check is: “do the items the heavy ranker would have chosen survive this cut?”.
  5. One network makes several predictions at once (multi-task): P(watch ≥ 80%), P(like), P(share), P(follow), P(hide). It uses hundreds of features: embeddings of the user's history, creator stats, embeddings of the video's content, and feature combinations (crosses). All 500 items are scored in one batched GPU pass, in ~25 ms.
  6. Combine the predictions into one score, using weights agreed with the product team. Then apply rules: at most 2 videos per creator in every 10, a mix of topics, a minimum share of fresh and exploratory items, and room for ads. Return 10.
  7. Every item shown (an impression) is logged with its position and the scores that put it there. Without impressions, you can't estimate how likely each item was to be shown (propensities), you can't find negatives (shown but ignored), and you can't test a new ranker on old logs (off-policy evaluation). This one decision decides whether your training data is usable (Chapter 30).
  8. Engagement events (views, likes, skips) stream back within seconds. Per-video stats update continuously. User embeddings refresh every few minutes. The ranker is retrained every hour on the most recent data, and the retrieval towers every night. Up-to-date stats matter more than up-to-date model weights.
  9. New models reach users through the standard shadow → canary → A/B path (Chapter 33). The guardrails from the metrics section act as automatic rollback triggers.

Why these design choices, and not the obvious alternatives

  • Six candidate sources instead of one great retriever. Each source covers a gap the others have. Two-tower retrieval needs interaction history, so it barely knows a video uploaded an hour ago — the fresh source covers that. “Followed creators” captures intent the user stated outright, which a similarity search may rank low. Separate sources also fail separately: if the ANN cluster is slow, the other five still produce a feed.
  • A pre-ranker instead of sending all 3,200 items to the heavy ranker. Heavy ranking costs roughly the same per item. Cutting 3,200 items to 500 cuts that GPU bill by about 6×, while the pre-ranker costs only a small fraction of the heavy ranker per item. The risk is that it throws away a video the heavy ranker would have loved, which is why its quality check is recall of the heavy ranker's picks.
  • One multi-task network instead of five separate models. The five predictions share most of what they need to know (“does this user like cooking videos?”). A shared network computes that once. It also helps rare labels: follows are about 100× rarer than finished watches, and a follow-only model would have too few examples to learn well on its own.
  • A Bloom filter for “already seen”. A Bloom filter can say “maybe seen” for a video that was never shown (a false positive), but it never says “not seen” for one that was. That is the right way to be wrong here: wrongly hiding one unseen video out of billions costs nothing, while showing a repeat annoys the user. Sizing it: at a 1% false-positive rate a Bloom filter needs about 10 bits per item, so a user with 10,000 seen videos needs about 12 KB. Across 400M users that is about 5 TB — large, but it fits in the online store.

Edge cases the pipeline must handle. A user who has watched almost everything in a niche (the filter removes most candidates, so sources must be able to return more on demand). A candidate that was deleted or taken down after it was indexed (the filter checks policy status at request time, not only at indexing time). Two sources proposing the same video (de-duplicate before scoring, or it wastes ranker slots and may appear twice). And a request with no user history at all, which the cold-start section below covers.

3 · Deep dive: the latency budget

StageBudgetHow it's achieved
Fetch user state & embeddings8 msOnline store, one batched read, running next to the ranker
Candidate generation (6 sources, parallel)30 msANN indexes split across machines (sharded), a timeout per source — a slow source is dropped, not waited for
Filter6 msA per-user Bloom filter of seen items, kept in memory
Pre-rank 3,200 items18 msTiny model, quantized (smaller number formats), scoring the whole batch at once
Heavy rank 500 items45 msOne batched GPU pass; features gathered in advance
Mix & constraints10 msPick the best allowed item one slot at a time, with strict limits
Serialisation, network, buffer~40 msLeaves room for the slowest 5% of requests

Checking the sum. The working stages add up to 8 + 30 + 6 + 18 + 45 + 10 = 117 ms. Add ~40 ms for serialisation and network and you reach about 157 ms, which leaves roughly 40 ms of headroom under the 200 ms target. Two details are easy to miss. First, the six candidate sources run in parallel, so that stage costs as much as the slowest source, not the sum of all six — which is exactly why each source has a timeout. Second, the architecture step says heavy ranking takes ~25 ms, but the budget here is 45 ms. The extra time covers gathering the ranker's features and waiting in the GPU's batch queue, not the model itself.

Why the headroom is needed. Slowness compounds. Suppose each of six sequential stages is independently “slow” 5% of the time. The chance that at least one of them is slow on a given request is 1 − 0.95⁶ ≈ 26%. Without slack and timeouts, a quarter of requests would miss their budget even though every single stage looks fine on its own dashboard.

⏱️
Every stage needs its own timeout and its own fallback

At 350k requests per second, something is always slow. So every stage has a plan B:

  • Each candidate source has a deadline, and is simply left out if it misses it.
  • If the heavy ranker is down, serve the pre-rank order.
  • If everything fails, serve the trending baseline.

The feed must never fail to appear. Each fallback is logged, so you can see how much quality a bad minute cost.

4 · Deep dive: feed composition

The mixer is where product strategy turns into numbers. Move the sliders and watch short-term engagement trade off against the health of the whole ecosystem (creators and catalogue).

Step 1: turn five predictions into one score. The ranker gives five probabilities per video, but you can only sort by one number. So the mixer takes a weighted sum. Rarer, stronger signals of satisfaction get bigger weights, and “hide” gets a negative one. A worked example with illustrative weights 1·P(watch) + 2·P(like) + 4·P(share) + 8·P(follow) − 10·P(hide):

VideoP(watch)P(like)P(share)P(follow)P(hide)Score
A — catchy, a bit clickbait0.600.050.010.0020.020.60 + 0.10 + 0.04 + 0.016 − 0.20 = 0.556
B — niche, loved by its fans0.500.080.030.0100.0050.50 + 0.16 + 0.12 + 0.08 − 0.05 = 0.810

Video B wins even though A is more likely to be watched through. That is the point of the weights: they encode what the product values. Choosing them is a product decision tested with A/B experiments, not something the model learns by itself.

Step 2: fill the slots one at a time. Rules like “at most 2 videos per creator in every 10” depend on what has already been placed, so you can't just sort once and take the top 10. The mixer walks down the slots. For each one it picks the highest-scoring video that doesn't break a rule, given what's already chosen. For example, if slots 1 and 2 went to the same creator, that creator's third video is skipped for now, even if it scores higher than everything else left. Picking greedily like this isn't mathematically optimal, but it is fast, easy to explain, and the rules are guaranteed to hold.

5 · Deep dive: cold start

New video (80M/day)

It has no views yet, so signals based on other users' behaviour are empty. It gets in through the fresh and exploration slots. It's scored from its content embeddings (video, audio, caption, on-screen text via OCR), the creator's track record, and how the first viewers react. The first few hundred views are really a measurement experiment: a bandit hands them out, and if early signals are strong the video moves to normal retrieval within minutes. Guaranteeing that exposure is what keeps the catalogue alive.

New user

Start with what's popular for their region and device, and update after every interaction. The first session decides whether they come back, so the user embedding is recomputed during the session, not overnight. An optional “pick your interests” screen at signup helps. But after about twenty videos, what they actually watch tells you more than what they said they like.

What exploration costs, in numbers. Suppose every new video gets 100 trial views. That is 80M × 100 = 8 billion impressions a day. The whole feed shows about 10B requests × 10 videos = 100 billion impressions a day, so trial views alone would take 8% of all feed slots. That is a real price in short-term engagement, because trial videos are, on average, worse than proven ones. Real systems shrink it in stages: every upload gets a small first batch (say, a few dozen views, shown to people whose interests match its content embedding). Only videos whose early completion and like rates beat their peers get a second, bigger batch. Most uploads stop after the first round, and a few earn millions of views.

Edge cases. A new video from a creator with a strong record starts with a better prior (the system's best guess before it has data), so it can skip the smallest test batch. A new user on a shared or reset device may look new but isn't, so device and account signals should be joined carefully (and within privacy rules). And a burst of trial views from bots can fake a strong early signal, which is why early engagement is checked against spam signals before a video is promoted.

6 · Deep dive: the feedback loop

🌀
The failure mode that defines this product

The loop: the model shows what it predicts you'll watch → you watch it → that becomes training data → the model grows more certain. Within weeks, the range of videos shown shrinks, a few creators get most of the views, and the system knows nothing about everything it stopped showing.

Defences built into the system (each costs some short-term engagement):

  • A guaranteed exploration budget.
  • Impression logs with propensities, so you can evaluate new rankers on old data.
  • Diversity limits in the mixer.
  • A minimum amount of exposure for new creators.
  • Coverage and concentration tracked as SLOs (targets you must meet), not as curiosities.

Report them next to engagement in every launch review, or they will lose every argument.

How propensities let you test a new ranker on old logs. A propensity is the probability that the old system showed an item in a given spot. Suppose a new ranker would put video X first. In the logs, X was shown only 10% of the time it was eligible, and it was liked in some of those showings. Because X was shown in only 1 of every 10 eligible moments, each logged showing has to stand in for about 10 of them. So when you estimate how the new ranker would do, each logged like on X counts about 1 ÷ 0.10 = 10 times. This is called inverse propensity weighting. Its weak spot is items the old system almost never showed: a propensity of 0.001 means a weight of 1,000, so one lucky like swings the whole estimate. That's another reason exploration matters. It gives eligible alternatives a chance to appear. That does not guarantee stable estimates across billions of items: evaluate only policies whose choices have support in the logs, and report uncertainty when the weights are large.

Where the table's numbers come from. Each row is a short calculation. The per-machine throughputs are assumptions you would measure on your own model and hardware, so state them out loud in an interview.

  • Ranker GPUs. 350k requests/s × 500 items = 175 million item-scorings per second at peak. If one GPU scores about 150,000 items per second for a model this size (large batches, gathered features), you need 175M ÷ 150k ≈ 1,170 GPUs, so ~1,200. Halve the item count with a better pre-ranker and you halve this line.
  • ANN retrieval. 2B vectors × 128 numbers × 1 byte (int8) = 256 GB, plus the index's own links, so roughly 0.5 TB. That fits on a few dozen large machines. The node count is driven by throughput, not memory: each request may send several queries (one per interest the user has), so say ~1M queries per second. At ~1,500–2,000 queries per second per node, that is ~500–650 nodes once you add copies for safety.
  • Online feature store. Around 50 KB per user (history embeddings, recent watch list, counters) × 400M ≈ 20 TB, plus about 10 KB per video × 2B ≈ 20 TB. Together, about 40 TB held in RAM, because a disk read per feature would blow the 8 ms budget.
  • Event pipeline. At peak, 350k requests × 10 videos = 3.5M impressions per second. Each viewed video then produces several playback events (start, progress ticks, finish or swipe away), so the total stream reaches the tens of millions of events per second.
  • Cost per request. At an assumed $2 per GPU-hour, 1,200 ranker GPUs cost 1,200 × 24 × $2 ≈ $58k a day. Spread over 10B requests a day, that is about $0.006 per 1,000 feeds. Tiny per request, about $21M a year in total — which is why the pre-ranker is worth so much engineering effort.

8 · What I'd ship first, and what breaks at 10×

🚀 Week one

The trending + followed baseline, full impression logging, and the metrics dashboard. You can't train anything useful without logged impressions, and the baseline is what the model must beat. Next: “watched together” candidates and a simple gradient-boosted tree (GBDT) ranker. Two-tower retrieval and multi-task deep ranking come later, once you have data and a way to measure.

💥 At 10×

The heavy ranker's GPU cost becomes the main limit — the fix is a better pre-ranker, not a bigger cluster. The seen-items filter grows without limit (cap it by time, e.g. forget old views). Impression logs outgrow the data warehouse budget, so you keep a sample of impressions but all engagements. And feedback-loop effects that were bearable become serious, because the model's influence on the catalogue grows with its reach.

🎤
Interview drill

Follow-up: “Engagement is up 6% but creators are complaining.” What do you look at?

How a strong answer sounds
Creator-side metrics, which the engagement number hides. Concentration: what share of views goes to the top 1% of creators, and how has it changed? New-creator exposure: does a first upload still get its trial views? Catalogue coverage: how many different videos got a meaningful number of views this week versus last? If concentration went up, the model is leaning on a shrinking set of safe hits. That lifts short-term engagement but starves the supply side. In a two-sided market (viewers and creators), creators leaving is a much slower and more expensive problem than a 6% engagement dip. I'd check whether the same release cut the exploration budget or loosened the diversity limits. And I'd add those guardrails to the launch criteria, so this trade-off is made on purpose rather than discovered later.

Follow-up: “Why not skip retrieval and rank everything with the big model?”

How a strong answer sounds
Do the maths: 2 billion items × 350,000 requests per second is 7×10¹⁴ scorings per second. Even at one microsecond each, that's impossible by many orders of magnitude — and the heavy ranker takes far longer than a microsecond per item. The funnel lets each stage spend as much compute per item as it can afford for the number of items it sees. The metric that links the stages is candidate recall, because the ranker can only order what retrieval gave it. The interesting question isn't whether to have a funnel, but how to measure what each stage throws away.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Make retention the main metric and use engagement signals as ranking targets — and protect the ecosystem with guardrails.
  • Six candidate sources, each with its own budget, timeout and recall metric, beat one clever retriever.
  • A cheap pre-ranker is what makes the expensive ranker affordable; measure what it throws away.
  • Log every impression with its position and scores, or your training data and offline testing of new rankers are unusable.
  • Exploration budgets and diversity limits are the built-in defence against the feedback loop.

Check your understanding

Why log impressions and not just engagements?

If you don't know what was shown, you can't find negatives (shown but ignored), correct for position bias (top slots get more clicks anyway), or estimate how a different ranker would have done.

A candidate source is slow at p99. What should the system do?

Each source has its own deadline. Losing 600 candidates costs a little quality; missing the latency budget ruins the whole request. Log the drop so the quality cost stays visible.

How does a brand-new video get its first audience?

Waiting for engagement is circular — it can't get views without views. A reserved exploration budget pays for the first measurement, and a strong early signal moves the video into normal retrieval within minutes.

Which is the clearest sign the feedback loop is closing?

The system settles on a shrinking set of safe hits. Engagement can rise while the catalogue quietly dies, which is why coverage and concentration belong in the launch criteria.

What is the pre-ranker's own success metric?

Its job is to shrink the candidate list without throwing away what matters. So measure how often it keeps the heavy ranker's top items — its recall of the eventual winners.
