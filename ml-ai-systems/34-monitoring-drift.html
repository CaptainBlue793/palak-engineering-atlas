Monitoring, Drift & ML Observability

When a web service breaks, it wakes someone up. When a model breaks, it keeps replying “200 OK” with a slightly worse answer, every time, for three months. ML monitoring exists because the failure is silent. And because the number you most want to watch, whether the model was right, usually arrives weeks late.

  • Monitor four layers: system, data, model and business
  • Distinguish data drift, concept drift and label shift, and measure each
  • Watch quality without labels, using prediction and proxy signals
  • Design alerts people don't learn to ignore
  • Triage an ML incident to the right layer quickly

Four layers, four kinds of question

Why four layers instead of one dashboard? Because each layer can fail while the others look perfectly healthy. A pipeline can start sending zeros (data layer broken) while the servers are fast and error-free (system layer fine). A model can keep receiving normal inputs but become wrong because the world changed (model layer broken, data layer fine). You only see the problem in the layer where it lives.

SystemIs it up and fast? Error rate, typical and worst-case latency (p50/p99), how loaded the machines are, queue depth, GPU memory, cost per request. Familiar, necessary, and nowhere near enough on its own.
DataAre the inputs what we expect? Schema, rates of missing and default values, the spread of each feature's values, freshness, volume. Most ML incidents start here.
ModelIs the behaviour steady? The spread of predictions, confidence, accuracy per slice once labels arrive, calibration, and how often it refuses or falls back.
BusinessIs it still useful? Conversion, fraud losses, support tickets avoided, escalation rate, complaints. Slow, noisy, and the only thing that really matters.
🧾
Log one row per prediction

Almost all of your ability to see what the model is doing comes from one well-designed log line. Without it, every investigation starts with weeks of adding logging you should have added months ago.

{ request_id, timestamp, user_segment, model_name, model_version, features_hash, feature_values (or a sampled subset), defaults_used, prediction, confidence, threshold, decision, latency_ms, fallback_used, prompt_version, cache_hit, cost_estimate }

If volume or privacy is a problem, log only a sample. Fully logging 1% of requests is enough to spot drift, and far better than 100% of nothing. When labels arrive later, they join this table by request_id.

Three kinds of drift

The table uses probability notation, so here it is in words. x is the model's inputs (the features) and y is the outcome it predicts (fraud or not). P(x) is “what inputs we see, and how often”. P(y) is “how often the outcome happens overall”. P(y | x), read “probability of y given x”, is “for a given input, how likely the outcome is” — that is the relationship the model learned. Each kind of drift changes a different one of these.

TypeWhat changedExampleDetectable without labels?
Data (covariate) driftP(x): the inputs changedA marketing campaign brings in younger users✅ Yes
Concept driftP(y|x): the link between inputs and outcome changedFraudsters change tactics, so the same features now mean something different❌ No, you need labels or proxies
Label shiftP(y): how often the outcome happens changedFraud attempts triple during a holiday⚠️ Partly, from the spread of predictions
Upstream breakThe data itself is simply wrongA pipeline change makes a feature always zero✅ Yes, and this is the most common of all

Why is concept drift invisible without labels? Because the inputs look exactly the same. Say transactions over £500 at night used to be fraud 2% of the time. Fraudsters learn the model's habits and switch to £480 daytime purchases. The input distribution barely moves, and the model keeps giving those purchases low scores. Only when the chargebacks arrive, weeks later, does anyone see that the old pattern stopped meaning what it used to.

How PSI and KS are computed

Both compare the reference distribution (the training data) with the current one (recent production traffic), one feature at a time. A worked example with a feature split into three bins:

BinTraining shareProduction share(prod − train) × ln(prod / train)
low50%40%(0.40 − 0.50) × ln(0.8) = 0.022
mid30%30%0 × ln(1) = 0
high20%30%(0.30 − 0.20) × ln(1.5) = 0.041

PSI is the sum of the last column: 0.022 + 0 + 0.041 = 0.063, which is below 0.1, so “stable”. Each bin adds a positive amount whenever the shares differ, and a bigger amount when the ratio between them is large. KS instead adds the shares up cumulatively and takes the biggest gap: after “low”, 50% vs 40%; after “mid”, 80% vs 70%; after “high”, 100% vs 100%. The largest gap is 0.10. One edge case: a bin with 0% on either side makes ln undefined, so real implementations replace zeros with a tiny share like 0.0001.

PSI (population stability index) and the KS statistic both measure how far today's values have moved from the training data. Rules of thumb for PSI: below 0.1 is stable, 0.1–0.25 is a moderate shift worth a look, above 0.25 is a big one. Treat them as reasons to investigate, not as automatic retraining decisions.

🔕
Drift is a symptom, not a verdict

Inputs can drift without hurting accuracy. And accuracy can collapse with no visible input drift. So use drift alerts to start an investigation. Save waking someone up for things that mean real user harm: a jump in fallbacks, a spike in missing values, a falling business metric, a slice that broke. Teams who page on every PSI wobble stop reading the alerts within a month.

Two examples of the mismatch. A campaign brings in many more users aged 18–24: the age feature's PSI jumps to 0.3, but if the model already handled young users well, accuracy doesn't move. Harmless drift. The opposite: the fraud pattern above changes, every PSI stays under 0.05, and recall quietly halves. Harmful, and invisible to drift metrics.

Monitoring quality when labels are late

Fraud labels take 60 days. Churn labels take 30. Loan defaults take years. You can't wait that long, so you watch leading indicators: earlier signals that tend to move before quality does.

📈

Prediction drift

The spread of scores the model outputs. If the average predicted fraud rate doubles overnight, something changed: the inputs, the population, or the model.

🧑‍⚖️

Proxy labels

Stand-ins for the real answer: what human reviewers decided, early signals like a chargeback filed or a session abandoned, and rule-based checks. Biased, but fast, and close enough to work as an alarm.

🎯

Sampled ground truth

Pay to label a small random sample, continuously. Expensive per item, but it's the only unbiased signal you'll have before the real labels land.

How big should that sample be? The same square-root rule as in A/B tests applies. Labelling 400 random predictions a week and finding 360 correct gives an accuracy estimate of 90%, give or take about 3 points (the standard error is √(0.9 × 0.1 / 400) = 0.015, and a 95% range is about twice that). That's enough to notice a drop from 90% to 80% within a week, but not a drop to 89%. To see smaller drops you need more labels, or you rely on the proxies for speed and the sample for honesty.

⏳
Backfill your dashboards

When labels finally arrive, recompute past performance and write it back into the dashboard. You get an honest answer to “how good was the model in week 12?”. It's just late. That honest curve is what lets you tune your leading indicators. Without it, you never learn whether your proxies were telling the truth.

Alerting that survives contact with a team

An alert is only useful if people act on it. Every false alarm teaches the on-call person that alerts can be ignored, and the lesson sticks. So the goal is not “alert on everything that might matter”. It is “every page is worth waking up for”. A concrete case of what goes wrong: a fixed rule “page if requests per minute drop below 5,000” fires every night at 3am, because nights are always quiet. After a week, nobody looks at it. The same metric compared with “the same hour last week” would stay silent at night and still catch a real outage.

PrincipleIn practice
Wake someone only for user impactError rate, latency SLO, fallback rate, a collapsed business metric. Not PSI.
Raise a ticket for investigationsDrift, rising rates of missing values, a slice getting worse. A human looks within a day.
Compare like with likeThis week against last week at the same hour. Traffic follows daily and weekly cycles, so fixed hourly thresholds page you every Monday.
Alert on how fast it's movingA metric dropping 20% in an hour matters more than its exact level.
Every alert has a runbookWhat it means, what to check first, who owns it, and how to make it stop.
Review and deleteAn alert that fired 40 times and nobody acted is noise. Fix the threshold or remove it.

Triage

When someone reports “the model got worse”, check the layers from cheapest and most common to slowest and rarest. First, did anything change: a deploy, a new model or prompt version, a pipeline edit? Then the data layer, since upstream breaks are the most common cause and show up instantly in missing-value rates. Then the model's outputs, sliced by segment. Only then the harder questions of concept drift or a change in the business itself. Answer the questions below to walk this path.

What's different for LLM systems

LLM systems break most of the tools above. The input is free text, so there is no neat feature histogram to compute PSI on. The output is free text too, so there is usually no single “correct” label to compare against. You still monitor the four layers, but with different signals: cheap measurable properties of the text (length, refusals, parse failures) as early alarms, and graded samples as the real quality measure.

SignalWhy it matters
Refusal rate, including refusing harmless requestsA safety change or a prompt edit can quietly make the assistant useless
How long the answers areChanging length is a cheap early sign that the model or prompt changed, and it drives cost
How often the output fails to parseWhen structured output (like JSON) breaks, the code around it fails before anyone notices a quality drop
Retrieval hit rate & whether citations are realIn RAG systems, quality usually collapses on the retrieval side, not the generation side (Ch 21)
Cache hit rate, tokens per request, costA prompt change that breaks prefix caching can multiply the bill overnight (Ch 19)
Quality scores on a sampleOngoing grading of sampled traffic by a judge model or a human. It's the only real quality signal (Ch 35)
Thumbs-down and escalation rateNoisy and biased, but immediate. The best cheap alarm you have
⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Monitoring only system metrics. Uptime says 100% while quality quietly halves.
  • No log per prediction. Then every investigation starts with a week of adding logging.
  • Waking people up for drift. People tune out within a month, and then they miss the real alerts too.
  • Watching only the overall number. A slice can fail completely while the average moves 0.3%.
  • Never backfilling evaluation. You never learn whether your leading indicators were right.
  • Ignoring the rate of missing and default values. It's the fastest sign of an upstream break, and the one least often monitored.

Interview drill

Warm-up: How do you monitor a model whose labels arrive 60 days late?

How a strong answer sounds
In layers. Immediately: input data quality, meaning schema, rates of missing and default values, and feature distributions compared with the training data. Plus the spread of predictions and how often they cross the decision threshold. Same day: proxies such as whether human reviewers agree, early outcome signals, and complaint rates. Continuously: a small random sample sent for labelling, so I have an unbiased quality estimate, even if it's small. Then, as real labels land, backfill the historical evaluation and check whether the leading indicators moved when quality did. That check is what makes the early signals trustworthy instead of decorative.

Deeper: Prediction volume is normal, latency is normal, but conversion dropped 8% overnight. Walk through it.

How a strong answer sounds
First: is it the model at all? Check whether anything was deployed. Model version, prompt version, feature pipeline, an upstream service, or a product change elsewhere in the journey. Then the data layer: missing and default rates per feature, and distributions against the training reference. One feature going constant is the classic cause, and it shows up instantly. Then the model layer: the spread of predictions and how often they cross the threshold, broken down by slice. An 8% overall drop is often one segment collapsing. Then serving: fallback rate and cache behaviour, because a silent fallback to a smaller model looks perfectly healthy on every system metric. If all that is clean, it's probably not the ML system. A change in traffic mix, a marketing campaign or an upstream page change can move conversion without anything in my stack changing. Throughout, I'd compare with the same hour last week, not with yesterday.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Monitor the system, data, model and business layers. System metrics alone hide every ML failure.
  • You can spot data drift without labels. You can't spot concept drift, so use proxies and sampled ground truth.
  • Wake people for user impact, raise a ticket for drift, and give every alert a runbook.
  • Log one row per prediction, and backfill the evaluation when labels arrive.
  • Monitor slices. The average is the last thing to move.

Check your understanding

Fraudsters change tactics but your input distributions look unchanged. What kind of drift is this?

The same inputs now lead to a different outcome. Input monitoring can't see it. That's exactly why proxies and sampled labels matter.

Which signal most quickly reveals “an upstream pipeline broke and a feature is now constant”?

It shows up within minutes at the data layer. Accuracy will show it too, in sixty days.

PSI for a feature is 0.32. What's the appropriate response?

Drift is a symptom. It could be a harmless change in who your users are, or the first sign of a break. Investigate it, but only wake people for signals tied to user impact.

Why backfill evaluation metrics when delayed labels arrive?

Backfilled truth is how you check the proxies you rely on day to day. Otherwise they're just guesses nobody ever tested.

Aggregate accuracy is flat, but one customer segment has collapsed. What was missing?

A small segment can fail completely and barely move the average. Track important slices one by one, and alert on each.
