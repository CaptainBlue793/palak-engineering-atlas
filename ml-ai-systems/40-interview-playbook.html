The ML System Design Interview Playbook

An ML design interview isn't a quiz about model architectures. You get forty-five minutes to show you can turn a vague business problem into a real system. That means a clear metric, a plan for the data, a budget for serving (speed and cost), and an honest list of what will break. Here's a structure that makes all of that easy for the interviewer to follow.

  • Run a 45-minute session with a repeatable structure
  • Say the right things in each phase — and know what to skip
  • Remember the numbers that make your estimates believable
  • Know what makes a staff-level answer different from a mid-level one
  • Practise actively instead of just re-reading
📚
What you need before this chapter
  • Choose metrics and thresholds with evaluation and validation.
  • Budget request latency using inference and serving.
  • Keep training inputs honest with feature stores and training/serving skew.
  • Recognise changing data with monitoring and drift.

What's actually being assessed

Start by naming one prediction and one action. For fake reviews, the prediction is the chance a review is fake. The action might be sending it to a human. Those are different decisions: a useful probability alone does not tell you how many reviews the team can inspect. Connecting them makes your requirements concrete.

Say this plan out loud in the first minute: “I'll spend five minutes on requirements, then metrics, data, features, a baseline and model, serving, and finish with monitoring and trade-offs — tell me if you'd rather go deeper somewhere.” It shows you have a plan, and it lets the interviewer steer you early — which is what they want.

What to say in each phase

Carry one example through the whole design. Suppose 2 million reviews arrive daily and humans can inspect 5,000. You can send only 5,000 ÷ 2,000,000 = 0.25% of arrivals to that queue. Choose a threshold using the expected daily volume, then measure precision and recall at that operating point. A model with a better overall score can still be worse in this narrow part of its ranking.

Next, trace the label. A moderator verdict arrives after submission, so it can be a training target but cannot be an input for the original decision. Store the features available at submission time alongside the later verdict. Reserve a random audit sample of unflagged reviews: otherwise you never learn how many fake reviews the model missed.

The three sentences that improve most answers

“The baseline is X — the model has to beat that to be worth the complexity.”
“The labels arrive N days late, so my monitoring uses these leading indicators.”
“I'd choose A over B here, and I'd switch to B if [specific condition].”
Each one shows you've run real systems, not just studied them. Most candidates say none of them.

Sort the statement into its phase

Some statements affect several phases. A slow graph feature belongs in the feature discussion, but its deadline also determines the serving design. In the exercise, choose the phase where the statement makes its main decision. In an interview, explain the connection instead of treating the phases as separate checklists.

Numbers to know cold

Write the units beside each number. At 4,000 requests/second and an average service time of 0.03 seconds, about 120 requests are in service at once. That is concurrency, not automatically 120 machines or threads. If one replica sustains 200 requests/second within the latency target, you need at least 4,000 ÷ 200 = 20 replicas before spare capacity. Load-test the actual batching and feature-fetch behaviour before choosing headroom.

A 100 ms request budget might allocate 10 ms to routing, 20 ms to features, 40 ms to scoring and 10 ms to response work. The remaining 20 ms covers queueing and variation. These are planning allowances, not a proof of the end-to-end 99th percentile: stage percentiles do not simply add. Measure the complete request path, including failures and overloaded dependencies.

Question archetypes

The compute table gives rough models, not hardware guarantees. For example, 8.4 × 10²² FLOPs ÷ 10¹⁵ useful FLOPs/second ÷ 86,400 seconds/day is about 972 GPU-days. At half that useful throughput, the time doubles. State the assumed throughput and distinguish it from advertised peak performance. Likewise, the KV-cache estimate assumes full multi-head attention; grouped-query attention stores fewer key/value heads.

What separates the levels

A good trade-off includes a trigger you can measure. For example: start with a tree model that meets the serving deadline. Try a larger model when error analysis shows a specific class of missed cases, then require a gain at the same review capacity. If feature-store timeouts rise, use a tested model with fewer features and log when it was used. Returning a default score silently would hide the outage inside apparently valid predictions.

Practise responding to changed assumptions. If review capacity halves to 2,500/day, the queue budget becomes 0.125% of arrivals. You may need a stricter threshold, more automation for clear cases, or a smaller product scope. Explain which errors increase under that choice. This shows the design follows its constraints rather than a memorised architecture.

MidNames the right parts and builds a sensible pipeline. Metrics are generic, serving is only sketched, and monitoring gets one mention.
SeniorPicks the metric based on the decision it supports, does the maths, splits the latency budget by stage, designs how labels are collected (including their delay), and names what can go wrong before being asked.
Staff+Questions the problem itself (“is a model even the right fix here?”). Says what they'd ship in week one versus in the second quarter. Spots the risks around people and teams — feedback loops, incentives, who gets woken up when it breaks. And gives a clear rule for when the design should change.

Red flags interviewers score against
  • Jumping to a model before defining the prediction task and its metric
  • No baseline, so no way to know whether any of this is worth building
  • Ignoring serving entirely — a design that can't meet its latency budget
  • Naming a technique without being able to explain what it costs (“we'd use RLHF”)
  • Silence for long stretches, or a monologue with no check-ins
  • Defending a choice after hearing new information, instead of changing your mind
  • Making up numbers and stating them as fact — say your assumption out loud instead

Interview drill

Opening: “Design a system to detect fake reviews.” What are your first 90 seconds?

How a strong answer sounds
“Before I design anything: what decision does this drive — do we remove the review, hide it pending review, or flag it for a human queue? That changes the precision/recall balance completely. What's the scale — reviews per day, and how many human reviewers do we have? What's the latency requirement: is this at submission time or a nightly sweep? And what's out of scope — am I also handling seller collusion and incentivised reviews, or just individual fake ones? Unless you say otherwise, I'll assume: 2M reviews a day, a human review queue that can handle ~5,000 a day, decisions within a minute of submission, and the metric is recall (share of fakes we catch) at the precision (share of flags that are really fake) our reviewers can keep up with. Does that match what you had in mind?”

Closing: How do you spend the last three minutes?

How a strong answer sounds
Sum up the design in four sentences: task and metric, data and labels, model and serving, monitoring and rollout. Then point out your two weakest spots yourself. For example: “the labels come from human review, and reviewers only see what the current model flags, so the labels are biased — I'd add random samples to the review queue. And my latency budget assumes the feature store answers in under 5 ms, which I'd want to check first.” Then say what you'd ship first: “week one, simple rules plus logging, so we have data to train on at all.” Naming your own weak spots comes across as senior, because it's what you'd do in a real design review. It also steers the remaining questions toward topics you're ready for.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • State your plan in the first minute and lead the session through eight phases.
  • Pick the metric based on the decision it drives, and always name the baseline you must beat.
  • Serving, delayed labels and monitoring are where most answers are weak — so that's where to be strong.
  • Know the maths well enough to estimate out loud with confidence.
  • Name your own weaknesses and give a decision rule for when the design should change.

Check your understanding

You're asked to “design a recommendation system”. What's the best first move?

Every later choice depends on what's being recommended, to whom, at what scale and within what budget. Turn the answers into clear stated assumptions, instead of asking open questions for ten minutes.

Roughly how much compute does training a 13B model on 3T tokens need?

6 × 13e9 × 3e12 ≈ 2.3e23. At ~1e15 useful FLOP/s per GPU that's around 2,700 GPU-days — the kind of estimate you should be able to do out loud. (The rule of thumb: training compute ≈ 6 × parameters × tokens.)

Which statement most signals production experience?

Late labels, and the monitoring they force on you, are a problem you only meet by running real systems. The other answers could be remembered from a blog post.

The interviewer challenges your database choice. What's the strongest response?

Interviewers are checking whether you reason about conditions or just repeat favourites. Spelling out when you'd choose each option is the answer that scores.

You have five minutes left and haven't covered monitoring. What do you do?

Covering every stage earns points. A design with no plan for monitoring or rollout looks like it came from someone who has never run a model in production. Extra polish on the model section rarely earns as much as simply bringing up drift and retraining.
