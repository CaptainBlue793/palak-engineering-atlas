Deployment, Rollout & Online Experiments

The offline metric is a prediction. The live experiment is the measurement. Some teams ship real improvements; others just ship changes. What separates them is mostly how carefully they get from the first to the second.

  • Roll out through shadow, canary and experiment with automated guardrails
  • Size an A/B test and know what effect it can actually detect
  • Avoid peeking, novelty effects and interference
  • Choose between A/B, interleaving and bandits
  • Read a result honestly and decide with it

The rollout ladder

Why not just switch everyone to the new model once it wins offline? Because offline evaluation runs on old, logged data, and production is where the real users, the real latency and the real side effects are. Each rung of this ladder answers one question, while exposing as few users as possible to a model that turns out to be broken.

1 · ShadowThe new model runs on real traffic. Its outputs are logged but never used. Catches skew, slowness and crashes at zero risk (Chapter 32). Lasts days.
2 · Canary1–5% of traffic, watched by automatic guardrails on errors, latency, cost and a fast proxy metric. This checks it's safe to run. It doesn't measure whether it's better.
3 · ExperimentA proper A/B test: users split randomly between old (A) and new (B). The share must be big enough to detect the effect you care about, and the length is fixed in advance (pre-registered). This is where you learn whether it helps.
4 · RampRaise the share in steps, re-checking guardrails at each level. Some effects only show up under heavy load or at scale.
5 · Full + holdbackShip to everyone, but keep a small group on the old version long term (a holdback). It measures the total effect over time and catches slow declines.
🛡️
Guardrails are not the same as the primary metric

The primary metric is what you hope will improve. Guardrails are what must not get worse. If one breaks, it should trigger an automatic rollback, no discussion needed.

Sizing an experiment

The most common experiment failure: the test was too small to ever detect the effect you hoped for. It lacked statistical power (the chance of spotting a real effect). Then you read its noise as a result. The minimum detectable effect (MDE) is the smallest change a test of a given size can reliably spot.

Why does size matter so much? Because every group of users is noisy. If 4% of users buy something, two random groups of 1,000 will not both show exactly 40 buyers; one might show 34 and the other 47, with no real difference at all. A test can only see an effect that is bigger than this natural wobble, and the wobble shrinks slowly, in proportion to 1/√(number of users).

A widely used rule of thumb, for a 5% false-alarm rate and 80% power, is:

users per arm ≈ 16 × p × (1 − p) / δ²

Here p is the baseline rate and δ is the absolute change you want to detect. Worked with the simulator's starting values: baseline 4%, and you hope for a 2% relative lift, so δ = 0.04 × 0.02 = 0.0008. Then 16 × 0.04 × 0.96 / 0.0008² ≈ 0.614 / 0.00000064 ≈ 960,000 users per arm (the simulator uses the slightly more exact constant 15.7 instead of 16, so it shows about 941,000). With 200,000 daily users and half of them in the test, each arm gets 50,000 a day, so the test needs about 19 days. Because δ is squared in the formula, halving the effect you want to detect multiplies the users needed by four.

Peeking: the most common way to fool yourself

Peeking means checking results while the test runs and stopping as soon as they look good. The test was designed so that only 5% of "wins" are false alarms. Checking daily and stopping when it goes green breaks that. You get something much worse.

Here is why. With no real effect, the measured difference between A and B drifts up and down randomly from day to day. On any single day, there's a 5% chance it happens to look significant. If you only look once, at the planned end, that is your 5%. But if you look on each of 21 days and stop at the first green, you get 21 chances for a random wobble to cross the line, and you only need one. If you must look early, use a method built for it

Some methods are designed for checking as you go. Sequential testing (always-valid p-values, mSPRT) and group-sequential designs with alpha spending (a planned error budget per look) let you monitor continuously without raising the error rate.

CUPED removes the part of each user's result that their behaviour before the experiment already predicts. It typically cuts variance (noise) by 30–50%, which shortens every test you run. It's usually the single most valuable upgrade to an experimentation platform.

The intuition: a heavy spender who spent £500 last month will probably spend a lot during the test too, whichever group they land in. That difference between users is noise the test has to see through. CUPED subtracts the part of each user's result that last month predicts, leaving mostly the part caused by the experiment. Since sample size scales with variance, cutting variance by 40% cuts the users (and days) needed by 40% too: the 19-day test above becomes about 11 days.

Sample-ratio mismatch deserves a number, because 48/52 sounds harmless. With 1,000,000 users split by a fair coin, the typical deviation from an exact 500,000 each is only about ±500 users (the square root of 1,000,000 × 0.5 × 0.5). A 480,000 / 520,000 split is off by 20,000, which is 40 times that. That can't be chance. Something is dropping or mis-assigning users, for example the new version crashes before it logs, or a bot filter treats the groups differently. And the users who went missing are not random, so every metric from the test is biased.

A/B, interleaving, or bandits?

⚖️

A/B test

The default. Unbiased, easy to interpret, and measures real product outcomes. But it's slow, and needs enough traffic for the size of effect.

🔀

Interleaving

For ranking: mix two rankers' results into one list and see whose items get clicked. It's 10–100× more sensitive because each user compares both. But it measures which ranking users prefer, not product outcomes.

🎰

Bandits

Keep shifting traffic toward whatever performs better. Great for many variants, short-lived decisions, and wasting as little traffic as possible on losers (minimising regret). Harder to get a clean effect estimate, and unsuitable for long-term metrics.

How interleaving works, in a small example. Ranker A returns [a1, a2, a3] and ranker B returns [b1, b2, b3]. The two take turns picking their best not-yet-used item for one merged list, like team captains picking players: [a1, b1, a2, b2, a3, b3] (who picks first is decided by a coin flip each time). Each shown item remembers which ranker picked it. If the user clicks b1 and b2, B wins this session. Over thousands of sessions, the share of wins tells you which ranker users prefer. It is so sensitive because the same user judges both rankers at the same moment, so differences between users cancel out.

When to pick each:

  • A/B — when you need to know the effect on a business outcome (revenue, retention) and guardrails, and you have enough traffic. This is the default for any launch decision.
  • Interleaving — when comparing two ranking or search models and traffic is too small for an A/B test to detect the difference. Use it to choose, then confirm with an A/B test.
  • Bandits — when there are many options and a quick, low-stakes reward, like picking one of 10 headlines for a news story that is only relevant for a day. Not when you need a trustworthy estimate of the effect.
🧭
A practical sequence for ranking changes

Offline NDCG (a ranking-quality score) to shortlist candidates → interleaving to pick the winner cheaply and quickly → a full A/B test on the winner to measure the product effect and check guardrails → ramp up with a long-term holdback. Each stage costs more than the last and is more trustworthy. That's exactly what you want.

Reading the result

Below is a realistic experiment readout. Two terms first. The p-value answers: “if the change truly did nothing, how often would noise alone produce a gap this big?” p = 0.013 means about 1.3% of the time, which is rare enough to believe the effect is real. The 95% confidence interval gives the range of true effects consistent with the data. The two agree: when the interval excludes zero, p is below 0.05.

primary   conversion +1.8%  (95% CI: +0.4% … +3.2%)   p = 0.013
guardrail p99 latency +6 ms (CI: +2 … +10)             ← within the +15 ms budget
guardrail cost/req   +9%                                ← above the +5% budget ⚠️
segments  new users +4.1%  ·  returning +1.1%  ·  EU +0.2% (CI spans zero)
SRM       50.02 / 49.98  ✅

decision: ship at 50% for two more weeks to confirm the cost figure,
          with a work item to bring cost/req back under budget before full ramp.

Reading it line by line: the primary metric is up, and even the low end of its interval (+0.4%) is positive, so it very likely helps. Latency rose but stays inside its budget. Cost broke its budget, so the result can't go to 100% as it is. The EU interval spans zero, which means “we can't tell”, not “no effect”. SRM passed, so the split itself can be trusted. The decision follows from the guardrail, not from the headline number.

📏
Report the interval, not just the point

“+1.8%” invites a decision the data can't support. “+1.8%, CI +0.4% to +3.2%” says: probably positive, possibly small. (The CI, or confidence interval, is the range the true effect likely falls in.) Also state the cost of being wrong. A change that's free to undo deserves a different bar than one that changes pricing or a safety threshold.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Peeking and stopping early. Turns a 5% error rate into 20–30%.
  • No sample-ratio mismatch check. A broken split silently ruins every result that follows.
  • Launching based on a canary. A canary checks safety, not value. It's far too small to measure an effect.
  • Treating “not significant” as “no effect”. Report the interval. A test that was too small tells you nothing; it's not a negative result.
  • Ignoring interference in marketplaces and social products, where the treatment leaks into the control group.
  • No long-term holdback. Once everyone has the change, total effects and slow declines become invisible.

Interview drill

Warm-up: Your test shows +3% conversion, p = 0.04, after six days of a planned fourteen. Ship it?

How a strong answer sounds
No, not on that basis. Stopping when the result first becomes significant is peeking. It multiplies the false-positive rate several times over. Effects caught early are also consistently overestimated. I'd run to the planned end date. Or, if stopping early matters for the business, I'd use a sequential design that stays valid under constant monitoring. I'd also check SRM, look for a novelty effect in the day-by-day results, and confirm the guardrails. If it's genuinely urgent, the honest options are to switch to an always-valid method, or accept a higher error rate and write that down. Not to pretend the fixed-horizon p-value still means what it says.

Deeper: How would you A/B test a change to a two-sided marketplace's ranking?

How a strong answer sounds
Simply splitting users at random doesn't work, because supply is shared. If treatment users book the best-rated providers, control users see fewer good options. The control group is contaminated, and the effect looks bigger than it is. Options: split by market or region (cluster randomisation). That leaves far fewer units, so much less power. Or use switchback testing: flip the whole market between versions in time blocks. That handles interference, but you must size the blocks carefully so effects don't carry over. Or use budget-split designs that divide the supply. I'd also measure both sides: buyer conversion, and provider bookings and earnings. A change that helps buyers by piling demand onto a few providers is usually bad for the marketplace. And I'd measure the interference directly: does control-group availability move when the treatment share changes?

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Shadow proves it runs, canary proves it's safe, an experiment proves it helps. One can't replace another.
  • Size tests from the baseline rate and the effect size. Halving the effect needs four times the sample.
  • Peeking inflates false positives. Use a fixed end date, or a sequential method designed for monitoring.
  • Watch for interference, novelty, SRM, ratio metrics and segment shopping.
  • Report confidence intervals and guardrails, and keep a long-term holdback.

Check your understanding

You check your A/B test daily and stop when p < 0.05. What's the real false-positive rate?

Each look is another chance for noise to cross the threshold. The fixes: a fixed end date, or an always-valid sequential method.

Your 50/50 experiment delivered 52.3% / 47.7% of users. What do you do?

With that many users, an imbalance that big is far beyond chance. Something is consistently dropping or mis-assigning users, so the groups aren't comparable. Fix it and re-run.

Why is interleaving so much more sensitive than an A/B test for ranking?

Most A/B noise comes from users simply being different from each other. Comparing within one user removes it. The cost: you only measure ranking preference, not product outcomes.

A new recommendation model shows +12% engagement in week 1 and +1% by week 4. What is this?

Users react to the change itself because it's new. Run long enough for the effect to settle, and report the settled value, not the first week.

Which is a guardrail metric rather than a primary metric?

Guardrails must not get worse and should trigger automatic rollback. The primary metric is the one you're trying to move.
