Training Deep Networks in Practice

Backprop is the theory. This chapter is the craft: the practical choices — how you set the starting weights, keep numbers in range, pick an optimizer, change the learning rate over time and prevent overfitting. These choices decide whether a model trains well in two hours or blows up into NaN (“not a number”) at step 300.

Initialization: the first thing that can kill a run

Each layer multiplies its input by a weight matrix. If the starting weights are a bit too small, the values (activations) shrink layer after layer until the signal is lost in noise. A bit too big, and they explode.

The fix is to pick random starting weights whose size depends on the layer's fan-in — the number of inputs feeding each unit. More inputs means smaller weights, so the sum stays about the same size.

Here's the arithmetic behind that. A unit adds up n inputs, each multiplied by a random weight. If the inputs have spread (variance) 1 and the weights have variance σ², the sum has variance of about n·σ², because the variances of independent random terms add up. With n = 512 and σ = 1, the output's standard deviation is about √512 ≈ 23: each layer makes the numbers 23 times bigger, and after 10 layers they overflow. Choose σ = √(1/512) ≈ 0.044 instead and the output variance is 512 × 1/512 = 1, the same as the input. That's the idea behind Xavier, which uses the average of fan-in and fan-out so the backward pass stays steady too. He initialization doubles the variance (√(2/n)) because ReLU zeroes out about half of the values, which would otherwise halve the spread at every layer.

Normalization: keep the scale under control

Initialization sets the scale at step 0. Normalization layers keep it stable for the rest of training. They re-centre and re-scale the activations inside the network so they don't drift too big or too small.

Why can't initialization alone handle this? Because the weights change with every update. A layer that started with nicely sized outputs can drift, and every layer after it then has to adapt to its changing input. Normalization resets the scale at each layer, on every step, so each layer sees inputs of a predictable size. In practice this lets you use higher learning rates and makes training much less sensitive to initialization.

A small example of what the operation does. Say one example's activations at some layer are [2, 4, 6]. LayerNorm takes their mean (4) and standard deviation (about 1.63), subtracts the mean and divides by the standard deviation: [−1.22, 0, 1.22]. Then it multiplies by a learned scale and adds a learned shift, one per feature, so the network can still choose a different size if that helps. BatchNorm does the same thing but computes the mean and standard deviation for each feature down the batch — across different examples — instead of across one example's features.

📦

BatchNorm

Normalizes each feature across the batch of examples. Great for CNNs. But it depends on batch size, behaves differently at inference (it uses saved running averages instead), and is awkward for sequences and for training across many machines.

📏

LayerNorm

Normalizes across features within one example. It doesn't care about batch size and works the same in training and inference — the default for transformers and anything that handles sequences.

⚡

RMSNorm

LayerNorm without subtracting the mean: it just divides by the root-mean-square (a kind of average size) of the values. Slightly cheaper and works just as well — used in most recent LLMs.

🏗️
Pre-norm vs post-norm

The original transformers normalized after each sub-layer (post-norm). They needed a careful warmup to avoid blowing up. Modern ones normalize before (pre-norm: x + f(norm(x))). That leaves the skip path untouched, so deep models train far more stably. If a training run only works with a long warmup, post-norm is a likely cause.

Optimizers: SGD, momentum, Adam

The optimizer decides how to turn gradients into weight updates.

  • Plain SGD (stochastic gradient descent) steps straight downhill, against the gradient.
  • Momentum builds up speed, like a ball rolling downhill, so it pushes through noise and small dips.
  • Adam also tracks how big each parameter's gradients usually are, and divides by that. Each parameter effectively gets its own learning rate.

In a little more detail, with g the current gradient:

  • Momentum keeps a running “velocity” v ← 0.9·v + g and steps by η·v. If the gradient points the same way for many steps, v grows to about 10× a single gradient (since 1 + 0.9 + 0.81 + … = 10), so progress speeds up along consistent directions. If the gradient flips sign every step — the zig-zag across a valley — the terms cancel and the side-to-side motion dies down.
  • Adam keeps two running averages: m, the average gradient (like momentum), and s, the average squared gradient. It steps by roughly η · m / √s. Dividing by √s cancels out each parameter's typical gradient size. A weight whose gradients are always around 0.001 and one whose gradients are around 10 both end up moving by about η per step. That's why Adam copes well with parameters of very different scales, and why its default learning rate works so often.

When to pick which: AdamW is the safe default for almost any new network, and the only sensible choice for transformers. SGD with momentum is worth trying for image CNNs when you have a known-good recipe to copy and time to tune, because it sometimes generalises slightly better. Plain SGD without momentum is mainly a teaching tool today.

Adam costs memory, and that memory decides your cluster

Adam keeps two extra numbers for every parameter: a running average of its gradient and a running average of the gradient squared (the first and second moments). For a 7B-parameter model in mixed precision, that's roughly 14 GB of weights + 14 GB of gradients + 56 GB of optimizer state in fp32. The optimizer is the biggest single item. This arithmetic is why ZeRO and FSDP exist (Chapter 23).

Where those numbers come from: 7 billion parameters × 2 bytes each (16-bit) = 14 GB for the weights, and the same again for one gradient per weight. Adam's two moments are kept in 32-bit, so 7 billion × 2 numbers × 4 bytes = 56 GB. Mixed-precision training usually also keeps a 32-bit master copy of the weights (another 28 GB), which takes the total past 110 GB before a single activation is stored — more than any one GPU holds. Plain SGD without momentum would need none of the 56 GB.

Learning-rate schedules

A constant learning rate is almost never the best choice. You want to start small, because the weights are random and the gradients are wild. Then rise to a peak, then lower it again (decay) so the model can settle into a minimum.

Why lower it at the end? Think back to Chapter 5's bowl. A large step gets you near the bottom quickly but then keeps bouncing around it, especially with noisy mini-batch gradients. Smaller steps late in training let the weights settle into the bottom instead of rattling around it. A typical LLM-style schedule: peak learning rate 3e-4, warmup over the first 2,000 of 100,000 steps (so at step 1,000 the rate is 1.5e-4), then a cosine curve that glides down to around 3e-5 by the last step. It falls slowly at first, fastest in the middle, and slowly again at the end.

Why warmup exists

At step 0, Adam's estimate of typical gradient size is based on almost no data. So its step sizes are erratic and can be huge. Warmup ramps the learning rate up slowly over the first few hundred to few thousand steps, giving those estimates time to settle. For large-batch and transformer training, removing warmup is one of the surest ways to blow up a run.

Regularization

Deep networks have so many parameters that they can memorise almost any training set, including its noise. Regularization is everything you do to make memorising harder than learning the real pattern. You know you need more of it when training loss keeps falling while validation loss flattens or rises (Chapter 7). You need less of it when both losses are high and close together — then the model is underfitting.

Dropout, one detail that matters. With a dropout rate of 0.1, each activation is set to zero with 10% probability during training. To keep the average size of the layer's output the same, the survivors are multiplied by 1 / (1 − 0.1) ≈ 1.11. At inference nothing is dropped and nothing is rescaled. That's why train mode and eval mode behave differently — and why forgetting to switch modes (see the mistakes below) gives noisy predictions.

Precision, clipping and accumulation

Three practical techniques you'll find in almost every real training script.

🎚️ Mixed precision

Do the maths in 16-bit numbers (bf16/fp16), but keep a 32-bit (fp32) master copy of the weights. Roughly 2× faster, and activations take half the memory. bf16 can hold numbers as large and small as fp32 can, so it doesn't need the extra trick of loss scaling — prefer it where your hardware supports it.

✂️ Gradient clipping

If the overall size of the gradients (the global norm) goes above a limit (commonly 1.0), scale them down to that limit. A step that would have blown up training becomes a slightly wasted step instead. Effectively required for language models.

➕ Gradient accumulation

Add up gradients over k small batches before updating the weights. This mimics a k× larger batch on a GPU that can't fit it. Remember to divide the loss by k, or your effective learning rate is quietly multiplied by k.

The three, with numbers:

  • Why fp16 needs loss scaling and bf16 doesn't. A number format splits its bits between range (how big or small a number can be) and precision (how many digits it keeps). fp16 can't store anything larger than 65,504, and values much below about 0.00006 start losing precision until, below roughly 0.00000006, they become zero. Many gradients are that small, so they vanish. Loss scaling multiplies the loss by, say, 1,024 before the backward pass (which scales every gradient up by the same amount), then divides the gradients by 1,024 before the update. bf16 has the same range as fp32 (up to about 3×10³⁸) and simply keeps fewer digits, so small gradients survive without the trick.
  • Clipping in action. Suppose one bad batch produces gradients whose overall length (norm) is 50, with a clip limit of 1.0. Every gradient is multiplied by 1.0 / 50 = 0.02. The direction of the step is kept; only its size is capped. When the norm is below the limit, clipping does nothing at all.
  • Accumulation in action. You want a batch of 256 but only 64 fit in memory. Run 4 forward and backward passes of 64, letting the gradients add up, divide by 4 (or divide each loss by 4), then take one optimizer step and reset. The update equals what a real 256 batch would give — except for BatchNorm, which still only sees 64 examples at a time.

The debugging order

When a training run misbehaves, work down this list in order. Don't just start changing settings at random.

Overfit 16 examplesCan the model push the loss to ~0 on a tiny batch it sees over and over? If not, it's a bug, not a tuning issue. Look at labels, masking, a frozen layer, or a broken gradient path (a detached graph).
Check the input pipelinePrint one batch. Check shapes, data types, value ranges, and that each label matches its input. Turn five samples back into text or images and look at them.
Watch gradient size per layerTiny gradients (≈1e-8) or huge ones (≈1e4) point straight at initialization, normalization or the learning rate.
Sanity-check the loss at step 0For K-class cross-entropy it should be about ln(K) (e.g. 2.30 for 10 classes, ~10.8 for a 50k-word vocabulary). A very different value means a label or shape bug.
Run the LR range testRaise the learning rate exponentially over a few hundred steps and plot the loss. Use roughly one-tenth of the rate where the loss first starts to blow up.
Only then tuneSchedule, weight decay, dropout, batch size. Change one thing at a time and log it (Chapter 31).

Two of those steps deserve a closer look.

  • Why ln(K) at step 0? A freshly initialized network has no idea yet, so it should give each of the K classes a probability of about 1/K. Cross-entropy is −log of the probability given to the correct class, so the loss is −log(1/K) = ln(K). For 10 classes that's ln(10) ≈ 2.30. A value far above it means the untrained model is already confidently wrong — usually a bug, or an output layer initialized too large.
  • An LR range test, concretely. Start at 1e-7 and multiply the learning rate by 1.1 every step; after about 170 steps it has reached 1. Plot loss against learning rate on a log axis. You might see the loss flat until 1e-5, falling fastest around 1e-3, then shooting up at 1e-2. The rate where it blows up is about 1e-2, so pick a peak of roughly 1e-3. The test costs a few minutes and replaces a lot of guessing.
🎲
Reproducibility is partial, and that's fine

Set the random seed in Python, NumPy and your framework, and log it. Getting the exact same numbers bit for bit also needs deterministic GPU code (often slower), a fixed data order, and identical hardware and library versions. For most work, aim for “same conclusions with different seeds” instead. If a result only shows up with one seed, it isn't a real result. Report mean ± std over 3 seeds for anything you'd defend in a review.

Hyperparameter search that isn't a waste of GPUs

Hyperparameters are the settings you choose rather than learn: learning rate, batch size, weight decay, dropout, number of layers. Each full training run is expensive, so the question is how to find good settings in as few runs as possible. A grid of 5 values for each of 4 settings is already 5⁴ = 625 runs — which is why the two tricks below matter.

Random > grid

Say 2 of your settings matter and 6 don't. A grid search spends almost all its budget varying the 6 that don't matter. Random sampling tries far more distinct values of the important ones for the same cost. Sample learning rate and weight decay log-uniformly (evenly across powers of ten, e.g. 1e-5 to 1e-2).

Kill bad runs early

Successive halving / ASHA: start many short runs, keep the best few, run those longer, and repeat. Combined with Bayesian search (which uses past results to pick the next settings; tools: Optuna, Ray Tune), this is typically 5–10× cheaper than running everything to the end.

A successive-halving round, sized: launch 32 configurations for 1,000 steps each. Keep the best 16 and run them to 2,000 steps, then the best 8 to 4,000, and so on until one remains. The bet is that a configuration that's clearly worse early rarely becomes the best later. That's mostly true, with one known exception: runs with a lower learning rate often look worse early and win late, so don't cut too aggressively at the first round.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Applying weight decay to biases and LayerNorm parameters. It quietly hurts; leave them out (most reference code does).
  • Leaving dropout on at inference. Frameworks switch it off in eval mode — forget model.eval() and your predictions come out mysteriously noisy.
  • Changing batch size without touching the learning rate. The effective step size changes too; scale the rate to match and keep warmup.
  • Not dividing by the accumulation count. If you add up gradients over k batches without dividing, your effective learning rate is k× too big.
  • Tuning on the test set. Every choice of settings must be made on the validation set.
  • Tuning settings when the data is the problem. A 2% gain from tuning won't rescue a leaked feature or noisy labels.

Interview drill

Warm-up: Your transformer diverges at step 400 with loss going to NaN. Name five things you'd check, in order.

How a strong answer sounds
(1) Learning rate and warmup — does it reach the peak rate too early for this batch size? (2) Gradient clipping — is it on, and how big is the overall gradient just before the spike? (3) Precision — fp16 without loss scaling overflows; switch to bf16 or turn on the loss scaler. (4) Data — is there a corrupt batch at that step? Log the batch index and re-run that batch alone. (5) Initialization and normalization — pre-norm vs post-norm, and scaling the residual layers for depth. I'd also save a checkpoint just before the spike so I can replay the exact batch instead of guessing.

Deeper: Why does AdamW exist when Adam already supports weight decay?

How a strong answer sounds
In Adam, the L2 penalty is added to the gradient. It then gets divided by the same per-parameter gradient-size estimate (second moment) as everything else. So parameters that have had large gradients get much weaker decay — how strongly each weight is regularized becomes an accident of its gradient history. AdamW separates the two: it shrinks the weights directly, outside the adaptive scaling. That makes weight decay a setting you can tune meaningfully, and models do better on new data. That's why it's the default for transformer training.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Scale starting weights by fan-in (He for ReLU, Xavier for tanh) so activations keep a steady size through many layers.
  • LayerNorm/RMSNorm for sequences, BatchNorm for CNNs; normalizing before each sub-layer (pre-norm) trains more stably.
  • AdamW is the default for transformers; its extra per-parameter state takes up most of the training memory.
  • Use warmup plus decay, and find the peak learning rate with an LR range test, not rules of thumb.
  • Debug in order: overfit 16 examples, inspect a batch, check gradient sizes, verify the step-0 loss — then tune.

Check your understanding

You're training a 24-layer transformer. Which normalization is the natural default?

LayerNorm normalizes within one example, so it doesn't depend on batch size and works the same in training and inference. That's essential for sequences of different lengths and for training across many machines.

A 10-class classifier's loss at step 0 is 6.8 instead of about 2.3. What does that suggest?

A model with random weights should be equally unsure about every class, giving a loss of ln(10) ≈ 2.30. A much higher value means it's confidently wrong — often shuffled labels or a shape mismatch.

You double the batch size from 256 to 512. What should you usually do to the learning rate?

A bigger batch gives a less noisy gradient, so you can afford a bigger step. Scaling the rate in proportion (linear scaling) with warmup is the standard recipe; multiplying by √2 is a more cautious option.

What problem does gradient clipping solve?

One bad batch can produce a gradient thousands of times larger than usual. Clipping caps the overall gradient size, so a step that could have wrecked training becomes a slightly wasted step.

Why is bf16 usually preferred over fp16 for training on modern hardware?

bf16 gives up some precision (fewer mantissa bits) to cover a much wider range of values. Deep learning copes fine with low precision but not with overflow, so bf16 removes a whole class of training crashes — without needing loss scaling.
