GPU Capacity & Cost Engineering

For most companies, GPUs are the most expensive computers they have ever rented. And most of that money pays for GPUs sitting idle. Cost engineering for ML isn't penny-pinching. It decides whether you have a product that earns more than each use costs (healthy unit economics), or a demo you can't afford to launch.

  • Work out what each unit of work costs: per token, per request and per successful outcome
  • Find where GPU time is actually wasted
  • Choose between on-demand, reserved, spot and owned hardware
  • Plan capacity with spare room, queues and slow start-up times in mind
  • Track cost per customer (tenant) and stop runaway spending

Know your unit

“We spend £180k a month on GPUs” is not a number you can act on. You need to divide the cost by a unit of useful work. These are the useful ones:

UnitWhy it's useful
Cost per 1M tokens (in / out separately)You can compare it directly with vendor prices; shows whether running your own models is worth it
Cost per requestLines up with what you charge for the product and with per-feature budgets
Cost per active user per monthThe number that decides whether the business model works
Cost per successful outcomeThe honest one — retries, hand-offs to humans and abandoned sessions all cost money but deliver nothing
Cost per experimentMakes research spending visible, so you can compare it with what it produced

Here is how you turn a GPU bill into cost per million tokens. Say one GPU rents for $2.50 an hour and, with good batching, produces 2,500 output tokens per second across all the requests it serves. In an hour that's 2,500 × 3,600 = 9 million tokens, so the cost is $2.50 ÷ 9 ≈ $0.28 per million tokens. That's only true if the GPU is busy the whole hour. If it's busy 40% of the time, you still pay for the full hour but produce 40% of the tokens, so the real cost is $0.28 ÷ 0.4 ≈ $0.69. This one division, cost ÷ utilization, explains most of the gap between a vendor's price sheet and your own bill.

🎯
Cost per successful outcome changes decisions

Say a cheap model answers 70% of support tickets correctly and an expensive one answers 92%. Once you count the tickets handed off to humans, the cheap model may cost more per solved ticket.

Cutting cost per token while more tickets get passed to humans is how teams save £20k on GPUs and spend £80k more on support salaries.

The arithmetic, per 1,000 tickets. Suppose a human agent costs £4 per ticket they handle. The cheap model costs £0.002 a ticket and fails on 30%: that's £2 of model cost plus 300 × £4 = £1,200 of human cost, so about £1.20 per ticket. The expensive model costs £0.03 a ticket and fails on 8%: £30 plus 80 × £4 = £320, so about £0.35 per ticket. The model that is 15 times pricier per call is more than three times cheaper per solved problem. The lesson: always add the cost of what happens after a failure.

The cost model

A monthly LLM bill comes from a handful of numbers multiplied together: requests per day × tokens per request × the cost of each token, divided by how busy your GPUs are. Input tokens are cheaper to process than output tokens, because the whole prompt can be handled in one parallel pass (prefill), while output comes out one token at a time (decode). That's why the simulator asks for them separately. Each lever below cuts one of those factors: routing sends easy requests to a smaller model, caching skips repeated work, shorter prompts cut input tokens, and so on.

Why do levers multiply rather than add? Because each one shrinks what's left after the others. If routing sends 60% of traffic to a model that costs a tenth as much, the bill falls to 0.4 + 0.6 × 0.1 = 46% of the original. If prompt caching then cuts the remaining cost by a further 30%, you're at 0.46 × 0.7 ≈ 32%. Two moderate levers together removed about two-thirds of the bill. The flip side: once the big levers are on, a small tuning win of 10% saves 10% of an already small number.

Where utilization goes

Utilization here means the share of the hours you pay for in which a GPU is doing useful work. It's different from MFU (model FLOPs utilization, Chapter 10), which asks how much of the chip's peak maths speed you use while it's working. Both multiply into your real cost: a GPU busy 50% of the time at 40% MFU is delivering about 20% of what you paid for. The table lists where the rest usually goes, roughly from biggest to smallest.

LossTypical sizeFix
Provisioned for peak, running at trough30–60%Autoscaling (adding and removing GPUs as load changes) with warm pools of ready machines; move batch work into the quiet hours
Small batches at low traffic20–50% of theoretical throughputContinuous batching (keep adding requests to the running batch); merge copies of the model onto fewer GPUs at night (Chapter 26)
Dev/experiment GPUs left idleoften >70% of those GPUsShut down machines after a period of idleness, share pools, give notebooks an expiry time
Fragmentation10–30%Match model size to GPU size; split one GPU into slices (MIG); pack several small models onto one GPU
Failed and abandoned runs5–20%Stop bad runs early (ASHA), check setup before launching, run cheap quick tests first
Retries and duplicate work2–10%Idempotency (repeating a request doesn't repeat the work), caching responses, cancelling work when the client disconnects
📉
Utilization is a cost metric, not a performance metric

If your GPUs are 40% busy, 60% of the bill buys nothing. But chasing 95% is also wrong. You need spare room for traffic spikes, for failover when a machine dies, and for deploys.

Aim for a target range (say 55–75% at peak). If you stay outside it for a while, act: scale down if you're below it, or scale up before you break your SLO (your promised service level, such as response time).

Why not 95%? Queueing. When a server is nearly full, any small burst of requests has to wait, and waiting time grows sharply as you approach 100%: in simple queueing models, going from 50% to 90% busy multiplies the average wait roughly ninefold. On top of that, if one of ten machines dies while all are 95% busy, the other nine can't absorb its load. At 70%, they can.

Buy, rent, reserve or scavenge

Cloud providers sell the same GPU in several ways. The cheaper the price per hour, the more you give up: either flexibility (you commit for a year or more) or reliability (the machine can be taken away). The skill is matching each kind of work to the cheapest option it can tolerate.

OptionRelative priceUse forRisk
On-demand cloud1.0×Bursty and unpredictable workHighest price per hour; scarce GPUs may not be available when you ask
Reserved / committed0.4–0.6×Your predictable baselineYou pay whether you use it — size it to the floor, not the peak
Spot / preemptible0.2–0.4×Training that saves checkpoints, batch inference, evaluationThe cloud can take the machine back at any moment — you need the recovery tools from Chapter 25
Owned hardware0.2–0.35× (amortised)Large, steady, long-term demandBig upfront cost, hardware losing value, running a datacentre, and a 3-year bet on a fast-changing market
Serverless / per-token APIsvariesLow or spiky volume, launching quicklyCheapest until volume grows, then usually the most expensive

The rule for reserving comes from one comparison. A reserved GPU at 0.5× the on-demand price is paid for every hour, used or not. An on-demand GPU is paid only when running. So reserving is cheaper only if you'd use that GPU more than 50% of the hours, in general more than the reserved price ratio. Suppose your demand never drops below 40 GPUs and peaks at 100 for four hours a day. The first 40 GPUs are busy 100% of the time, well past the break-even, so reserve them. GPUs 41–100 are only needed about 17% of the time, far below it, so rent those on demand. Reserving all 100 would mean paying half price for 60 GPUs that sit idle 20 hours a day.

Owned hardware follows the same logic with a longer horizon. Its low amortised price assumes the machines stay busy for their whole three-to-five-year life. If demand halves after a year, or a much faster GPU generation arrives, the effective price per useful hour rises sharply.

🧮
The layered capacity strategy

Think of it in three layers:

  • Reserve your floor — the demand that exists every single hour.
  • Use on-demand machines for the daily peak.
  • Put work that can be interrupted — training, evals, batch scoring — on spot, so it soaks up whatever capacity is cheap.

Most teams save 30–50% just by matching what they commit to with the shape of their demand, before touching a single model.

Capacity planning is deciding how many GPUs to have running at each hour. Too few and requests queue up or fail. Too many and you pay for idle machines. Cold start is what makes GPU autoscaling hard

A "cold start" is the time a new machine needs before it can serve. Downloading a container and loading 140 GB of model weights takes minutes. Autoscaling that reacts in ten minutes cannot handle a spike that arrives in two.

Ways to soften it:

  • Keep a warm pool of ready machines, sized to your typical spike.
  • Download container images onto machines ahead of time.
  • Cache weights on fast local disks (NVMe).
  • Load weights lazily and stream them in.
  • Scale on a leading signal that warns you early (queue length rising, known traffic patterns), not a lagging one.

Sizing the warm pool, with numbers: each replica handles 20 requests per second and takes 8 minutes to start. Your traffic can jump by 100 requests per second within a couple of minutes, for example when a US morning starts. Then you need about 100 ÷ 20 = 5 replicas already running and idle to absorb that jump while new ones boot. Those 5 idle replicas are the price of not failing during the 8-minute gap. Faster start-up (cached weights, pre-pulled images) shrinks the gap, and with it the warm pool you have to pay for.

Attribution and control

Once many teams and customers share the same GPUs, nobody feels responsible for the total. Attribution means splitting the bill so every pound has an owner; control means limits that stop one owner from spending everyone else's budget. Without both, costs grow quietly until the month-end invoice.

🏷️

Tag everything

Label every cost with team, model, environment and tenant. Unlabelled spending ends up as “platform overhead”, where nobody owns it.

📊

Showback then chargeback

First just show teams their cost per model and per feature (showback). Later, bill it to their budget (chargeback). Behaviour usually changes before any money moves.

🚨

Quotas and anomaly alerts

Give each tenant a token quota and a spending cap. Alert when spend jumps 3× from one day to the next — a runaway loop, a leaked key and an abusive customer all look the same on the bill.

How do you attribute a GPU that serves many tenants at once? You can't tag the machine, so you meter the work. Log input and output tokens per request with the tenant's ID, then split the fleet's cost in proportion to tokens, weighting output tokens more because they take more GPU time. If tenant X used 12% of the weighted tokens in a month on a $50,000 fleet, their share is $6,000. Idle capacity can be shared out the same way or shown as its own line, which has the useful side effect of making waste visible.

💸
Denial of wallet

An attacker (or a buggy client) who can trigger long generations can multiply your bill without breaking anything. Defences:

  • Per-tenant limits on requests and tokens.
  • A hard cap on output tokens.
  • Request timeouts.
  • A spending cap per API key that suspends the key automatically.
  • Alerts on unusual cost.

Treat cost as part of staying online: running out of budget takes you down just as surely as running out of GPUs (Chapter 36).

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Cutting cost per token while more requests get handed to humans. Measure cost per successful outcome.
  • Reserving capacity for the peak. Commit to the floor; use on-demand for bursts; put interruptible work on spot.
  • Autoscaling on GPU utilization. It hits nearly 100% as soon as anything runs, so it can't tell you how much room is left — use queue length instead.
  • Ignoring development and experiment GPUs. Often the biggest single source of waste, and the least watched.
  • No per-tenant cost tracking. You can't price a product if you don't know what each customer costs you.
  • Buying hardware for a workload you haven't measured for six months. That's a three-year commitment based on a three-week trend.
🎤
Interview drill

Warm-up: Your GPU bill doubled with flat traffic. Where do you look?

How a strong answer sounds
If traffic is flat, the cost per request changed. I'd check in this order. First, prompt or context length: the two most common causes are a retrieval change that returns more chunks, or a system-prompt edit that broke prefix caching (reusing work for a shared prompt start). Both can double input tokens overnight. Second, output length, which a prompt or model change can inflate. Third, routing: are requests that used to go to the small model now going to the large one, or is a fallback firing all the time? Fourth, infrastructure: replicas that scaled up and never scaled down, an idle training cluster, a stuck batch job, or dev machines nobody shut down. Finally, abuse: a per-tenant breakdown will show a single account that grew 50×. To stop it happening again, add per-tenant cost tracking, a cost-per-request dashboard and an anomaly alert — so next time someone gets paged the same day, instead of finding out at month end.

Deeper: Self-host or use a per-token API?

How a strong answer sounds
It depends on volume and on how much control you need. Below a few hundred million tokens a month, APIs almost always win: you pay for no idle machines, you have nothing to operate, and you get newer models right away. Above that, self-hosting can be several times cheaper per token — if you keep your GPUs busy. That needs steady traffic, good batching and someone to own the system. The break-even sum must include engineering time, spare machines for failover, and the cost of falling behind on model quality. Often non-cost factors decide it anyway: where data must be stored, a minimum latency you need, custom fine-tunes, or a contract saying data never leaves your infrastructure. A common middle path: host a small distilled model for the large volume of easy traffic, and call an API for the rare hard cases. That's the routing idea from Chapter 27, applied to buying decisions.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Pick a unit to measure cost by — cost per successful outcome stops you from making "savings" that cost more elsewhere.
  • Most GPU spend pays for idle time; aim utilization at a target range, not at the maximum.
  • Match what you commit to with the shape of your demand: reserve the floor, use on-demand for bursts, put interruptible work on spot.
  • Because GPU machines start slowly, autoscale on early-warning signals and keep warm pools ready.
  • Label costs, track them per team and tenant, set quotas and alert — running out of money takes you down like an outage.

Check your understanding

Which single change usually gives the largest cost reduction for an LLM product?

Not calling the expensive model at all saves more than making it cheaper. Quantization and batching then multiply the savings on the traffic that's left.

You reserve capacity equal to your peak demand. What's the problem?

Commit only to the floor — the demand that exists every hour. Serve the peak with on-demand machines, and put interruptible work on spot.

Why is GPU utilization a poor autoscaling signal?

Queue length or queue wait time tracks your latency target directly, and warns you before users notice anything.

Your cheaper model cut cost per token by 60% but support costs rose. What metric would have caught this?

Hand-offs to humans, retries and abandoned sessions cost money but deliver nothing. Only a metric measured per successful outcome counts them.

What is “denial of wallet”?

Nothing breaks; the bill just grows. The defences are per-tenant quotas, output caps, spending limits that suspend keys automatically, and alerts on unusual cost.
