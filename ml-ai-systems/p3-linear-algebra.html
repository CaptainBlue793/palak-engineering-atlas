Vectors, Matrices & the Dot Product

In machine learning, almost everything is a list of numbers. A customer is a list of features. A word is a list of 768 numbers called an embedding. A neural-network layer is a grid of numbers that turns one list into another. Linear algebra is the maths of those lists and grids. You need only a small, very practical part of it: vectors, lengths, the dot product, cosine similarity and matrix multiplication. This chapter teaches exactly that part, with pictures you can drag.

  • Read a vector both as data (a list of features) and as geometry (an arrow)
  • Compute a vector's length and the distance between two vectors
  • Compute a dot product by hand and say what a large, zero or negative value means
  • Use cosine similarity to compare meanings, as search engines and attention do
  • Multiply a matrix by a vector — one dot product per row — and see it as a layer
  • Predict the output shape of any matrix multiplication

Why linear algebra runs ML

Three facts explain why this small area of maths is at the heart of the whole field:

🧾

Data is vectors

A house becomes [bedrooms, area, age]. An image becomes a long list of pixel values. A word becomes an embedding: hundreds of numbers learned so that similar words get similar lists.

🧱

Models are matrices

A neural-network layer is a matrix of weights. Running the layer means multiplying the input vector by that matrix. A large language model is mostly thousands of matrix multiplications in a row.

⚡

Hardware is built for it

GPUs exist to do enormous numbers of multiply-and-add operations in parallel — exactly what matrix multiplication needs (Chapter 10).

So when this course says “compare these embeddings” or “this layer projects to 4,096 dimensions”, it's talking about the operations in this chapter.

Vectors: lists of numbers and arrows

A vector is an ordered list of numbers — exactly a 1-D NumPy array. Its length (how many numbers) is called its dimension. You can read it in two ways, and switching between them is the key skill:

🧾 As data

A customer described as [age, orders, days_since_last_visit] = [34, 12, 5]. Each position always means the same feature. 3 features, so it's a 3-dimensional vector.

➡️ As geometry

An arrow from the origin to the point (34, 12, 5) in 3-D space. Customers with similar behaviour sit close together and their arrows point in similar directions. With 768 numbers it's a 768-dimensional space — impossible to picture, but the maths works exactly the same.

Two basic operations work element by element:

  • Adding vectors adds matching positions: [1, 2] + [3, 1] = [4, 3]. Geometrically, put the second arrow's tail at the first arrow's head.
  • Scaling multiplies every number by the same amount: 2 × [1, 2] = [2, 4]. The arrow keeps its direction and doubles in length. A negative number flips it round.

These two operations are enough for a famous party trick of word embeddings: king − man + woman lands close to queen. The direction from “man” to “woman” is roughly the same as from “king” to “queen”, so adding that difference moves you between them (Chapter 15).

Length and distance

A vector's length, also called its norm and written ‖a‖, comes straight from Pythagoras. In 2-D, the arrow to (3, 4) is the long side of a right triangle with sides 3 and 4, so its length is √(3² + 4²) = √25 = 5. In more dimensions you just keep adding squares: ‖[1, 2, 2]‖ = √(1 + 4 + 4) = 3.

The distance between two vectors is the length of their difference. The distance between (1, 1) and (4, 5) is ‖(3, 4)‖ = 5. This straight-line distance is called Euclidean or L2 distance. You'll also meet L1 or Manhattan distance, which adds absolute differences instead (3 + 4 = 7) — like walking city blocks rather than flying straight.

import numpy as np
a = np.array([3.0, 4.0])
print(np.linalg.norm(a))                       # 5.0
b = np.array([1.0, 1.0]); c = np.array([4.0, 5.0])
print(np.linalg.norm(c - b))                   # 5.0   — L2 distance
print(np.abs(c - b).sum())                     # 7.0   — L1 distance
unit = a / np.linalg.norm(a)                    # [0.6 0.8] — same direction, length 1

That last line normalises a vector: divide by its length to get a unit vector (length 1) pointing the same way. You'll see normalisation constantly, because it lets you compare directions without lengths getting in the way. The k-nearest-neighbours model (Chapter 6) and vector databases (Chapter 20) are built entirely on distances like these.

📏
Scale changes distance

If one feature is income (tens of thousands) and another is age (tens), the income difference dominates every distance, and age is effectively ignored. That's why features are usually standardised before distance-based models — the one-liner from P2.

The dot product

The dot product of two vectors of the same length multiplies matching entries and adds the results:

[1, 2, 3] · [4, 5, 6] = 1×4 + 2×5 + 3×6 = 4 + 10 + 18 = 32

That looks like simple arithmetic, but it has a beautiful geometric meaning. It equals ‖a‖ × ‖b‖ × cos(angle between them). Cosine is 1 when the arrows point the same way, 0 at a right angle, and −1 when they point in opposite directions. So the dot product is one number that says how much two vectors agree, scaled by how long they are. Another way to see it: it's the length of a's “shadow” on b (its projection), times the length of b. Drag the sliders.

A worked example of why it's useful. A linear model predicts a house price as a dot product of weights and features: weights [50,000, 1,000, −2,000] (per bedroom, per m², per year of age) dotted with a house [3, 120, 20] gives 150,000 + 120,000 − 40,000 = £230,000. Each feature contributes its value times its weight. Nearly every model you'll meet starts with dot products like this.

Cosine similarity: comparing meanings

If you divide the dot product by both lengths, you're left with just the cosine of the angle. That's cosine similarity:

cos_sim(a, b) = (a · b) / (‖a‖ × ‖b‖)

It ranges from −1 (opposite) through 0 (unrelated) to 1 (same direction), ignoring how long the vectors are. That's exactly what you want for comparing meaning: a long document and a short one about the same topic should count as similar. Here are tiny 3-number “embeddings” for five words (real ones have hundreds of numbers, learned from data), and their pairwise cosine similarities:

Cosine similarity between toy word embeddingsTable
Green = similar (above 0.8), red = dissimilar (below 0.3). “cat” and “dog” point in almost the same direction; “car” is far from both. This is how semantic search finds documents that mean the same thing without sharing words (Chapter 20), and how attention decides which tokens are relevant to each other (Chapter 14).
def cosine(a, b):
    return a @ b / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b))   # @ on two 1-D arrays is the dot product

cat = np.array([0.9, 0.8, 0.1]); dog = np.array([0.8, 0.9, 0.15])
print(round(cosine(cat, dog), 3))                          # 0.992

A practical trick: if you normalise every vector to length 1 first, cosine similarity is just the dot product. Vector databases often store normalised embeddings for exactly this reason — a dot product is the cheapest comparison there is.

Matrices and matrix–vector multiplication

A matrix is a 2-D grid of numbers — a stack of row vectors. Its shape is written (rows, columns). Multiplying a matrix by a vector is simply one dot product per row. That's the whole idea.

W @ x, one row at a timeStep-through
  1. W has shape (2, 3): 2 rows, each with 3 numbers. x has 3 numbers. Every row of W must be the same length as x, or the dot products can't happen. The result has one number per row of W — so 2 numbers.
  2. Dot the first row with x: 1×4 + 0×5 + 2×6 = 4 + 0 + 12 = 16. This row “cares about” the first and third inputs and ignores the second.
  3. Dot the second row with x: 0×4 + 3×5 + 1×6 = 0 + 15 + 6 = 21. This row weighs the second input most heavily.
  4. In code it's one operator: W @ x gives [16 21]. The GPU does all the dot products in parallel — which is why GPUs are built for this (Ch 10).

This is exactly what a neural-network layer does. Each row of the weight matrix is one “pattern detector”, and each output number says how strongly the input matches that pattern. A layer with weight matrix of shape (4,096, 1,024) takes a 1,024-number input and produces 4,096 outputs — 4,096 dot products of length 1,024, about 4 million multiply-adds. Add a bias vector and a simple non-linear function, and you have the building block of every deep network (Chapter 8).

Seeing a matrix as a transformation

There's one more way to picture a matrix: as a machine that moves every point in space. A 2×2 matrix sends the point (x, y) to a new point. Watch what it does to a square and an arrow. Some matrices stretch, some rotate, some squash space flat.

Matrix times matrix, and the shape rule

Multiplying two matrices is the same idea repeated: every row of the first dotted with every column of the second. In ML this happens when you push a whole batch through a layer at once: stack 32 input vectors as the rows of X, and one multiplication handles all of them.

📐
The shape rule for matrix multiplication

For A @ B, the inner sizes must match, and they disappear from the result: (m, k) @ (k, n) → (m, n). So (32, 512) @ (512, 256) gives (32, 256), but (32, 512) @ (256, 512) is an error. Also: A * B is not matrix multiplication in NumPy — it multiplies matching entries one by one. Matrix multiply is @.

X = np.random.rand(32, 512)       # a batch: 32 examples, 512 features each
W = np.random.rand(512, 256)      # a layer: 512 inputs → 256 outputs
b = np.zeros(256)                  # one bias per output
H = X @ W + b                      # (32, 512) @ (512, 256) → (32, 256), then b broadcasts over the rows
print(H.shape)                     # (32, 256)
print(W.T.shape)                   # (256, 512) — transpose swaps rows and columns

Order matters: in general A @ B is not the same as B @ A (often one of them isn't even allowed). The transpose .T flips a matrix across its diagonal, turning rows into columns; you'll use it to make shapes line up. The identity matrix, with 1s on the diagonal and 0s elsewhere, leaves any vector unchanged — the matrix version of multiplying by 1.

Counting the work is also useful: an (m, k) @ (k, n) product does m × n dot products of length k, so about m × n × k multiply-adds, or 2mnk floating-point operations. For the example above: 2 × 32 × 256 × 512 ≈ 8.4 million. That kind of count is how you'll estimate training cost in P8 and Chapter 16.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Using * when you meant @. * multiplies element by element; @ is matrix multiplication. When shapes happen to broadcast, the wrong one runs silently.
  • Getting the inner dimensions backwards. (m, k) @ (k, n) works; (m, k) @ (n, k) needs a transpose: A @ B.T.
  • Comparing unnormalised vectors with a dot product. Long vectors win just for being long. Use cosine similarity, or normalise first.
  • Forgetting to scale features before distances. The feature with the biggest numbers dominates.
  • Assuming A @ B = B @ A. Matrix multiplication isn't commutative; order changes the answer.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What does a dot product of two embeddings tell you, and why do search systems often normalise embeddings first?

How a strong answer sounds
The dot product multiplies matching entries and adds them; geometrically it's the product of the two lengths and the cosine of the angle between them. So it's large when the vectors point the same way. For meaning we care about direction, not length, so we use cosine similarity — and if every embedding is normalised to length 1, the dot product equals the cosine. That turns comparison into the cheapest possible operation, which matters when you compare a query against millions of vectors.

Shapes: A batch of 16 sequences, each 128 tokens long with 768-dimensional embeddings, goes through a layer with a (768, 3072) weight matrix. What shape comes out, and roughly how many multiply-adds?

How a strong answer sounds
The input is (16, 128, 768). The layer multiplies the last axis: (…, 768) @ (768, 3072) → (16, 128, 3072). That's 16 × 128 = 2,048 token vectors, each doing 3,072 dot products of length 768: 2,048 × 3,072 × 768 ≈ 4.8 billion multiply-adds, or about 9.7 GFLOPs.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • A vector is a list of numbers: read it as data (features) or as an arrow in space. Add and scale element by element.
  • Length (norm) comes from Pythagoras; distance is the length of the difference. Normalise to get unit vectors; scale features before using distances.
  • The dot product multiplies and adds; it equals ‖a‖‖b‖cos θ, so it measures agreement. A linear model is a dot product of weights and features.
  • Cosine similarity divides out the lengths and compares direction only — the standard way to compare embeddings.
  • Matrix × vector is one dot product per row; that is exactly what a neural-network layer computes. A matrix can also be seen as a transformation of space.
  • Shape rule: (m, k) @ (k, n) → (m, n). Use @, not *. Cost ≈ 2mnk FLOPs.

Check your understanding

In NumPy, A has shape (3, 4) and B has shape (4, 2). What is the shape of A @ B?

The inner sizes (4 and 4) match and disappear: (3, 4) @ (4, 2) gives (3, 2). Each of the 3 rows of A is dotted with each of the 2 columns of B.

What is [2, −1, 3] · [4, 5, 1]?

Multiply matching entries and add: 2×4 + (−1)×5 + 3×1 = 8 − 5 + 3 = 6. The list [8, −5, 3] is the element-by-element product, before adding.

Two embedding vectors have cosine similarity close to 0. What does that suggest?

Cosine 1 means the same direction, −1 opposite, and 0 means a right angle: no shared direction. In embedding space that usually means the two items are unrelated.

What is the length (norm) of the vector [6, 8]?

√(6² + 8²) = √(36 + 64) = √100 = 10. (14 is the L1 length, 6 + 8.)

A layer has a weight matrix of shape (128, 64) and receives a batch X of shape (10, 64). Which expression gives an output of shape (10, 128)?

W.T has shape (64, 128), so (10, 64) @ (64, 128) → (10, 128). X @ W would be (10, 64) @ (128, 64) — the inner sizes don't match — and * isn't matrix multiplication.
