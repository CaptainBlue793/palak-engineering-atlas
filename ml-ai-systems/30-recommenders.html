Recommenders & Ranking Systems

Pick 20 items out of 200 million, personalised for this user, in 100 milliseconds. And do it without slowly turning your product into a feed where everything looks the same. Recommenders are the most commercially valuable ML systems there are. They are also where feedback loops (the model learning from its own past choices) hurt the most.

  • Build candidate generators, including two-tower retrieval
  • Handle implicit feedback, position bias and negative sampling
  • Balance relevance against diversity, freshness and business rules
  • Recognise feedback loops and design exploration to escape them

Why a funnel

You can't score 200 million items with a heavy model in 100 ms. So you use a chain of stages. Each stage handles fewer items with a more expensive model. Cheap and rough at the top, expensive and precise at the bottom. It's like hiring: a quick CV screen for thousands, then long interviews for a handful.

The arithmetic shows why there is no alternative. Say the heavy ranker needs about 0.06 ms per item when it scores items in a batch (the figure the simulator below uses). For 300 candidates that is 300 × 0.06 = 18 ms. For a 50-million-item catalogue it is 50,000,000 × 0.06 ms = 3,000,000 ms, which is 50 minutes per request. Retrieval exists to turn that 50 million into about a thousand, using a model cheap enough to search the whole catalogue in under 10 ms.

A typical feed request flows like this: retrieval returns about 1,000 candidates; a filter removes items already seen, blocked or out of stock; the heavy ranker scores a few hundred; re-ranking picks and orders the final 20 or so that fit on screen.

Retrieval sets the ceiling

The ranker can only sort what retrieval gives it. If the perfect item isn't among the 1,000 candidates, no clever ranking can bring it back. Teams often spend months tuning the ranker while retrieval quietly drops the best item. So treat candidate recall as a core metric. It measures how often the item the user eventually engaged with was in the candidate set.

Measuring it is simple. Take 1,000 logged sessions where the user clicked something. Re-run retrieval for each one and check whether the clicked item is among the returned candidates. If it is there in 620 of them, candidate recall is 62%. That means that in 38% of sessions, the ranker never had a chance.

Candidate generation

Retrieval is rarely one model. Real systems run several candidate sources side by side, each good at finding a different kind of item, and merge their results. For example: 400 from two-tower retrieval, 300 from “similar to what you watched last”, 200 from people you follow, 100 trending. Mixing sources is cheap insurance: if one source misses a whole category, another usually catches it.

Two-tower, precisely

You train two encoders: one for the user and context, one for the item. They're trained so that the dot product of the two vectors (a simple similarity score) predicts relevance. Training uses in-batch negatives: the other items in the same batch act as "wrong answers" (Chapter 15).

Item vectors are computed nightly and put in a search index. At request time you embed the user once and run an ANN query. Keeping the two sides separate is the whole point: the item tower can be computed offline. That's what makes millisecond search over hundreds of millions of items possible.

A tiny example with 3-number embeddings. The user tower outputs u = [0.9, 0.1, 0.4] (say: likes cooking, not sport, some travel). A baking video has v₁ = [0.8, 0.0, 0.2]; a football clip has v₂ = [0.1, 0.9, 0.0]. The scores are u·v₁ = 0.72 + 0 + 0.08 = 0.80 and u·v₂ = 0.09 + 0.09 + 0 = 0.18. The baking video is retrieved. Real embeddings have 64–256 numbers, but the calculation is the same.

The cost: user and item features never meet before the dot product. So subtle combinations of the two ("this user likes this kind of item at night") have to wait for the ranker.

Ranking

The ranker sees only a few hundred candidates, so it can afford hundreds of features per item. These include user history, item statistics, context (time, device, session) and combinations of them. Modern rankers are usually multi-task: one model predicts several outcomes at once, such as click, like and share.

# one model, several heads — trained jointly, combined at serving
p_click, p_watch_30s, p_like, p_share, p_hide = model(user, item, context)

score = w1·p_click + w2·p_watch_30s + w3·p_like + w4·p_share − w5·p_hide

# the weights encode product strategy, and they are argued about constantly.
# optimising p_click alone is how you get clickbait.

Why predict several outcomes instead of one? Because each is a partial, noisy view of “the user liked it”, and they fail in different ways. A worked example with weights w = (1, 2, 4, 8, 20):

  • Clickbait video: p_click 0.30, p_watch_30s 0.05, p_like 0.01, p_share 0.00, p_hide 0.04. Score = 0.30 + 0.10 + 0.04 + 0 − 0.80 = −0.36.
  • Solid tutorial: p_click 0.12, p_watch_30s 0.10, p_like 0.04, p_share 0.01, p_hide 0.00. Score = 0.12 + 0.20 + 0.16 + 0.08 − 0 = 0.56.

By click probability alone, the clickbait wins easily (0.30 vs 0.12). Once watching, liking and hiding count, the tutorial wins. The rarer and more deliberate an action is, the larger its weight usually is.

🎣
Your objective becomes your product

A feed that optimises clicks gets clickbait. One that optimises watch time gets longer, slower videos. One that optimises engagement gets outrage. This isn't a modelling failure. The system is doing exactly what you asked.

Teams keep the goal pointed at what they actually want with three tools. Multi-task heads with weights the team agrees on openly. Explicit negative signals (hide, report, “not interested”). And long-term metrics like next-week retention.

Position bias and implicit feedback

Your training labels are clicks. Clicks are implicit feedback: users never told you what they liked, you infer it from behaviour. And clicks depend hugely on where an item was shown, because people mostly click what's at the top. This is position bias. Train on clicks naively and the model learns that whatever it already ranked first is good.

Here is the correction with numbers. Suppose users look at position 1 on 90% of visits and at position 5 on only 30%. Item A sits at position 1 and gets 9 clicks per 100 impressions. Item B sits at position 5 and gets 3 clicks per 100. Raw click rates say A is three times better. But divide each by the chance anyone looked: A = 0.09 / 0.90 = 0.10, B = 0.03 / 0.30 = 0.10. Given that a user actually saw them, they were equally good. That division is inverse propensity weighting; the “chance of looking” is the propensity.

Ways to correct it: inverse propensity weighting (divide each label by the estimated chance that the user even looked at that position). Or add position as a feature in training, then set it to a fixed value at serving time. Or randomly swap positions for a small slice of traffic to measure the curve honestly.

BiasWhat it doesMitigation

Negative sampling

You see positives (clicks). But the model also needs negatives, examples of what the user didn't want, and you have to pick them yourself. That choice changes the model a lot:

  • Random negatives are too easy and teach little. A random item for a baking fan is probably a car review; the model learns to tell baking from cars, which it already knew.
  • In-batch negatives (other items in the same training batch) are convenient, but lean toward popular items.
  • Hard negatives, items shown but not clicked, teach the most. They are also the most distorted by position bias: an item shown at position 9 and not clicked may simply never have been seen.

Most production systems mix all three.

Re-ranking: diversity, freshness, rules

Ranking purely by relevance often gives a bad feed: five near-identical items, all from one creator, all three weeks old. So the final stage reshuffles the top candidates to follow some rules.

The simulator below uses a common greedy method. Fill the feed one slot at a time. For each remaining item, take its relevance and subtract a penalty that grows with how many already-picked items share its category or creator. Pick the item with the best adjusted score, then repeat. With λ = 0.5, after “Sourdough starter guide” (bread, creator A) is picked first:

  • “No-knead bread” (0.90, bread, creator A) is penalised 0.5 × (0.18 + 0.22) = 0.20, leaving 0.70.
  • “20-minute weeknight curry” (0.81, different category and creator) has no penalty: 0.81.

So the curry takes slot 2, even though its raw relevance is lower. Rules like freshness boosts (“at least two items under 24 hours old”) and business limits (“at most one sponsored item in the top five”) are applied in the same final pass.

Cold start and exploration

🆕

New item

No interactions yet, so "users like you" signals are empty. Use content embeddings, what you know about the creator, and a deliberate budget of impressions so it can earn some data.

👤

New user

Show what's popular for their region and device, ask about preferences during sign-up, and adapt quickly to signals from the first session. The first ten minutes decide whether they come back.

🎲

Exploration

Give a small share of slots to a bandit algorithm (Thompson sampling or UCB), which balances trying new items against showing known good ones, instead of the ranker. Without it, the system can only learn about what it already shows.

What an impression budget looks like in practice: every new item is guaranteed, say, 500 impressions to users whose content embedding says they might like it. After those 500 showings it has a measured click and watch rate, and the normal ranker takes over. Items that do well keep being shown; items that flop fade out. The cost is small (a few slots per feed), and without it the ranker would never pick an item with zero history over one with proven numbers.

🌀
The feedback loop, stated precisely

The model chooses what is shown → users interact with what is shown → that interaction becomes tomorrow's training data → the model becomes more confident in its own choices.

Left alone, this kills diversity, locks in popular items, and makes the system blind to anything it stopped showing. The defences are built into the system: exploration traffic, logging impressions (what was shown, not just what was clicked), off-policy evaluation, and diversity guardrails. Monitor those guardrails as SLOs (targets with alerts), don't just tune them once.

Measuring it

A recommender needs metrics at every layer, because a problem in one layer is invisible from another. Two offline metrics need a word of explanation:

  • Recall@k — of the items the user really engaged with, what fraction appear in your top k? If the user clicked 4 items and 3 of them are in your top 10, recall@10 = 0.75.
  • NDCG (normalised discounted cumulative gain) — rewards putting relevant items high in the list. Each relevant item earns less the lower it sits (divided by log₂(position + 1)), and the total is divided by the best possible score, so 1.0 means a perfect order. A relevant item at position 1 earns 1.0; at position 3 it earns 1 / log₂4 = 0.5.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Tuning the ranker while retrieval drops the answer. Measure candidate recall first.
  • Training on clicks without position correction. The model learns to copy yesterday's ranking.
  • Only logging clicks. Without impressions you can't compute propensities, negatives or off-policy estimates.
  • Optimising a single engagement metric. You'll hit it, in the most literal and least useful way.
  • No exploration. The system becomes confidently blind to everything it doesn't already show.
  • Ignoring cold start. New items never collect data, so the catalogue quietly shrinks to what was popular a year ago.

Interview drill

Warm-up: Why separate retrieval from ranking instead of using one model?

How a strong answer sounds
Compute. A ranker with hundreds of features that combines user and item details costs milliseconds per item. You can't run it over 200 million items in a 100 ms budget. Retrieval uses a factorised model: the item side can be computed in advance and indexed for approximate nearest-neighbour search. That gives candidates in under 10 ms, at the cost of not modelling how user and item features interact. The funnel lets each stage use the most expensive model it can afford for the number of items it sees. The key metric linking them is candidate recall, because the ranker can only sort what retrieval returns.

Deeper: Engagement is up 8% after a launch, but you're uneasy. What do you check?

How a strong answer sounds
Whether the gain is the kind you want. I'd break the 8% down by surface and user group. Is it concentrated in a few heavy users, or spread widely? I'd check the negative signals that engagement doesn't show: hides, reports, unfollows, “not interested”, and satisfaction per session. I'd check the health guardrails: catalogue coverage, creator concentration, share of impressions going to new items, topic diversity. Then I'd look further ahead, at next-week and next-month retention. Short-term engagement gains from more sensational content often reverse. And I'd check whether the metric moved for a mechanical reason, like more items per screen or an autoplay change, rather than better recommendations. If the long-term metrics are flat and the guardrails moved the wrong way, that 8% is a cost, not a win.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Use a funnel: cheap rough retrieval, then expensive precise ranking, then re-ranking with rules.
  • Two-tower models make personalised retrieval over hundreds of millions of items possible.
  • Implicit feedback is biased by position and exposure. Correct it, or the model just learns your own ranking.
  • Multi-task objectives with explicit weights keep the system aimed at the outcome you want.
  • Exploration, diversity guardrails and cold-start handling are what stop the feedback loop closing in.

Check your understanding

Your ranker is excellent but engagement is flat. Candidate recall@1000 is 0.42. What do you fix?

Retrieval sets the upper limit. No ranking improvement can show an item that was never retrieved.

Why can the item tower of a two-tower model be computed offline?

Splitting the model in two is the whole trick. Compute and index item vectors in advance, embed the user once at request time, and let ANN search do the rest. The price: user and item features can't interact before the dot product.

Training a ranker on raw click logs without position correction leads to what?

Position dominates the click signal. More data just makes the model more confident in the same self-confirming pattern. That's exactly why propensity correction and random swaps exist.

Which signal is most useful for escaping a recommender's feedback loop?

The loop is a data problem: you only learn about what you show. Only deliberate exploration gives you information about the rest of the catalogue.

You optimise purely for click probability. What's the predictable outcome?

The system optimises exactly what you asked for. The standard defence is multi-task objectives, with negative signals and long-term metrics.
