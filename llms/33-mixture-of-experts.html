Mixture of Experts

In a dense transformer every token goes through every weight. A mixture of experts (MoE) replaces each MLP with several MLPs, the experts, and a small router that sends each token to only a few of them. The model gains parameters, and so capacity, without each token costing more compute. Mixtral, DeepSeek-V3 and the Qwen3 MoE models all work this way.

This chapter builds an MoE layer from scratch and trains six versions of Chapter 8's tiny GPT for the same 4,000 steps. With 4.6 times the parameters and the same compute per token, 32 small experts with 4 active per token reached a validation loss of 1.703 against the dense model's 1.783. The router showed MoE's characteristic problem: top-1 routing collapsed, sending 99.7% of one layer's tokens to a single expert, and even with a balancing loss it only partly recovered. Here it all ran slower than dense, because a simple implementation loops over experts.

Sparse MLPs and routing

The MLP is where most of a transformer's parameters live, and where much of what it knows is thought to be stored. An MoE layer keeps E separate MLPs and a router, a single linear layer that gives every token a score for every expert. A softmax turns the scores into probabilities; the token goes to its top k experts, and its output is their outputs weighted by those probabilities:

def forward(self, x):                                       # x: (tokens, d)
    probs = self.router(x).float().softmax(-1)                   # (tokens, E)
    w, idx = probs.topk(self.k, dim=-1)                         # each token's k experts
    if self.k > 1:
        w = w / w.sum(-1, keepdim=True)                         # renormalise over the chosen k
    out = torch.zeros_like(x)
    for e, expert in enumerate(self.experts):
        tok, slot = (idx == e).nonzero(as_tuple=True)             # the tokens routed to expert e
        out.index_add_(0, tok, w[tok, slot, None] * expert(x[tok]))
    return out

With 8 experts of the dense model's size and k = 1, the layer holds 8 times the MLP's parameters but each token passes through one expert, so the compute is the same as dense. Total parameters grow; active parameters, the ones a token uses, do not.

Load balancing

A router learns, and it can learn to send everything to the experts that are already good, which then get more training and become better still. This is router collapse. It was measured by the share of each layer's tokens that went to its busiest expert, where 12.5% would be perfectly even for 8 experts:

32 experts, top-4, balancing loss 0.01 (even: 3.1%)17%8.1%8.2%7.7%7.7%

Without a balancing loss, top-1 routing collapsed completely by step 500: in at least one layer every token went to one expert, so that layer was really a dense MLP with seven idle copies. The Switch Transformer's balancing loss, E × Σ (share of tokens sent to expert e) × (average router probability for e), pushes towards an even split. At a weight of 0.01 it did not stop the early collapse but slowly pulled it back, to 61% by the end. Top-2 and fine-grained routing were far more stable, because sending each token to several experts gives the losing ones gradient too. Surprisingly, the fully collapsed run had a slightly lower loss than the balanced one, 1.749 against 1.763; nothing here limited how many tokens an expert could take, so collapse cost capacity but no tokens. That changes as soon as experts have a capacity.

Memory vs compute

In large-scale training each expert usually lives on a different GPU and can take a fixed number of tokens per batch, its capacity, set as a factor times an even share; tokens beyond it are dropped (passed through unchanged). Measured on validation batches, the share of routing assignments a capacity limit would drop:

Capacity factor1.01.252.0
8 experts, top-1, no balancing loss41%35%21%
8 experts, top-1, balancing loss18%11%5.9%
8 experts, top-215%6.3%0.5%

The collapsed router would have dropped 41% of its tokens at capacity 1.0, which is why balancing is not optional at scale. Newer models also balance without an extra loss (DeepSeek-V3 adjusts a per-expert bias) and drop no tokens at inference.

The other cost is memory and speed. Every expert must be stored, so the MoE models held 63.5 million parameters to use 14 million per token, and their training peaked at 4.3 to 5.4 GB against 3.3 GB. They also trained 2 to 4 times slower, because this implementation loops over experts and gathers each one's tokens separately: many small matrix multiplies instead of one large one. Production systems use grouped matrix-multiply kernels and expert parallelism to recover most of that. For serving, an MoE needs the memory of its total parameters but the bandwidth of its active ones, which is why it suits large multi-GPU deployments better than one consumer GPU. Training a small MoE

What did the experts learn? For one layer of each 8-expert model, the tokens each expert took far more often than average were listed. They specialised by kind of token, not by topic: in the top-1 model one expert took prepositions and particles ("up", "around", "out", "in"), another verbs of wanting and trying ("want", "tried", "wanted"), another possessives ("your", "his", "my", "their"), another subject pronouns ("I", "They", "We", "You"), and another story openers ("Once", "One", "every"). The Mixtral paper reports the same: routing follows syntax and token type far more than subject matter, even across very different domains. "Expert" is a misleading name; they are partitions of the computation, chosen by a router to be useful, not specialists a person would recognise.

Use case: more model for the same serving cost

Many of the strongest open models are now MoEs. DeepSeek-V3 activates about 37 billion of its 671 billion parameters per token; Qwen3-30B-A3B about 3 of 30. For a provider serving many users on a cluster, that means near-large-model quality at a fraction of the compute per token, because decoding at scale is limited by compute and bandwidth per token. The measurements here are the fine print: the whole model must still fit in memory, which on one GPU often makes a dense model of the active size the better choice, balancing needs care during training, and the speed gain depends on kernels and expert parallelism that a simple implementation does not have.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Training top-1 routing without balancing; one expert took 100% of a layer's tokens by step 500.
  • Assuming a balancing loss prevents collapse; at 0.01 it only pulled the busiest expert back from 99.7% to 61%.
  • Confusing total and active parameters; 63.5 million stored for 14 million used per token.
  • Expecting MoE to be faster on one GPU; a loop over experts trained 2 to 4 times slower here.
  • Ignoring capacity; a collapsed router would drop 41% of tokens at capacity 1.0.
  • Reading experts as topic specialists; they split by token type: pronouns, prepositions, openers.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is a mixture of experts, and why use one?

How a strong answer sounds
Each MLP in the transformer is replaced by several expert MLPs and a router, a linear layer that scores the experts for every token; the token goes to its top k and the output is their probability-weighted sum. Parameters grow with the number of experts but compute per token only with k, so you get more capacity for the same compute. In my runs on a tiny GPT, 32 small experts with 4 active, 4.6 times the parameters at the same compute, reached validation loss 1.703 against 1.783 for the dense model, a bigger gain than doubling the dense MLP. Real examples: Mixtral uses 12.9 of 46.7 billion parameters per token, Qwen3-30B-A3B 3.35 of 30.5 billion. The costs are memory for all experts, load balancing, and kernels that make sparse compute efficient.

Deeper: What is router collapse and how do you prevent it?

How a strong answer sounds
The router is trained too, and it can reinforce itself: experts that get more tokens improve, so they get even more, until one expert takes everything and the layer is effectively dense. In my top-1 run without a balancing loss, one layer sent 100% of tokens to one expert by step 500. The standard fix is an auxiliary loss, E times the sum over experts of the fraction of tokens routed there times the mean router probability, which is minimised by an even split; at weight 0.01 it pulled the busiest expert back from 99.7% to 61% over training. Routing to several experts helps a lot: top-2 stayed near 28% and 32 experts with top-4 near 8%. With capacity limits, collapse also drops tokens, 41% in my measurement against 14% for the fine-grained model, so I would use top-k above 1, a balancing loss or DeepSeek-style bias adjustment, and monitor per-expert load.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • An MoE layer routes each token to k of E expert MLPs: parameters grow with E, compute with k.
  • At the same compute as dense, 32 small experts with 4 active lowered the loss from 1.783 to 1.703.
  • Top-1 routing collapsed to one expert without a balancing loss, and only partly recovered with one.
  • Routing to several experts kept loads stable and cut capacity drops to 0.4% at factor 2.
  • MoEs need memory for every expert and good kernels; a simple loop trained 2 to 4 times slower.
  • Experts specialise by token type, not topic.
