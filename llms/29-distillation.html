Distillation

A large model is expensive to serve; a small one is cheap but worse. Distillation trains the small model, the student, to imitate the large one, the teacher, using the teacher's outputs or its full probability distributions as training targets. Many of the small open models in use were made this way.

This chapter distils Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct into the Qwen2.5-0.5B base model three ways, on the same 4,000 prompts: from human-written replies (ordinary SFT, the comparison), from the teacher's own replies, and from the teacher's next-token probabilities. Judged by a reward model, SFT on human replies closed 23% of the gap between the untrained student and the teacher; the teacher's replies closed 68%, and its probabilities 71%. The students also inherited exactly what the data showed them and nothing else: they did the teacher's arithmetic as well as the teacher, but ignored the instruction to reply with only the number every time.

Hard labels vs soft targets

Ordinary training uses hard labels: at each position the target is the one token that came next, and every other token is equally wrong. A teacher knows more than that. Where the next word could be "framework", "library" or "tool", its distribution says so, and that this is a near-tie, not a mistake. Hinton, Vinyals and Dean's 2015 paper called this the "dark knowledge" in the probabilities a model assigns to wrong answers. Training on the teacher's full distribution, soft targets, gives the student far more information per token than one right answer does.

Logit distillation

Logit distillation runs the teacher and the student on the same text and trains the student to match the teacher's next-token distribution at every position, by minimising the cross-entropy from the teacher's probabilities to the student's (equivalently, the KL divergence, since the teacher's own entropy is a constant). Qwen's vocabulary has 151,936 entries; storing all of them per token is wasteful, so the teacher's distribution was cut to its top 64 tokens and renormalised, which keeps nearly all of its mass:

with torch.no_grad():
    tl = teacher.lm_head(t_hidden)                              # teacher logits on the reply's positions
    tv, ti = tl.topk(64, -1); tp = tv.softmax(-1)           # its top 64 tokens, renormalised
sl = student.lm_head(s_hidden).float()
log_q = sl.gather(1, ti) - sl.logsumexp(-1, keepdim=True)   # student log-probs of those tokens
loss = -(tp * log_q).sum() / n_tokens                           # cross-entropy to the soft targets

The first version stored the student's full log-softmax for the backward pass and ran out of memory with both models on the GPU; computing the log-probabilities of just the 64 tokens as gather minus logsumexp fixed it. The cost is the teacher's forward pass at every step: the logit student took 289 seconds to train against 130 for the others.

Distilling from generated data

The simpler method, sequence-level distillation, needs only the teacher's text. The teacher answers the training prompts, and the student is fine-tuned on those answers with an ordinary SFT loss. It works with a teacher behind an API that returns no probabilities, and with a different tokenizer, which logit distillation cannot handle. The teacher answered the 4,000 Dolly prompts here in 13 minutes (with Qwen's default decoding: greedy, repetition penalty 1.1, up to 256 tokens). All three students were then full fine-tunes of the 0.5B base model for one epoch of 250 steps of 16 conversations, learning rate 10⁻⁵, the same recipe as Chapter 19.

Asked for the top JavaScript frameworks, the SFT student listed a few and then repeated "Backbone.js" until it ran out of tokens, a greedy-decoding loop the teacher-trained students avoided. The logit student added as well as the teacher, but every reply was a sentence, "The sum of 964 and 494 is 1458.", although the teacher itself answered with just the number every time. The students learnt the teacher's behaviour on the prompts they were shown, Dolly questions that never ask for a bare answer, where the teacher writes full explanations; they learnt nothing about the instruction-following the teacher shows elsewhere. The SFT student, trained on Dolly's terse human answers, did follow the format. A student inherits the teacher's behaviour on the distillation data's distribution, not the teacher. To pass on a skill, put prompts that need it in the distillation set.

The first grader for these additions read the first number in the reply, so "The sum of 964 and 494 is 1458" scored as 964 and both distilled students showed 0% arithmetic. Splitting the question into "is the sum there" and "is the format followed" showed what had actually happened.

Licences and terms

Distillation raises a question SFT on your own data does not: are you allowed to train on another model's outputs? Here the teacher, Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, and the student, Qwen2.5-0.5B, are both released under Apache 2.0, and the Dolly prompts under CC BY-SA 3.0, so the student can be shared under those terms. That is not true of every model. Some open models come with licences that restrict using their outputs to improve other models (Qwen2.5-3B, for example, uses a research licence, not Apache 2.0), and the terms of service of most commercial APIs forbid using outputs to develop competing models. Logit distillation also needs the weights or at least full probabilities, which APIs rarely expose. Before distilling, check the teacher's licence and terms of use as carefully as the training data's, and record which teacher produced which data.

Use case: a small, cheap model for one product

A team serving a high-volume feature, say classifying and summarising support tickets, cannot afford a large model on every request. A common path is to have a large model answer a few thousand real, representative requests, then fine-tune a small model on its answers; the small model serves the traffic at a fraction of the cost. Open models have been built the same way: Meta's smaller Llama 3.2 models used larger models' outputs as targets, and DeepSeek-R1's smaller "distill" models were fine-tuned on the large model's reasoning traces. The measurements here are the checklist for doing it well: the student matches the teacher only on the kinds of prompt it was distilled on, so the prompt set must cover every behaviour the product needs, format instructions included, and quality must be checked by something other than a copy of the teacher.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Distilling on prompts that miss a needed behaviour; the students ignored "only the number" every time, though the teacher obeyed.
  • Judging the student with a model built from the teacher; it may favour teacher-like replies.
  • Storing the full softmax for the backward pass; it ran out of memory with both models on the GPU.
  • Grading a skill and a format with one number; "first number in the reply" showed 0% for students that added correctly.
  • Ignoring the teacher's licence or API terms when using its outputs as training data.
  • Expecting soft labels to come free; the teacher's forward pass more than doubled training time.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is knowledge distillation, and why do soft targets help?

How a strong answer sounds
Distillation trains a small student to imitate a large teacher. The simplest form is sequence-level: the teacher answers a set of prompts and the student is fine-tuned on those answers. Logit distillation goes further: on the same text, the student is trained to match the teacher's whole next-token distribution, by cross-entropy to the teacher's probabilities. Those soft targets carry much more information than one correct token, since they say which alternatives were nearly as good. In my experiment, distilling Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct into the 0.5B base model, SFT on human replies closed 23% of the reward gap between the untrained student and the teacher, the teacher's replies 68%, and its top-64 probabilities 71%, with the lowest KL to the teacher, 0.358.

Deeper: Your distilled student matches the teacher on your eval but users say it ignores formatting instructions. What happened?

How a strong answer sounds
The student learns the teacher's behaviour on the distillation prompts, not the teacher's general abilities. In my run the students were distilled on open-ended Dolly questions, where the teacher writes full explanations; asked an addition with "reply with only the number", the logit student was right 95% of the time, as often as the teacher, but wrote a sentence every time, while the teacher answered with just the number. I would add prompts with format constraints and other instructions to the distillation set, or mix in instruction-following SFT data, and evaluate format compliance separately from correctness. I would also check the judge: mine was trained from the teacher's own model, which can make teacher-like replies look better than they are.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Distillation trains a small student on a teacher's replies (hard labels) or probabilities (soft targets).
  • Soft targets carry more per token; here they gave the lowest KL to the teacher (0.358) and the highest reward.
  • Reward gap closed: human-reply SFT 23%, teacher's replies 68%, teacher's probabilities 71%.
  • Students inherit behaviour on the distillation prompts only: right sums, but never the requested format.
  • Logit distillation needs the teacher's weights or full probabilities and a shared tokenizer, and costs a teacher pass per step.
  • Check the teacher's licence and terms before training on its outputs.
