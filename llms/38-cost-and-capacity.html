Training & Serving Cost

Almost every decision about a language model is eventually a cost decision: how large a model to train, on how many tokens, whether to fine-tune or prompt, whether to host it or call an API. The arithmetic is short, and with a few measured numbers it is accurate enough to plan with.

This chapter measures them on this machine's GPU. Training GPT-2 small reached 29.7 trillion operations a second, 31% of the card's measured peak, drawing 210 W, so one million training tokens cost 0.0017 kWh. Serving a 0.5B model one request at a time used 1.14 joules per generated token, with the card mostly idle at 88 W; serving 32 at once used 0.043 joules, 26 times less. Batching, not the hardware, set the cost per token. The calculator below turns these into GPU-hours and cost for your own models and prices.

FLOPs for a training run

A forward pass costs about 2N floating-point operations per token for a model with N parameters: one multiply and one add per weight (P6 checked this against PyTorch's counter). The backward pass costs about twice the forward, so training costs about 6N operations per token, and a run on D tokens about 6ND. Attention adds a term that grows with the sequence length, 12 × layers × sequence × width per token, which for GPT-2 at 1,024 tokens is 15% on top. For GPT-3 (175 billion parameters, 300 billion tokens) 6ND is 3.2 × 10²³ operations; for a 70-billion-parameter model trained on 15 trillion tokens, 6.3 × 10²⁴. Chapter 11's scaling laws say how to split a budget between N and D; at the compute-optimal point it is roughly 20 tokens per parameter, though most current models are trained far past it, because a smaller model trained longer is cheaper to serve.

GPU-hours and utilisation

Operations become time through the achieved throughput, which is always well below the card's peak. Measured here, with bf16 autocast and fused AdamW, against this card's measured bf16 peak of 96.6 trillion operations a second:

Model FLOPs utilisation (MFU), the useful operations divided by the peak, is the number to plan with. Large, well-tuned training runs report 35 to 55%; small models and unoptimised code get less. GPT-2 here ran without Chapter 13's compilation, which reached 70,000 tokens a second, so the same run can be made twice as cheap by engineering alone. With these numbers, training GPT-2 small on 2.5 billion tokens, about 20 per parameter, would take 2.5 billion ÷ 34,578 tokens a second ≈ 20 hours on this card and use about 4.2 kWh. GPT-3's 3.2 × 10²³ operations at the same rate would take 340 years, which is why such runs use thousands of GPUs for weeks. GPU-hours are operations divided by achieved throughput; cost is GPU-hours times the price per hour; and the price per hour is the number that changes most between providers and contracts.

Serving cost per million tokens

Serving is priced per token, and its cost per token is set by how many tokens the GPU produces while it is being paid for. Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct decoding 128 tokens per request with Hugging Face's generate, at three batch sizes:

Thirty-two requests at once produced 31 times the tokens for 16% more power. At batch 1 the card drew 88 W of its rated 300, because each decoding step mostly waits on memory and kernel launches (Chapters 16 and 26), so a GPU serving one user at a time is paid for but mostly idle. Since GPUs are usually rented by the hour, cost per million tokens is the hourly price divided by the millions of tokens produced in that hour: at batch 32, a card costing p per hour serves a million tokens for p ÷ 8.5; at batch 1, for 3.6p. Electricity is the smaller part: at 0.012 to 0.32 kWh per million tokens it is a fraction of a typical hourly rental. Real serving engines with continuous batching (Chapter 26) keep the batch full, which is where the economics of large providers come from.

Self-hosting vs an API

The comparison is not the API's price per token against the GPU's price per hour; it is against the GPU's price per hour divided by the tokens you actually generate in that hour. A self-hosted model at high, steady load can be far cheaper per token than an API; the same model serving bursty traffic for a few users can be far more expensive, because the hours are paid for whether or not anyone is asking. Then add what the hourly rate leaves out: the engineer time to run the server, redundancy for when it fails, and the model quality you give up by choosing one that fits your hardware. Self-hosting wins with steady high volume, privacy or latency requirements, or a fine-tuned model an API cannot serve; an API wins for spiky or small volume and when the best available model matters most. The calculator does the break-even for your own numbers.

Planning a budget

A budget is honest when it states its assumptions. For training: N, D, the MFU you assume (measure it on a short run first; here 31 to 42%), the price per GPU-hour, and a margin for failed runs and experiments, which in practice often cost as much as the final run. For serving: the expected tokens per day, the batch size your traffic allows at your latency target, the measured tokens per second at that batch, and the hours you pay for whether used or not. Every number in this chapter came from a few minutes of measurement, and each one moved the result by a large factor: batch size by 26, compilation by 2, utilisation by 3 between a good and a poor run. Use case: should we host our own model?

A team spending heavily on an API asks whether to serve an open model itself. The measurements here are the questions to answer first. What batch will the traffic support at the latency target, and what does the model produce at that batch on the intended hardware? What share of the paid hours will be busy, given daily peaks and quiet nights? What is the engineering cost of running, monitoring and updating the server, and what quality is lost by moving to a model that fits? Teams that answer these with a week of measured traffic usually find the answer is clear one way or the other; teams that compare list prices usually get it wrong.

Common mistakes
  • Planning at the GPU's peak; measured utilisation here was 31 to 42%.
  • Pricing serving at batch 1; batch 32 used 26 times less energy per token.
  • Comparing an API's per-token price with a GPU's hourly price without dividing by real, busy throughput.
  • Leaving out failed runs and experiments from a training budget.
  • Forgetting the attention term at long sequences; it added 15% for GPT-2 at 1,024 tokens.
  • Assuming hardware sets the cost; batching and compilation moved it by 26 and 2 times here.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How would you estimate the cost of training a 7-billion-parameter model on 1 trillion tokens?

How a strong answer sounds
Training costs about 6 operations per parameter per token, so 6 × 7 × 10⁹ × 10¹² = 4.2 × 10²² operations, plus a little for attention at long sequences. Then divide by the achieved throughput, not the peak: measure the model FLOPs utilisation on a short run; on my GPU it was 31% for GPT-2 and 42% for a smaller model, and well-tuned large runs reach 40 to 50%. On a GPU with a dense bf16 peak of 1,000 TFLOPS at 40% MFU, that is 4.2 × 10²² / 4 × 10¹⁴ ≈ 1.05 × 10⁸ GPU-seconds, about 29,000 GPU-hours, or about 15 days on 80 GPUs. Multiply by the hourly price, then add a large margin, often comparable to the run itself, for experiments, restarts and evaluation.

Deeper: What determines the cost per million tokens of serving a model?

How a strong answer sounds
The price of the hardware per hour divided by the tokens it actually produces in that hour. Tokens per hour depend overwhelmingly on batch size, because decoding one sequence leaves the GPU waiting on memory: I measured a 0.5B model at 77 tokens a second at batch 1 and 2,351 at batch 32 on the same card, with power rising only from 88 to 102 W, so energy per token fell from 1.14 to 0.043 joules. Then utilisation: hours paid for but idle multiply the cost. So the levers are continuous batching and paged KV memory to keep batches full, quantisation and a smaller or MoE model to raise tokens per second, and matching capacity to traffic; latency targets limit how far batching can go.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Training costs about 6ND operations; attention adds a sequence-length term (15% for GPT-2 at 1,024).
  • Plan with measured utilisation: 31 to 42% of peak here, not 100%.
  • GPU-hours = operations ÷ achieved rate; cost = GPU-hours × price, plus a margin for experiments.
  • Serving cost per token is set by batch size: 1.14 J per token at batch 1, 0.043 at batch 32.
  • Compare self-hosting and APIs on busy throughput and utilisation, not list prices.
  • Measure a few minutes first; batching, compilation and utilisation each changed costs by large factors.
