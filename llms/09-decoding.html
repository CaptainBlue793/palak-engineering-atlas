Decoding: Greedy, Temperature, Top-k & Top-p

A language model produces a probability for every token; it never says which one to write. That choice is made by a few lines of code outside the model, the decoding rule, and the same model can be repetitive, creative or incoherent depending only on those lines.

This chapter writes every common rule from scratch, checks each against Hugging Face's implementation, and measures them on Qwen2.5-0.5B continuing 30 Wikipedia passages. Always picking the most likely token sounds safest, but it fell into a loop in 73% of continuations, and beam search, which searches harder for likely text, in 40%. Sampling at temperature 0.6 or above removed the loops entirely, and above 1.0 the text dissolved into nonsense. For short factual answers, temperature mattered far less: accuracy on 3-digit addition stayed between 81% and 84% from greedy up to temperature 1.0.

Greedy and its loops

Greedy decoding takes the most probable token every time. It is deterministic and often fine for a few words, but over a paragraph it repeats. Each repetition makes the next one more likely, because the repeated text is now in the context. To measure it, Qwen2.5-0.5B continued 30 WikiText passages by 200 tokens with each rule. Two measures of repetition are used: the share of 4-token sequences that already appeared earlier in the same continuation, and the share of continuations whose last 100 tokens are more than half repeats, which here means stuck in a loop.

Greedy decoding repeated 61% of its 4-grams and was looping in 22 of 30 continuations. Real Wikipedia text continuing the same passages repeats 1.9%. Given a sentence about Du Fu's influence on Japanese poetry, greedy Qwen finished the sentence, then wrote the whole sentence again word for word, and then drifted into the format of a quiz it had seen in training.

Temperature

Sampling instead draws the next token at random according to its probability, so a 30% token is chosen about 30% of the time. Temperature reshapes the distribution first by dividing the logits by T: below 1 sharpens it towards the top token (T → 0 is greedy), above 1 flattens it. For "The capital of France is", Qwen2.5-0.5B gives " Paris" 33%; at T = 0.5 that becomes 81%, at T = 1.5 only 9%, with the long tail of unlikely tokens gaining what Paris loses.

"Distinct pairs" is the share of adjacent token pairs that are different from each other; "judge perplexity" is how surprising the text is to a bigger model, Qwen2.5-1.5B, given the same passage (lower means more expected; median of 30). Between T = 0.6 and 1.0 the loops vanish and diversity approaches real text. The judge column contains a trap. Greedy text scored the best perplexity, 1.7, far better than real Wikipedia's 13.7, because repeating the passage is very predictable. Human writing is not the most likely text; it is surprising at a steady, moderate rate. Any decoding rule that maximises likelihood will drift towards bland repetition, which is why "lowest perplexity" cannot be the goal of decoding.

Top-k and top-p

Temperature alone has a problem: at T = 1 the tens of thousands of individually unlikely tokens still add up to a real chance of picking something absurd, and one bad token can derail the rest. Truncation fixes that by cutting the tail before sampling. Top-k keeps only the k most likely tokens. Top-p (nucleus sampling) keeps the smallest set of tokens whose probabilities add up to p. Then it renormalises and samples.

def next_token(logits, generated, temperature=1.0, top_k=0, top_p=1.0, rep_penalty=1.0):
    logits = logits.float().clone()                                  # (B, V) scores for the next token
    if rep_penalty != 1.0:                                          # tokens already used: make them less likely
        seen = logits.gather(1, generated)
        logits.scatter_(1, generated, torch.where(seen > 0, seen / rep_penalty, seen * rep_penalty))
    if temperature == 0:
        return logits.argmax(-1, keepdim=True)                      # greedy
    logits = logits / temperature
    if top_k:
        kth = logits.topk(top_k).values[:, -1:]
        logits = logits.masked_fill(logits < kth, float("-inf"))
    if top_p < 1.0:
        sp, si = logits.sort(descending=True)
        p = sp.softmax(-1)
        drop = p.cumsum(-1) - p >= top_p                              # the tokens above already reach top_p
        logits = logits.masked_fill(drop.scatter(1, si, drop), float("-inf"))
    return torch.multinomial(logits.softmax(-1), 1)

On 64 rows of random logits, this kept exactly the same tokens as Hugging Face's TopPLogitsWarper and TopKLogitsWarper, and its repetition penalty gave identical numbers to RepetitionPenaltyLogitsProcessor. All the measurements here use it.

The reason top-p usually beats top-k is that the right number of candidates changes from token to token. Along one 200-token continuation, the nucleus at p = 0.9 contained a single token at 112 of the 200 steps, where only one continuation made sense, but more than 321 tokens at the most open 10% of steps, and 3,433 at the most open. A fixed k = 40 would be far too many at the first kind of step and far too few at the second. Top-p 0.9 and top-k 40 ended up close on these measures (repeats 0.058 and 0.046, judge perplexity 9.5 and 9.4), and both kept the variety of T = 1 while cutting its judge perplexity from 27.5 to under 10.

Repetition penalties

A repetition penalty lowers the score of every token that has already appeared, in the prompt or the output; Hugging Face divides positive logits by the penalty and multiplies negative ones. It attacks greedy's loops directly. Greedy with penalty 1.1 cut 4-gram repeats from 0.605 to 0.079 and ended the loops, while keeping judge perplexity low at 3.0. Stronger penalties overcorrect: at 1.6 no 4-gram repeated at all, but the model is also discouraged from reusing "the", names and words the topic needs, and its text wandered off into a different subject. The penalty does not know which repetitions are wrong; a name in a biography should appear many times.

Beam search and when it helps

Beam search keeps the b most likely partial sequences at each step instead of one, and returns the most likely complete one. It finds text the model rates more probable than greedy does: average log-probability −0.28 with 8 beams against −0.39 greedy. That is exactly the problem for open-ended text, since more likely here means more repetitive. With 8 beams, 47% of 4-grams repeated and 40% of continuations looped; on the Du Fu passage it copied the prompt sentence verbatim, just as greedy did.

Beam search helps when there is one correct output and the model's probabilities track correctness: machine translation, speech recognition and short structured answers. Here it was the best setting on 3-digit addition, at 85%, though by a margin within the noise of 60 questions.

Up to T = 1.0, accuracy moved by no more than the run-to-run spread (individual runs at T = 0.7 ranged from 80% to 88%). Short answers are mostly high-confidence tokens, which sampling rarely overturns; only when the temperature flattens even confident distributions does accuracy fall. Voting over 5 samples gained little too: 81.7% and 35.0%. For tasks with one right answer, use greedy or a low temperature, for reproducibility more than accuracy; for long reasoning chains, where sampling explores different paths, voting can pay off (Chapter 36).

Use case: choosing settings for a product

Most LLM APIs expose temperature and top-p, and the defaults are a compromise. The measurements suggest a simple policy. For extraction, classification, code and other tasks with one right answer, use temperature 0 or close to it: accuracy did not improve with sampling, and results become reproducible, which makes bugs and evaluations repeatable. For writing, brainstorming and conversation, sample with temperature around 0.7 to 1.0 and top-p around 0.9, which removed every loop here while keeping the text plausible. Change one of the two, not both. If a model still repeats, a mild repetition penalty around 1.1 is safer than a strong one. And when several candidates are useful, such as suggestions or test cases, sample several at a moderate temperature rather than raising it.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Using greedy for long open-ended text; it looped in 73% of continuations.
  • Choosing the setting with the lowest perplexity; greedy scored 1.7 against real text's 13.7 by repeating itself.
  • Turning temperature above about 1.2 without truncation; at 1.3 judge perplexity jumped to over 11,000.
  • Using beam search for chat or stories; it is greedy's repetition, searched for harder.
  • Strong repetition penalties; at 1.6 the model avoided words the topic needed and wandered off.
  • Expecting sampling or voting to fix a model's knowledge; voting over 5 samples gained at most 2 points.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What do temperature, top-k and top-p do?

How a strong answer sounds
All three change how the next token is drawn from the model's probabilities. Temperature divides the logits before softmax: below 1 it sharpens the distribution towards the top token, with 0 meaning greedy, and above 1 it flattens it. Top-k keeps only the k most likely tokens; top-p keeps the smallest set whose probabilities reach p; both then renormalise and sample, which removes the long tail of individually unlikely tokens that can derail text. Top-p adapts to the model's confidence: along one continuation the 0.9 nucleus was a single token at 112 of 200 steps but hundreds of tokens at others, which a fixed k cannot match. On Qwen2.5-0.5B, greedy looped in 73% of continuations, sampling at 0.6 to 1.0 removed the loops, and above 1.3 the text became nonsense.

Deeper: Why does maximising likelihood, with greedy or beam search, produce worse open-ended text than sampling?

How a strong answer sounds
Because the most likely continuation is not what people write. Once a phrase appears, repeating it becomes highly probable, so maximising likelihood feeds on itself into loops; beam search finds even more likely sequences and so repeats more, 47% of 4-grams with 8 beams against real text's 2%. A larger judge model rated greedy text at perplexity 1.7, more predictable than real Wikipedia at 13.7, which shows the problem: human text is moderately surprising throughout. Sampling with truncation keeps that moderate surprise while cutting the tail of nonsense. Likelihood-maximising decoding still suits tasks with one correct output, such as translation or short answers, where beam search scored best on addition here, and greedy is the reproducible choice for exact tasks.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Decoding is outside the model; the same model loops, writes well or babbles depending on it.
  • Greedy looped in 73% of 200-token continuations; beam search in 33–40%.
  • Temperature 0.6–1.0 removed the loops; above 1.3 the text fell apart.
  • Top-p adapts to confidence: the 0.9 nucleus was 1 token at 112 of 200 steps and thousands at others.
  • Likelihood is not quality: greedy text was more predictable to a judge than real text.
  • For exact answers, temperature up to 1.0 hardly changed accuracy; use greedy for reproducibility.
