Pretraining Data

Scaling laws (Chapter 11) say how many tokens a model needs. They say nothing about which tokens. Most of what goes into a large model's pretraining is text from the open web, and the raw web is mostly not text a model should learn from: menus, login screens, lists of links, templates, spam and the same page copied thousands of times. Turning it into training data is a pipeline of filters, and every step changes the model.

This chapter runs that pipeline on 100,000 pages of raw web text from Common Crawl, with each filter written from scratch, and trains the same 30-million-parameter model on 41 million tokens of each version. Quality filters threw away 72% of the bytes and made the model better on every test set, by 0.15 bits per byte on unseen web text. Near-duplicate detection found 17,630 pairs of pages that were at least 80% identical. Then two experiments show the other side: what happens when a few documents are repeated many times, and how the mixture of sources decides what a model is good at.

  • Write heuristic quality filters and measure what each removes

  • Check training data for test-set contamination

Where the text comes from

Common Crawl, a non-profit, has crawled the public web every month since 2008 and publishes the result, petabytes of pages. Nearly every open pretraining dataset starts from it: C4 (Google, 2019), The Pile (EleutherAI, 2020), RefinedWeb, Dolma and FineWeb, alongside smaller, higher-quality sources such as Wikipedia, books, scientific papers and code from GitHub. Here, the raw material is the first 100,000 pages of C4's unfiltered release, the crawl text before any cleaning: 621 MB, or 178 million GPT-2 tokens.

Raw web text is dominated by things that are not prose. Every page carries its site's navigation, cookie banners, sign-in prompts, footers and link lists, and many pages consist of nothing else. As a baseline, C4's own published cleaning of a different 100,000 pages of the same crawl leaves 217 MB.

Quality filters

The filters used here follow two well-known recipes, C4's and DeepMind's Gopher rules: simple, fast heuristics that catch most of what is not natural prose. First, as in C4, every line is dropped that does not end in sentence punctuation or has fewer than three words, which removes most menus and buttons. Then a page is dropped if it breaks any of these rules:

STOP = {"the", "be", "to", "of", "and", "that", "have", "with"}
RULES = {                                                      # d: page text after the line filter, w: its words
    "too short (< 50 words)":            lambda d, w: len(w) < 50,
    "odd word length":                   lambda d, w: not (3 <= sum(map(len, w)) / len(w) <= 10),
    "symbols over 10% of words":         lambda d, w: (d.count("#") + d.count("...")) / len(w) > 0.1,
    "few alphabetic words":              lambda d, w: sum(any(c.isalpha() for c in x) for x in w) / len(w) < 0.8,
    "fewer than 2 common English words": lambda d, w: len(STOP & {x.lower() for x in w}) < 2,
    "code or boilerplate markers":       lambda d, w: any(k in d.lower() for k in ("lorem ipsum", "javascript", "{", "cookie policy")),
}                                                              # plus "too long" and "mostly bullet points"

On the 100,000 raw pages, which took 17 seconds:

The line filter alone removed two thirds of the bytes. Of the page rules, "too short" was the first to reject 33,261 pages, most of them pages with almost no prose once their menus were gone; the boilerplate markers rejected another 6,629 first. Many pages broke several rules: 23,191 had fewer than two of the eight commonest English words, mostly non-English pages or lists. Heuristics like these are blunt: "javascript" also rejects a good article about JavaScript, which is why modern pipelines add learned quality classifiers on top, such as FineWeb-Edu's model trained to score how educational a page is.

Exact and near-duplicate removal

The web repeats itself. Exact duplicates are easy: hash each page after lower-casing and collapsing whitespace, and keep the first of each hash. That found 1,036 duplicate pages in the raw crawl and 932 among the filtered pages.

Near-duplicates, pages that differ in a name, a date or a footer, need more. MinHash estimates how much two pages' sets of 5-word phrases overlap (their Jaccard similarity) from short signatures: hash every phrase with 128 different hash functions and keep the smallest value of each. Two pages agree on any one of those minimums with probability equal to their Jaccard similarity. Locality-sensitive hashing then avoids comparing every pair: split each signature into 16 bands of 8 values, and only compare pages that match exactly on at least one band.

sig = signature(shingles(page))                    # 128 minimum hash values over its 5-word phrases
for b in range(16):                                  # 16 bands of 8 values
    buckets[(b, sig[8 * b : 8 * b + 8].tobytes())].append(page_id)
# pages sharing a bucket are candidates; keep the pairs whose real Jaccard similarity is at least 0.8

On the 57,056 filtered pages this took 31 seconds. Banding produced 24,411 candidate pairs, and 17,630 of them were at least 80% similar on checking, yet removing one page of each pair took out only 749 pages: most of the pairs belonged to a few large clusters. Reading them shows what they were: privacy-policy pages generated from the same template with only the site's name changed, sign-in and password-reset pages from the same forum software, and property listings from the same real-estate system. Each cluster is one text repeated with tiny variations, exactly what a model would otherwise learn to recite.

What cleaning does to the model

The same model, Chapter 7's GPT with 6 layers of width 384 and GPT-2's tokeniser (30 million parameters, most of them the 50,257-token vocabulary), was trained on 41 million tokens of each version, and scored in bits per byte (Chapter 10) on three texts it never saw:

Filtering was the big step: the same number of training tokens, a quarter of them now prose, improved every test set by 0.1 to 0.2 bits per byte, 5% on Wikipedia to 12% on the stories. Deduplication changed little at this scale (0.017 better on Wikipedia, 0.006 worse on the stories); with only 41 million tokens and near-duplicates making up 1.3% of pages, there was little repetition to remove. Its benefit in large runs comes from preventing memorisation and wasted compute on repeats, which the next experiment shows directly. C4's own pipeline, which also removes non-English pages with a language classifier, pages with offensive words, and more boilerplate, was best of all.

Repeated documents

Deduplication matters most when a few documents are repeated many times. To see it, the model was trained again on C4's cleaned text, but with 4 of every 32 sequences in each batch drawn from just the first 1,000 documents (486,006 tokens). Over the run, the model saw those documents about 10.5 times each, while the rest of the text was seen less than once.

Repetition made the model worse everywhere it was tested, by 0.03 bits per byte on Wikipedia and 0.02 on the stories, ten times the difference between two identical runs. Part of that is simply fewer distinct tokens; the rest is capacity spent on the repeated documents. On those 1,000 documents its loss fell to 3.98 nats per token, against 4.86 for the model trained without repetition: it was starting to memorise them. At large scale this is how verbatim copies of popular text, from licences to personal data that appears on many pages, end up reproducible by a model, and why pipelines deduplicate across the whole corpus.

Data mixtures

Most models train on a mixture of sources, and the proportions decide what the model is good at. To measure it, the same model was trained with a growing share of each batch taken from TinyStories (Chapter 8) instead of web text, from none to all of it:

The first slice was worth far more than the rest. Moving 6.25% of the tokens to stories cut the stories score from 1.53 to 0.97 bits per byte, two thirds of the total improvement available, while costing Wikipedia only 0.023 and web text 0.005. Going from 75% to 100% stories gained 0.02 on stories and lost 0.75 on Wikipedia. A small share of a domain buys most of its benefit, because the model only needs to learn what is different about it; it is why pretraining mixtures include modest slices of code, mathematics and other languages, and why labs tune the proportions with small runs like these before a large one.

Test-set contamination

A model's test scores mean nothing if the test text was in its training data (Chapter 10). The standard check, used for GPT-3, is to look for long word sequences shared between the two: every 13-word run in the test set, against every training document. WikiText-2's test set has 206,132 distinct 13-word runs. None of them appeared in any of the 100,000 raw pages or the 56,307 cleaned ones, so this sample is clean for that test set. That is a property of this sample, not of the web: WikiText is built from Wikipedia articles, which are copied across many sites, and a crawl a thousand times larger would almost certainly contain some. Large training sets are decontaminated by removing every document that shares such runs with any benchmark before training, and reports should say they did.

Use case: FineWeb and the value of filtering

In 2024 Hugging Face released FineWeb, 15 trillion tokens built from 96 Common Crawl snapshots, and documented every step the way this chapter does at small scale: train small models on each variant of the data, and keep a step only if it improves them. Their ablations found the same pattern as here. Heuristic filters like C4's and Gopher's helped clearly, deduplication across many crawl snapshots needed care, and a learned classifier trained on Llama 3's judgements of which pages were educational produced FineWeb-Edu, a subset on which small models scored markedly better on knowledge and reasoning benchmarks. Teams building domain models do the same thing on their own data: a few small training runs, on cleaned and uncleaned versions, before spending a large budget.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Training on raw crawl text; the same tokens filtered were 0.1 to 0.2 bits per byte better on every test set.
  • Trusting a filter without measuring it; "javascript" as a marker also deletes good articles about JavaScript.
  • Exact deduplication only; templated pages differ by a name and pass straight through.
  • Comparing all pairs for near-duplicates; MinHash with banding needs one pass and a few candidate checks.
  • Letting a few documents repeat many times; 10 passes over 1,000 documents made the model worse on everything else.
  • Never checking for benchmark text in the training data, then reporting benchmark scores.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How would you turn raw web crawl data into pretraining data?

How a strong answer sounds
Start from extracted page text, from Common Crawl for instance. Identify the language and keep the languages you want. Apply line-level filters that drop menus and boilerplate, then document-level heuristics in the style of C4 and Gopher: minimum length, word-length range, symbol and non-alphabetic ratios, presence of common words, boilerplate markers, and optionally a learned quality classifier. Remove exact duplicates by hashing normalised text and near-duplicates with MinHash and locality-sensitive hashing. Remove personal data and toxic content according to policy, decontaminate against benchmarks with n-gram overlap, and finally mix sources in chosen proportions. Measure every step by training small models: on 100,000 pages, the filters kept 28% of the bytes and improved a small model by 0.1 to 0.2 bits per byte on every test set at the same token budget.

Deeper: How does MinHash find near-duplicates among millions of documents without comparing every pair?

How a strong answer sounds
Represent each document by its set of shingles, say 5-word phrases. For each of k hash functions, keep the minimum hash over the document's shingles; the probability that two documents share a minimum equals their Jaccard similarity, so the fraction of matching signature entries estimates it. Then use banding: split the k values into b bands of r rows and put each document into one bucket per band; documents sharing any bucket become candidates. With 16 bands of 8 rows, pairs above roughly 0.7 similarity almost always collide and dissimilar pairs almost never do. Only candidates are compared exactly. On 57,000 pages this took 31 seconds in plain Python and NumPy and found 17,630 pairs at least 80% similar, mostly templated privacy policies and sign-in pages.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Most pretraining text starts as Common Crawl; most raw crawl text is navigation, boilerplate and templates.
  • Line and page filters kept 28% of the bytes, and the model trained on it was better on every test set.
  • Exact hashing finds copies; MinHash with banding finds near-copies, here templated pages in big clusters.
  • 12.5% of tokens from 1,000 repeated documents cost 0.02–0.03 bits per byte and started memorisation.
  • In a mixture, the first 6% of a new domain bought two thirds of its benefit at almost no cost elsewhere.
  • Check for benchmark text with long n-gram overlap; none was found in this sample.
