Embeddings & the Vocabulary Matrix

A token id is just a number. The first thing a language model does with it is look up a row in a big table, a vector of a few hundred or thousand numbers, and the last thing it does is the reverse: score every row of a vocabulary matrix against its final vector to decide what comes next. Those two matrices are where words meet numbers.

This chapter opens them up in GPT-2 and Qwen2.5. Selecting a row was 530 times faster than the equivalent matrix multiply. The nearest neighbours of " king" included " queen", " prince" and " monarch". The famous king − man + woman sum did land on " queen", but only once " king" itself was excluded. And the vocabulary matrix was 31% of GPT-2's parameters but 14% of Qwen2.5-7B's.

The embedding lookup

An embedding layer is a matrix with one row per token in the vocabulary. GPT-2's is 50,257 rows by 768 columns; each row is that token's vector, and turning a sequence of ids into vectors just means picking out those rows. Mathematically this is the same as multiplying a one-hot vector (all zeros except a 1 at the token's id) by the matrix, and that is how it is often drawn. It is never computed that way:

import torch, torch.nn.functional as F
W = gpt2.transformer.wte.weight                 # (50257, 768): one row per token
ids = torch.randint(0, 50257, (4096,), device="cuda")

rows = W[ids]                                   # the lookup: select 4,096 rows       0.017 ms
onehot = F.one_hot(ids, 50257).float()          # (4096, 50257): 823 MB of mostly zeros
same = onehot @ W                               # the same numbers by matrix multiply  9.18 ms
(rows - same).abs().max()                       # 0.0: identical

On this GPU the lookup took 0.017 ms and the matrix multiply 9.18 ms, about 530 times longer, after building an 823 MB matrix that is 99.998% zeros. nn.Embedding is that row selection, and in training only the rows of tokens that appeared in a batch get a gradient. The matrix starts random; everything a row comes to mean, it learns from the training objective of Chapter 1, predicting the next token.

What nearby vectors mean

Tokens used in similar contexts get pushed towards similar vectors, because the model gains by treating them alike. The usual way to compare two vectors is cosine similarity, the cosine of the angle between them: 1 for the same direction, 0 for unrelated, −1 for opposite. Ranking all 50,257 GPT-2 rows by cosine similarity to a token's row gives its nearest neighbours:

Two things stand out. First, the closest neighbours are often the same word in another form: capitalised, without its leading space, or plural. Chapter 2 showed these are unrelated ids; training has pulled their vectors together, but not all the way: "hello" and " hello" have a cosine similarity of 0.68 in GPT-2. Second, the neighbours are related, not synonymous: " seven" is next to " six", and " Paris" next to " London", because they appear in the same kinds of sentence. Qwen2.5-0.5B, trained on multilingual text, puts 巴黎 and 法国 (Paris and France in Chinese) among the neighbours of " Paris".

Read these numbers against a baseline. Two random GPT-2 tokens have an average cosine similarity of 0.27, not 0, because all the rows share a common direction: the average row is a vector of length 2.05, while a typical row is 3.95 long. In Qwen2.5-0.5B the random baseline is 0.15. A similarity of 0.3 means little in GPT-2.

Vector arithmetic works too, up to a point. The vector for " king" − " man" + " woman" is closest to " king" itself in both models; only after excluding the three input words is the answer " queen" (cosine 0.69 in GPT-2, ahead of " princess" and " Queen"). Published analogy demonstrations usually exclude the inputs, which makes them look cleaner than they are.

The unembedding (LM head)

At the other end of the model, the final hidden vector for the last position has to become a score, a logit, for every token in the vocabulary. That is one more matrix multiply: the hidden vector times a matrix with one row per token, giving a dot product with each row. Softmax turns those scores into the probabilities of Chapter 1. This output layer is called the unembedding or LM head.

The output rows are scored by dot product, so a token whose row points in the same direction as the hidden vector gets a high score. For the prompt "The capital of France is", GPT-2's final vector scored " the" highest (8.5%) and " Paris" fifth (3.2%); Qwen2.5-0.5B put " Paris" first at 32%.

Weight tying

Input and output matrices are the same shape, vocabulary × hidden size, and both relate tokens to vectors. Weight tying uses one matrix for both. GPT-2, Qwen2.5-0.5B and Qwen2.5-1.5B are tied: in each, the output layer and the embedding are the same tensor in memory. To check, score the final hidden vector against the input embedding matrix directly:

out = model(**inputs, output_hidden_states=True)
h = out.hidden_states[-1][0, -1]                         # final vector at the last position
logits = h @ model.get_input_embeddings().weight.T       # score it against the INPUT matrix
(logits - out.logits[0, -1]).abs().max()                 # GPT-2 3e-05, Qwen2.5-0.5B 4e-06: the same, up to rounding

Tying saves a whole vocabulary matrix of parameters, and was found to improve small language models: an input token and an output token that mean the same thing share a vector, and rare tokens get trained from both ends. The cost is that one vector must serve two jobs, describing a token as input and recognising when it should come next. Large models can afford to separate them, and Qwen2.5-7B does: its input and output matrices are separate, 545 million parameters each.

In small models the vocabulary is a large part of the whole: over a quarter of Qwen2.5-0.5B is its vocabulary matrix, which is why it is tied. The rest of a model grows with the square of the hidden size, while the vocabulary matrix grows only in proportion to it, so its share falls as models get bigger; at 7B, a second 545-million-parameter matrix is affordable. Qwen2.5's matrices have 151,936 rows although its tokeniser has 151,665 tokens: the row count is rounded up to a multiple of 128, which suits GPU matrix multiplies, and the extra rows are never used.

Adding tokens to a trained model, for new special tokens or a new language, means adding rows. Hugging Face's resize_token_embeddings starts each new row at the average of the existing rows (with a tiny random spread): the two rows added to GPT-2 here were 0.0001 from the mean. The new tokens then mean "an average token" until fine-tuning trains them.

Tokens that training rarely touched end up there too. Ranked by distance from GPT-2's mean embedding, the nearest of all 50,257 is " externalToEVA", followed by unused control-character and broken-byte tokens and by "quickShip" and " TheNitrome"; " externalToEVA", "quickShip" and " TheNitrome" were among the glitch tokens listed in the 2023 study (Chapter 2). " SolidGoldMagikarp" itself, though, ranks only 14,357th: undertrained tokens do not all look alike from the embedding alone.

Use case: adding chat tokens to a base model

Turning a base model into a chat model usually adds special tokens that mark where each turn starts and ends, such as Qwen's <|im_start|> and <|im_end|>. If a tokeniser gets new tokens, the model's embedding (and, untied, its output matrix) needs new rows: model.resize_token_embeddings(len(tokenizer)). Those rows start at the average embedding and mean nothing until fine-tuning trains them, so the fine-tuning data must use the new tokens often, and in parameter-efficient fine-tuning (Chapter 21) the embedding and output rows must be included in what is trained; otherwise the model can never learn to produce the token that ends its turn, and it keeps generating. Qwen2.5 avoids this by shipping those tokens already in its base vocabulary.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Reading a cosine similarity without a baseline; random GPT-2 tokens average 0.27.
  • Trusting analogy demos that quietly exclude the input words.
  • Computing a lookup as a one-hot matrix multiply; it was 530 times slower and needed 823 MB.
  • Adding tokens without training their rows, or freezing the embeddings in fine-tuning so new tokens stay untrained.
  • Forgetting the vocabulary in a parameter budget; it is over a quarter of a 0.5B model.
  • Assuming every model is tied; check tie_word_embeddings in the config before editing one matrix.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What are the embedding layer and the LM head, and what does weight tying do?

How a strong answer sounds
The embedding layer is a vocabulary-by-hidden-size matrix; turning token ids into vectors selects one row per id. It is equivalent to multiplying one-hot vectors by the matrix but computed as an indexed lookup, which on GPT-2 was 530 times faster. The LM head goes the other way: it takes the final hidden vector and computes its dot product with one row per vocabulary token, giving a logit for each, and softmax turns those into next-token probabilities. Weight tying uses the same matrix for both, saving vocabulary × hidden parameters and letting each token's vector be trained from both ends. GPT-2 and the small Qwen2.5 models are tied: scoring the final vector against the input matrix reproduced the model's logits to within 3e-5. Large models like Qwen2.5-7B often untie them, because the saving is a smaller fraction and separate matrices give more capacity.

Deeper: Why does the vocabulary take 28% of a 0.5B model but 14% of a 7B one, and what does that suggest for small models?

How a strong answer sounds
The vocabulary matrix has vocabulary × hidden parameters, so it grows linearly with the hidden size, while attention and MLP layers each grow with its square and models also add layers. Qwen2.5 uses the same 152k-token vocabulary from 0.5B to 7B: 136 M parameters at hidden size 896, out of 494 M, against 545 M per matrix at hidden size 3,584, out of 7.6 B. For small models that means a big vocabulary is expensive: tying is close to essential, a smaller vocabulary might buy more transformer capacity for the same budget, and the trade-off against compression (Chapter 2) has to be made consciously. It also means parameter counts of small models overstate their "thinking" capacity, since a large part is a lookup table.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • An embedding is a row lookup in a vocabulary × hidden matrix; it equals a one-hot matmul but was 530 times faster.
  • Nearby rows are related tokens: other forms of the same word first, then words used in similar sentences.
  • Compare cosine similarities with a baseline: random GPT-2 pairs average 0.27.
  • The LM head scores the final vector against one row per token; softmax makes those scores probabilities.
  • Weight tying shares one matrix for input and output; GPT-2 and small Qwen2.5 models do, Qwen2.5-7B does not.
  • The vocabulary was 31% of GPT-2 and 28% of Qwen2.5-0.5B, but 14% of Qwen2.5-7B; new tokens start as the average row.
