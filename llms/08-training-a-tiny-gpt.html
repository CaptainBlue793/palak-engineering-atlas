Training a Tiny GPT

Chapter 7's model, with GPT-2's weights, already knew English. This chapter starts it from random numbers instead and teaches it from scratch: 13.9 million parameters, a tokeniser trained for the job, and half a million short children's stories from the TinyStories dataset. Everything runs on this one GPU.

Four minutes and 98 million tokens later, it writes stories like "Once upon a time, there was a little girl named Lily. She loved to go on adventures, especially on the beach with her family." Its validation loss fell from 9.12 to 1.69, with training and validation loss within 0.03 of each other the whole way. Then the same model was trained on just 1,000 stories, and it did the opposite: its training loss went to 0.05, its validation loss rose from 3.62 to 6.71, and its stories came out copied word for word.

  • Turn a token stream into training batches of input and target windows
  • Run a training loop and read its loss curve
  • Use validation loss to tell learning from memorising
  • Judge a model by its samples at each stage of training
  • Recognise overfitting, and measure memorisation directly

Batches of token windows

The data is one quarter of TinyStories: 529,930 short stories, generated by GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 for a 2023 Microsoft research paper, using only words a young child knows, plus 21,990 held-out stories for validation. A byte-level BPE tokeniser with 8,192 tokens was trained on 60,000 of them in 1.1 seconds, using Hugging Face's trainer, which Chapter 2 showed gives the same result as the from-scratch one. It averaged 4.1 bytes per token on this simple English, and the whole training set became 117.3 million tokens in one long stream, stories separated by an end-of-text token.

A training example is any window of that stream. Take 256 consecutive tokens as the input and the same window shifted one token to the right as the targets: at every position, the target is simply the next token. One window therefore holds 256 next-token predictions at once, which the model computes in a single pass (Chapter 7). A batch is 64 windows from random places.

def batch(data, B, T, g):                                # data: 1-D tensor of 117 million token ids
    i = torch.randint(0, data.numel() - T - 1, (B,), generator=g)   # B random starting points
    x = torch.stack([data[j : j + T] for j in i])               # inputs
    y = torch.stack([data[j + 1 : j + T + 1] for j in i])       # targets: the same window, one token later
    return x.to("cuda"), y.to("cuda")

for step in range(6000):
    for group in opt.param_groups:
        group["lr"] = lr_at(step)                                  # 200 warm-up steps, then cosine decay (Chapter 14)
    x, y = batch(train, 64, 256, g)
    with torch.autocast("cuda", dtype=torch.bfloat16):
        _, loss = model(x, y)                                  # mean cross-entropy over 64 × 256 predictions
    opt.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)
    loss.backward()
    torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0)
    opt.step()                                                  # AdamW, peak lr 1e-3, weight decay 0.1

The loss curve

Before training, the loss was 9.12, close to ln 8,192 = 9.01, the loss of a model that knows nothing (Chapter 7). Each step then saw 16,384 tokens; the GPU processed about 452,000 tokens a second while training, so 6,000 steps, 98 million tokens, took 241 seconds including the evaluations, using 3.3 GB of GPU memory. That was 0.84 passes over the data: no story was needed twice.

The shape is the one every language model run has: a cliff, then a long slope. The first 250 steps removed most of the loss, learning which tokens are common and how short phrases go. Everything after that, the grammar, the names, the plots, came out of the slow part, 1.54 more units of loss over 5,750 steps, and the curve was still falling at the end. Loss is a logarithm, so the slow part matters more than it looks: going from 2.26 to 1.69 means the probability the model gives the right next token rose on average by a factor of e0.57 = 1.8.

Train vs validation loss

Training loss measures how well the model predicts text it learns from; validation loss measures text it never sees. Both were measured every 250 steps on 25 batches with dropout off. Throughout this run they stayed close: 1.663 training, 1.692 validation at the end, a gap of 0.03. That gap is what says the model learnt to write stories rather than to remember these ones, and with fewer than one pass over the data it had little chance to memorise. A small gap with a loss still falling means the model would benefit from more training or more size, not from regularisation; Chapter 11 measures how much.

To see the other failure, the identical model and settings were trained on just the first 1,000 stories, 231,572 tokens, for 3,000 steps: 212 passes over the same text.

Validation loss was best after 300 steps, about 21 passes, and climbed from then on while training loss went towards zero. Measured directly: of the 8-token runs in 30 generated stories, 66.5% appeared word for word in its 1,000 training stories, against 5.4% for the main model's stories checked against the same 1,000. Its first sample, 380 characters long, was copied entire from one of them: "Once upon a time, there was a little boy named Timmy. Timmy loved to play outside and watch the birds fly in the sky…" The model had stopped learning stories and started storing them. With this little data the fixes are to stop at the validation minimum, get more data, or use a smaller model; dropout (Chapter 6) slows the slide but cannot replace data.

Use case: small models, simple data

Ronen Eldan and Yuanzhi Li made TinyStories at Microsoft Research in 2023 to ask how small a language model can be and still write fluent, coherent English. Their answer was: very small, if the data is simple enough. Models of a few million to a few tens of millions of parameters, trained on stories restricted to a young child's vocabulary, wrote consistent stories with correct grammar, which models of that size trained on web text never managed. The run here reproduces the effect in four minutes. The same idea, that carefully generated and filtered data can stand in for scale, fed into Microsoft's Phi series of small models and into the wide use of synthetic data in training today (Chapter 12).

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Forgetting to shift the targets; a model trained to predict the token it was given learns to copy, and its loss heads to zero.
  • Reporting only training loss; on 1,000 stories it reached 0.05 while validation loss was 6.71.
  • Validating on text that overlaps the training set, which hides memorisation; split by document, not by window.
  • Stopping when the curve flattens; the slow part held 1.54 units of loss and all of the story-telling.
  • Judging by loss alone; read samples at fixed seeds and prompts so stages are comparable.
  • A first loss far from ln(V); 9.12 against 9.01 here was right, 30 would mean a bug.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Walk me through one training step of a GPT.

How a strong answer sounds
Sample a batch of windows from the tokenised corpus, say 64 windows of 256 tokens; the targets are the same windows shifted one token, so every position has its next token as the label. Run the model forward to get logits for every position, compute the mean cross-entropy against the targets, which here is 16,384 predictions in one loss. Zero the gradients, backpropagate, clip the gradient norm, and take an optimiser step, usually AdamW with a warm-up and decaying learning rate. In bf16 autocast on one consumer GPU this ran at about 450,000 tokens per second for a 14-million-parameter model, so 6,000 steps took four minutes and took validation loss from 9.1 to 1.7.

Deeper: How do you tell if a language model is memorising rather than learning, and what do you do about it?

How a strong answer sounds
Watch validation loss on held-out documents alongside training loss. When training loss keeps falling and validation loss turns upward, the model is fitting the specific training text: training the same small model on 1,000 stories took training loss to 0.05 while validation loss rose from a best of 3.62 to 6.71. Measure memorisation directly too, by checking how much generated text appears verbatim in the training data; 66.5% of its 8-token runs did, against 5.4% for a model trained on half a million stories, and a whole 380-character sample was copied. The fixes are more data or fewer passes over it, stopping at the validation minimum, a smaller model, and then regularisation such as dropout or weight decay. In pretraining, where data is seen about once, the gap stays tiny; it was 0.03 here.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • A training example is a window of the token stream with targets shifted by one: 256 predictions per window.
  • A 13.9 M model trained on 98 M TinyStories tokens in four minutes, from loss 9.12 to 1.69.
  • The curve drops steeply then slowly; the slow part is where grammar and story structure are learnt.
  • Training and validation loss within 0.03 means learning, not memorising.
  • Samples at fixed seeds show the order of learning: words, phrases, grammar, plot, consistency.
  • On 1,000 stories the model overfit: validation loss rose from 3.62 to 6.71 and 66.5% of its 8-token runs were copied.
