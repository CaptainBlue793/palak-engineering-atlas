Debugging a Training Run

Training runs fail in a small number of ways, and most of them do not crash. The loss falls but too slowly, or falls beautifully on a bug, or plateaus at a level that looks plausible. Debugging is reading the right signals and knowing what each bug does to them.

This chapter trains Chapter 8's tiny GPT for 1,500 steps correctly, then ten more times with one bug planted each time, logging the loss, gradient norm, update-to-weight ratio, largest activation and share of zero gradients every 25 steps. Only one bug crashed, at step 1. The most dangerous ones looked like success: with the targets not shifted, the training loss reached 0.0017 while the model learnt nothing useful (validation 25.6), and counting padding in the loss gave a training loss of 1.22, better than the correct run's 2.33, for a worse model.

  • Overfit one batch before training anything big
  • Diagnose NaNs, infinities and fp16 underflow
  • Read learning-rate and initialisation problems from the gradient norm, update ratio and activations
  • Recognise a loss that is too good, and the data bugs behind it
  • Know the signals worth logging on every run

Overfit one batch first

The first test of any new model or training script costs a minute: train on one batch, over and over, and check that the loss goes to nearly zero. A working model can memorise 32 sequences; if it cannot, something in the model, the loss or the optimiser is broken, and no amount of data will fix it. One of the bug runs here did exactly this by accident (the same batch every step), and its training loss fell from 9.12 to 0.16 by step 100 and 0.0013 by step 500. Useful as a check, it is also a picture of overfitting: on unseen text that model scored 10.97, worse than the 9.01 a model guessing uniformly over the 8,192 tokens would get, and its stories read "there was a little. She gives him a boy named Timmy's he wanted to take."

Two more numbers to know before training: the loss at step 0 should be close to ln(vocabulary size), here ln 8,192 = 9.01 (the correct run started at 9.12), because a fresh model should be nearly uniform. And the validation loss should track the training loss until the model has seen the data several times.

NaNs and infs

Only one of the ten bugs crashed: storing the weights and Adam's state in fp16 produced NaN at step 1. Adam divides by the square root of its running average of squared gradients plus a small ε, 10⁻⁸; in fp16 the smallest positive number is about 6 × 10⁻⁸, so ε rounds to zero, and any parameter whose gradient was zero divides 0 by 0. fp16 autocast without loss scaling did not crash but was quietly worse: between 10% and 16% of all gradients were exactly zero, against 0.02% normally, because values below fp16's range underflowed, and the run ended at 3.18 instead of 2.35. Both are fixed by bf16, which has fp32's range (Chapter 13 and P1), or by keeping fp32 master weights and scaling the loss when fp16 is required. When NaNs do appear, find the first step and the first tensor that has them, with a hook or torch.autograd.detect_anomaly, instead of lowering the learning rate and hoping.

Loss spikes

Three runs had their dynamics wrong rather than their arithmetic. None showed a dramatic spike in the 25-step log; each settled on a plateau far above the correct run, which is harder to notice than a spike. The gradient norm, update ratio and activations told them apart:

BugFinal validationWhat gave it away
Learning rate 3 × 10⁻², 30 times too high4.84Updates 2 to 4 times the normal ratio; activations reached 15,310, 460 times the correct run's
Learning rate 10⁻², no warm-up, no clipping4.34The first step changed the layer by 50% of its own size (update ratio 0.50); gradient norm up to 19
Initialised with standard deviation 1, 50 times too large5.73Loss 75.5 at step 0 against ln V = 9.01; gradient norm 12,956; activations 8,500 from the start

The update-to-weight ratio is the most useful single number here: around 10⁻³ to 10⁻² per step is healthy for Adam, and a value near 1 means each step is rewriting the layer. Activations in the thousands mean the network has found a way to make some values huge, which usually ends in a crash at a longer run or a larger scale. Real loss spikes, as Chapter 14 measured, come from exactly these conditions; warm-up, clipping and a lower peak rate are the standard fixes.

A flat loss

A loss that falls and then stops at a mediocre level is the least alarming-looking failure. Never zeroing the gradients produced one: each step's gradient was added to all the previous ones, so the effective step grew and pointed into the past, and the run ended at 3.00 with activations of 868, 26 times normal. The zero-gradient share was a tell: it read exactly 0.0%, since accumulated gradients are almost never zero. Other common causes of a flat loss are a learning rate that is too low, a frozen layer or a detached tensor (check that every parameter you expect to train has a gradient), and a schedule that decays to nothing too early. Comparing to a known-good baseline on the same data, as here, is what makes "stuck at 3.0" visible as a problem.

Data bugs that look like model bugs

Four bugs were in the data or the evaluation, not the model, and two produced a training loss that looked better than correct:

BugTraining lossValidation lossWhat it does
Targets not shifted (predict the current token)0.001725.6Copying the input is trivial; the model learns nothing about what comes next and writes "time time time time…"
Padding counted in the loss1.222.94Predicting padding after padding is easy, so the average looks great while real text is learnt less
Dropout left on at evaluation2.432.456 vs 2.371Evaluation in training mode adds noise and costs 0.085
A different tokeniser at evaluation—10.41The correct model scored worse than uniform guessing on text encoded with GPT-2's tokeniser

The rule from all four: a loss that is too good is a bug until proven otherwise, and training loss alone proves nothing. Check validation loss on text prepared by the same code that will be used in production, look at generated samples (the unshifted model's were obviously broken), mask padding and other non-text tokens out of the loss, and call model.eval() before evaluating.

Use case: a fine-tune that "worked"

A team fine-tunes a model on its support conversations; the loss curve is smooth and ends low, and the model is shipped. Users then find it worse than before. Every one of the data bugs here has caused real incidents like that: padding or the prompt counted in the loss, so a low number hid little learning on the replies; a chat template that differed between training and serving (Chapter 20); evaluation on a split that overlapped the training data. Large training teams keep the routine measured here: a known-good baseline, the same handful of signals on every run with alerts on gradient norm and activations, and samples read by a person at every checkpoint, because the bugs that matter most do not crash.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Trusting a very low training loss; unshifted targets gave 0.0017 and a model that writes "time time time".
  • Counting padding in the loss; it made a worse model look better (1.22 against 2.33).
  • Pure fp16 weights and optimiser; Adam's ε rounds to zero and training NaNs at step 1.
  • fp16 autocast without a loss scaler; up to 16% of gradients underflowed to zero.
  • Skipping the step-0 check; a 50-times initialisation started at loss 75.5 against ln V = 9.0.
  • Evaluating in training mode or with a different tokeniser than training used.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Your training loss is falling nicely but the model is useless. What do you check?

How a strong answer sounds
A training loss that falls nicely with a useless model points at the data or the loss, not the optimiser. First, the targets: are they shifted by one token? If the model predicts the token it was given, the loss goes to nearly zero; in my test it reached 0.0017 while validation loss was 25.6 and the model wrote "time time time". Second, what is counted in the loss: padding or prompt tokens make the average easy; counting padding gave a training loss of 1.22 against a correct 2.33, for a worse model. Third, whether validation uses the same tokenizer and preprocessing as training, and model.eval(). Then I would read samples, compare with a known-good baseline, and check validation, not training, loss.

Deeper: What would you log on every training run, and what does each signal catch?

How a strong answer sounds
Training and validation loss, and the step-0 loss against ln of the vocabulary size, which caught a 50-times initialisation at 75.5 against 9.0. The gradient norm before clipping, which flags instability and bad initialisation (12,956 there, against about 0.7 normally). The update-to-weight ratio per layer, around 10⁻³ to 10⁻² for Adam; a first step with ratio 0.50 identified a run with no warm-up. The largest activation, which grew to 15,000 with a learning rate 30 times too high. The share of exactly-zero gradients, which caught fp16 underflow at 10 to 16% and unzeroed gradients at exactly 0%. And generated samples at each checkpoint. Together they distinguish ten different bugs, only one of which crashed.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Overfit one batch and check the step-0 loss (≈ ln V) before training anything big.
  • Of ten bugs, one crashed; the rest had to be read from the signals.
  • Learning-rate and initialisation problems show in the gradient norm, update ratio and activations before the loss.
  • fp16 failures: NaN at step 1 with fp16 Adam state; 10 to 16% zero gradients without a loss scaler.
  • A loss that is too good is a bug: unshifted targets (0.0017) and counted padding (1.22).
  • Validate on production preprocessing, in eval mode, and read samples.
