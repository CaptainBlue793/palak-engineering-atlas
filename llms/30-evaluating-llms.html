Evaluating LLMs

Every claim in this course has rested on a measurement, and every measurement on choices: which questions, which prompt, how the answer is read, who judges. This chapter turns the measuring on itself, using MMLU, a 57-subject multiple-choice benchmark, and Qwen2.5-0.5B- and 1.5B-Instruct.

How the answer was read moved the 0.5B model's score from 36% to 44%. Nine harmless changes to the prompt moved the 1.5B model's between 51% and 57%. Training on 250 of the questions, on purpose, lifted their score from 55% to 92% while the other 250 stayed at 58%, and a published contamination test caught 1 question in 106; moving the options around caught it at once. An LLM judge changed its verdict on 35% of pairs when the two answers swapped places, and one sentence of padding flipped 54% of its decisions.

  • Compare scoring by answer probability with parsing generated text
  • Measure how much prompt format alone moves a score, with error bars

  • Measure an LLM judge's biases before trusting it

What benchmarks measure

A benchmark is a fixed set of questions with known answers and a rule for scoring them. MMLU (2020) has 14,042 four-option questions across 57 subjects, from high-school maths to professional law; the 500 used here were drawn at random and cover all 57. A score on it measures how often a particular model, given a particular prompt, read in a particular way, picks the keyed option. Each of those words matters. And with 500 questions the standard error of an accuracy near 55% is about √(0.55 × 0.45 / 500) ≈ 2.2 points, so differences smaller than about 4 points are not established by one run.

The first choice is how to read the answer. One way scores the probability the model gives to each option's label as the next token and takes the highest; the other lets the model write an answer and parses the label out of it:

500 MMLU questionsLetter probabilityGenerate, then parseReplies with a label found
Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct43.8%36.4%79.2%
Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct55.6%52.8%91.0%

Letter probability always gets an answer and never fails to parse, so it scored higher; generating reflects how the model is actually used, including the 21% of the 0.5B model's replies where no label could be found. Neither is wrong, but scores computed the two ways are not comparable, and published tables often do not say which was used.

Prompt format sensitivity

The same 500 questions, the same 1.5B model, the same letter-probability scoring, nine prompt formats that change nothing about the question:

FormatAccuracy
Labels A–D, original order, system message, no examples (the default)55.6%
Labels 1–452.6%
Labels (a)–(d)56.0%
No system message53.4%
Five solved examples first51.2%
Options rotated by 1 / 2 / 357.0% / 55.8% / 54.2%
Options reversed53.6%

The range is 5.8 points, from 51.2% to 57.0%, about two and a half standard errors; two papers reporting this model with different formats could differ by that much without either being wrong. Few-shot examples, usually expected to help, cost 4.4 points here. Part of the variation is a preference for particular labels: with the default format the model answered B 166 times against 115 questions whose answer was B, and C only 109 times against 145. Moving the options moves which answers benefit from that bias. Report the exact prompt, and if possible average over several orderings.

Contamination

A benchmark measures generalisation only if the model has not seen its questions. Web-scale training data contains benchmark questions, answer keys and discussions of them, so contamination is a standing risk, and it is invisible from the outside. One published test, "TS-Guessing", hides one wrong option and asks the model to reproduce it word for word; a model that has memorised the question should be able to. On the untouched 1.5B model it found 0 of 179.

A test only means something if it can detect the thing it looks for, so the 1.5B model was contaminated on purpose: LoRA-tuned on 250 of the 500 questions, each with its options and answer, for five epochs, then measured on both halves after each epoch.

Contamination inflated the seen questions by 38 points and did nothing for the unseen ones, which stayed within noise of where they started: the model had memorised answers, not learnt the subjects. The hidden-option probe, run on the same memorised questions, caught 1 in 106. Its "0 of 179" on the untouched model therefore said almost nothing. Rotating the options did better: on the seen questions accuracy fell from 92.4% to 77.6%, because part of what was memorised was the answer's letter, while the unseen questions barely moved. Practical checks follow: compare a benchmark with a freshly written or perturbed version of it, look for a gap between seen-looking and new questions, and prefer benchmarks with private or recently written test sets.

LLM-as-judge and its biases

For open-ended answers there is no answer key, so a strong model is often asked which of two answers is better. Here Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct judged 80 pairs: a human answer from Dolly against Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct's answer to the same question, each pair asked in both orders, with the judge's choice read from the probability of "A" against "B".

Judge: Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, 80 pairsMeasured
Same winner in both orders65%
Picked whichever answer was shown first58.8%
Preferred the human answer in both orders30%
Changed its verdict when one sentence of empty praise was added to the losing answer53.7%

On 35% of pairs the verdict depended only on the order, and the padding, "In summary, this is a comprehensive and thorough answer that carefully addresses every aspect of the question in detail", flipped more than half of the decisions while adding no content. Larger judges are more consistent than this 1.5B model, but the same biases, position, length and self-preference, are measured in the best ones too. Always ask in both orders and count only consistent verdicts, control or report length, and check the judge against human labels on a sample before relying on it.

Building your own eval

Public benchmarks answer "is this model generally capable"; a product needs "does it do our task". The measurements here give the recipe for an eval set of your own. Collect real inputs, a few hundred at least, so that a standard error near 2 to 3 points is possible. Write the expected output or a scoring rule for each before looking at any model's answers. Fix the prompt, the decoding and the parser, and version them with the set. Score both ways when the task allows: an exact check where there is one (Chapters 25 and 29 each found a grader bug that changed the result, and Chapter 28 a broken constraint), a judge where there is not, asked in both orders. Keep part of the set private, and add new cases from real failures. Then a change in the score means a change in the model.

Use case: choosing a model for a product

Teams choosing between models, or deciding whether a fine-tune helped, are usually shown leaderboard numbers computed by someone else with an unknown prompt, parser and contamination history. The measurements here explain why those numbers rarely transfer: a 6-point swing from formatting, a 7-point swing from how the answer is read, and contamination that inflates seen questions by tens of points without any general gain. Careful teams run every candidate through one in-house harness on their own task, with the exact prompt they will ship, error bars, and a judge checked for position and length bias, and treat public benchmarks as a first filter only.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Comparing scores computed different ways; letter probability and generation differed by 7.4 points on the 0.5B model.
  • Treating a 3-point difference as real on 500 questions; one standard error is 2.2 points and format alone moved 5.8.
  • Assuming few-shot examples help; they cost 4.4 points here.
  • Trusting a contamination test that was never shown to work; the probe found 1 in 106 on a model that had memorised them.
  • Asking a judge in one order only; 35% of its verdicts changed when the answers swapped.
  • Letting answer length decide; one sentence of empty praise flipped 54% of verdicts.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Why can two papers report different MMLU scores for the same model?

How a strong answer sounds
Because a score depends on the harness, not only the model. How the answer is read matters: scoring the probability of each option's letter gave Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct 43.8% on 500 questions, letting it generate and parsing the label gave 36.4%, partly because 21% of replies had no parseable label. The prompt matters: nine formats that do not change the question, label styles, option order, a system message, few-shot examples, ranged from 51.2% to 57.0% for the 1.5B model, against a standard error of 2.2 points. Few-shot examples even cost 4.4 points. Models also have label biases, this one over-chose B, so option order shifts the score. To compare, you need the same prompt, scorer, questions and, ideally, several orderings averaged.

Deeper: How would you check whether a model has been trained on a benchmark's test set?

How a strong answer sounds
I would first make sure my test can detect contamination at all, with a positive control. When I fine-tuned a 1.5B model on 250 MMLU questions with answers, its score on them rose from 55% to 92% while the other 250 stayed at 58%, yet a published hidden-option completion probe flagged only 1 of 106 of the memorised questions, so its negative result on an untouched model meant little. Better signals were behavioural: perturbing the questions, here rotating the options, dropped the memorised questions from 92% to 78% while unseen ones did not move, and the gap between a benchmark and a freshly written equivalent. Where training data is available, n-gram overlap search. And I would prefer benchmarks with private or post-cutoff test sets.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • A score measures a model, a prompt, a reader and a question set together; report all four.
  • On 500 questions one standard error is about 2.2 points; format alone moved the score 5.8.
  • Letter probability and generate-then-parse differed by up to 7.4 points.
  • Contamination on purpose lifted seen questions 55% → 92%, unseen not at all; rotating options exposed it.
  • A contamination test needs a positive control: the hidden-option probe caught 1 in 106.
  • An LLM judge flipped on 35% of swaps and 54% of padded answers: ask both orders, control length.
