Optimisers, Schedules & Stability

Every training run in this course has used AdamW with a warm-up, a cosine decay and gradient clipping at 1.0, without asking why. This chapter takes them apart on Chapter 8's TinyStories model: 21 runs of 1,500 steps, each about a minute, changing one thing at a time.

The optimiser mattered most: after the same 1,500 steps, AdamW reached a validation loss of 2.08 and the best plain SGD 3.30. The learning rate came next, with a narrow good range: 3 × 10⁻³ gave 2.01, ten times lower 2.42, three times higher 3.74. A learning-rate range test found the same range in 22 seconds. And at a learning rate that was too high, no combination of warm-up and gradient clipping rescued the run: all four stalled near a loss of 4, with gradient spikes as large as 763.

  • Find a good learning rate with a sweep or a range test
  • Choose a warm-up and decay schedule
  • Use gradient clipping and read a gradient-norm trace
  • Recognise and respond to loss spikes and divergence

SGD to AdamW

Plain stochastic gradient descent moves every parameter by the same multiple of its gradient. Momentum keeps a running average of past gradients and moves along that instead, which smooths out the noise of small batches. Adam also keeps a running average of each parameter's squared gradient and divides by its square root, so every parameter takes a step of roughly the same size whatever the scale of its gradients. That matters in a transformer, where embeddings, attention and norm parameters see gradients of very different sizes. AdamW applies weight decay, a gentle pull of the weights towards zero, directly to the weights rather than through the gradient, so Adam's division does not undo it.

# one AdamW step for one parameter tensor p with gradient g (β1 0.9, β2 0.95)
m = β1 * m + (1 - β1) * g                    # momentum: average gradient
v = β2 * v + (1 - β2) * g * g                # average squared gradient, per parameter
p -= lr * (m / (1 - β1**t)) / (sqrt(v / (1 - β2**t)) + 1e-8)   # bias-corrected, scaled per parameter
p -= lr * wd * p                                # decoupled weight decay (the W in AdamW)

Each optimiser was given a learning rate suited to it, SGD several, and trained the same model for 1,500 steps:

OptimiserLearning rateValidation loss after 1,500 steps
SGD0.1 / 0.3 / 1.04.07 / 3.59 / 3.30
SGD + momentum 0.90.033.40
Adam1 × 10⁻³2.08
AdamW, weight decay 0.11 × 10⁻³2.08

Adam's per-parameter scaling was worth more than a unit of loss over SGD. Weight decay made no measurable difference in a run this short (2.079 against 2.083); its job is to keep weights from growing over long runs, which is why every large model uses it.

Finding a learning rate

The learning rate is the most important number in a run. A sweep, the same run at several values, shows its usual shape: too low learns slowly, too high is worse than both.

AdamW peak learning rate1 × 10⁻⁴3 × 10⁻⁴1 × 10⁻³3 × 10⁻³1 × 10⁻²3 × 10⁻²
Validation loss2.962.422.082.013.744.36

The curve is lopsided. Below the best value, each factor of three cost 0.07, then 0.34, then 0.54; above it, a single factor of three cost 1.7. That asymmetry is why the usual advice is to pick the largest learning rate that is safely stable, and why being slightly too low is far less costly than slightly too high.

A range test gets a similar answer from one short run: start the learning rate tiny and multiply it every step, here from 10⁻⁶ to 1 over 600 steps, and watch the loss. It fell fastest and reached its lowest, 3.68, around 4 × 10⁻³, rose from about 10⁻², and the run blew up past 0.1. In 22 seconds it pointed at the same region the six-run sweep found, though a sweep is still the better final check, because the best rate for a full run is usually somewhat below where the range test's loss bottoms out.

Warm-up and decay

A schedule changes the learning rate during the run. Warm-up ramps it up over the first steps, while Adam's running averages are still unreliable and the weights are random. Decay lowers it at the end so the weights settle into a minimum rather than bouncing around it. With a 100-step warm-up and the same peak of 10⁻³:

Schedule after warm-upValidation loss
Constant2.119
Cosine decay to 10%2.077
Linear decay to 10%2.071
Warmup-stable-decay: constant, then linear decay over the last 20%1.999

Any decay beat none, by 0.04 to 0.12. Warmup-stable-decay (WSD) was best here, but read it with the learning-rate sweep in mind: the peak of 10⁻³ is below this model's best rate, so the schedule that spends longest at its peak gets the most useful learning in. Cosine and linear were within 0.006 of each other. WSD has a practical advantage beyond the number: because most of the run is at a constant rate, you can stop, branch off a decay and evaluate at any point, then continue, which is why several recent open models use it.

Gradient clipping, spikes and divergence

The gradient's overall size, its norm, is worth logging every step: it usually sits in a narrow band, and a sudden jump means one batch produced an outsized update. Gradient clipping rescales the gradient whenever its norm exceeds a threshold, so no single step can move the weights too far.

loss.backward()
norm = torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), max_norm=1.0)   # returns the norm before clipping: log it
opt.step()

To see what these defences can and cannot do, the model was run at 10⁻², a learning rate the sweep showed is too high, with and without a 100-step warm-up and with and without clipping at 1.0:

At learning rate 10⁻²Largest gradient normValidation loss
Warm-up and clipping1773.87
Clipping, no warm-up2464.23
Warm-up, no clipping7634.21
Neither774.17
Learning rate 3 × 10⁻³, both (for comparison)—2.01

No run diverged to infinity; all four stalled. The loss fell to between 4.2 and 5.1 in the first 150 steps and then hovered around 4 to 4.5 for a thousand more, while the gradient norm jumped by tens or hundreds at random steps. Warm-up and clipping together finished best, but the four ended within 0.4 of each other and nearly 2 units worse than a sensible learning rate. The spikes followed no tidy pattern: with warm-up, clipping went with a largest norm of 177 rather than 763, but without warm-up the clipped run saw 246 and the unclipped one 77. Clipping limits how far each spike moves the weights; it does not stop spikes happening. The defences make a run survive occasional bad batches; they do not make a bad learning rate good. When the gradient norm keeps spiking, lower the learning rate.

Large runs see rarer, sharper loss spikes even at good settings, often from a batch of unusual data or from growing attention logits. The standard responses, in order, are clipping, a lower peak or a longer warm-up, normalisation inside attention (QK-norm), and as a last resort rewinding to a checkpoint from before the spike and skipping the batches that caused it, which several published large runs describe doing.

Use case: recovering a large run from a loss spike

Published accounts of large training runs read like this chapter at scale. Meta's logbook for OPT-175B (2022) records dozens of restarts after loss spikes and divergences, handled by rewinding to an earlier checkpoint, lowering the learning rate and skipping data. Google's PaLM paper (2022) reports about 20 loss spikes despite gradient clipping, dealt with by restarting about 100 steps before each spike and skipping a few hundred batches around it; they concluded the spikes came from particular combinations of batch and weights rather than bad data alone. The everyday version is the same: log the gradient norm every step, keep regular checkpoints, and when the norm spikes repeatedly, lower the learning rate rather than trusting clipping to absorb it.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Training a transformer with plain SGD; after the same steps it was over a unit of loss behind AdamW.
  • Using a learning rate from another model or paper without a sweep; three times too high cost 1.7.
  • Erring on the high side; the loss curve was far steeper above the best rate than below it.
  • No decay at the end; a constant rate finished 0.04 to 0.12 behind every decaying schedule.
  • Not logging the gradient norm; it is the earliest sign of instability.
  • Relying on clipping to rescue a too-high learning rate; every variant at 10⁻² stalled near 4.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Why do transformers use AdamW with warm-up and cosine decay?

How a strong answer sounds
Adam adapts the step size per parameter by dividing by a running root-mean-square of its gradients, which suits transformers, where different parameter groups have gradients of very different scales; on a small GPT, Adam reached a loss of 2.08 in 1,500 steps where the best SGD reached 3.30. AdamW decouples weight decay from that scaling so it acts as intended. Warm-up ramps the learning rate up while the moment estimates are unreliable and the network is random, avoiding early instability. Decay at the end lets the weights settle; any decay beat a constant rate here, and warmup-stable-decay, which holds the peak and decays only at the end, did best and lets you branch off evaluations mid-run.

Deeper: Your run's loss suddenly spikes. What do you do?

How a strong answer sounds
Look at the gradient norm and the data around the spike. If the norm is spiking repeatedly, the learning rate is probably too high for the current state: at a rate three times past the best, every combination of warm-up and clipping I tried stalled around a loss of 4, with norm spikes up to 763. Make sure clipping is on, typically at 1.0, so a single bad step cannot move the weights far, and that the warm-up is long enough. If it is a rare spike in a long run, rewind to a checkpoint from before it, optionally skip the batches that triggered it, and continue, possibly at a slightly lower peak; PaLM and OPT both did this. For recurring instability at scale, look at architectural fixes such as QK-norm, and check for bad data such as long runs of repeated tokens.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Adam's per-parameter scaling beat SGD by over a unit of loss; weight decay matters over long runs, not short ones.
  • The learning rate has a narrow good range and a cliff above it: 3e-3 gave 2.01, 1e-2 gave 3.74.
  • A range test found the same region in one 22-second run.
  • Any decay beat a constant rate; WSD did best here and allows mid-run evaluations.
  • Log the gradient norm: at too high a rate it spiked by orders of magnitude; clipping limits each spike's step, not the spikes.
  • Warm-up and clipping cannot rescue a too-high learning rate; lower it.
