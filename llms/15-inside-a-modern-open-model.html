Inside a Modern Open Model

GPT-2 came out in 2019. The open models people run today, Llama, Qwen, Mistral, Gemma, are still decoder-only transformers built from the same blocks, but almost every part has been swapped for a slightly better version. This chapter opens Qwen2.5 next to GPT-2 and rebuilds one of its layers from scratch.

The rebuilt layer matched the real one to within 0.000002. Then each modern change was added in turn to Chapter 8's small TinyStories model and trained for 2,000 steps. Rotary positions, RMSNorm, the SwiGLU MLP and dropping the biases together cut the validation loss by 0.09, from 1.988 to 1.897 averaged over three seeds, with the same parameter count. Grouped-query attention then gave back 0.02 of that, in exchange for a key-value cache three times smaller.

RMSNorm and SwiGLU

RMSNorm simplifies LayerNorm. LayerNorm subtracts each vector's mean, divides by its standard deviation, then scales and shifts by learned parameters. RMSNorm skips the mean and the shift: it divides by the root-mean-square and scales. It is cheaper, and in practice the mean of a hidden vector is close to zero anyway; in Qwen2.5's fourth layer the average absolute mean of a hidden vector was 0.06, against a root-mean-square of 1.55.

The SwiGLU MLP replaces "widen, GELU, narrow" with a gate: two parallel projections up, one passed through SiLU and multiplied element-wise by the other, then one projection down. The gate lets each feature decide how much of the other passes. It has three matrices instead of two, so models make it about two thirds as wide to keep the parameter count level.

def rms_norm(x, w, eps):
    return w * (x * torch.rsqrt(x.pow(2).mean(-1, keepdim=True) + eps))   # no mean, no bias

def qwen_layer(lyr, x):                                     # one Qwen2.5 decoder layer, from scratch
    h = rms_norm(x, lyr.input_layernorm.weight, 1e-6)
    a = lyr.self_attn
    q = a.q_proj(h).view(B, T, 14, 64).transpose(1, 2)          # 14 query heads
    k = a.k_proj(h).view(B, T, 2, 64).transpose(1, 2)           # only 2 key heads ...
    v = a.v_proj(h).view(B, T, 2, 64).transpose(1, 2)           # ... and 2 value heads
    q, k = rope(q, pos), rope(k, pos)                            # Chapter 5, base 1,000,000
    k, v = k.repeat_interleave(7, 1), v.repeat_interleave(7, 1)  # each shared by 7 query heads
    o = F.scaled_dot_product_attention(q, k, v, is_causal=True)
    x = x + a.o_proj(o.transpose(1, 2).reshape(B, T, 896))
    m = lyr.mlp
    h = rms_norm(x, lyr.post_attention_layernorm.weight, 1e-6)
    return x + m.down_proj(F.silu(m.gate_proj(h)) * m.up_proj(h))   # SwiGLU

Given the real input of Qwen2.5-0.5B's fourth layer, captured during a forward pass, this returned the layer's real output to within 2.0 × 10⁻⁶. Two smaller details are visible in it: Qwen keeps biases on the query, key and value projections but nowhere else, and the order of operations is GPT-2's pre-norm block from Chapter 6 unchanged.

Rotary positions in practice

Chapter 5 checked RoPE against Qwen2.5 directly. Two settings in the config decide how it behaves. The base, 1,000,000 instead of the original paper's 10,000, slows the rotation of every pair so that distant positions stay distinguishable over long contexts; in Qwen2.5 the slowest pair turns once every four million tokens. And the maximum position, 32,768 for the 0.5B model and 131,072 for the larger ones, is what the model was trained to handle; going further needs RoPE scaling (Chapter 18). There is no position table at all, which is why a 1,024-row limit like GPT-2's never appears.

Grouped-query attention

In GPT-2 every query head has its own key and value head. In Qwen2.5-0.5B, 14 query heads share just 2 key-value heads, 7 to each. This is grouped-query attention (GQA); the extreme case, one key-value head for all, is multi-query attention. The queries still look at the sequence in 14 different ways, but they look up 2 sets of keys and values instead of 14.

It barely changes the parameter count: the key and value projections in a Qwen2.5-0.5B layer are 114,688 weights each against the query's 802,816. What it changes is the key-value cache, the stored keys and values of every earlier token that generation reuses (Chapter 16). In bf16, per token:

A 32,768-token context in Qwen2.5-7B therefore needs 1.75 GiB of cache instead of 12.3 GiB. Serving many users at once is mostly a question of how many caches fit beside the weights (Chapter 26), so GQA is one of the biggest practical changes since GPT-2. Its effect on quality is small, as the next section measures.

Where the parameters sit has shifted too. With attention's key and value projections shrunk and the gated MLP widened, a Qwen2.5-0.5B layer has 1.84 million parameters in attention and 13.07 million in the MLP, 88% of the layer, against two thirds in GPT-2 (Chapter 6).

The two that clearly mattered were RoPE and SwiGLU. RMSNorm and dropping biases were within the noise for quality; they are kept because they are cheaper and, at scale, more stable. Use case: the Llama recipe as a default

Meta's first Llama paper (2023) listed its departures from the original transformer in three lines: pre-normalisation with RMSNorm, the SwiGLU activation, and rotary embeddings. Llama 2 added grouped-query attention to its largest model, and Llama 3 to all of them. Because Llama's weights were widely released, that block became the template: Mistral, Qwen, Yi, DeepSeek's dense models and many others use it with small variations, which is also why tools such as llama.cpp and vLLM can support so many models with one code path. When a new open model is announced, the useful first question is what it changed from this block, usually little: a different attention pattern, a mixture-of-experts MLP (Chapter 33), or different numbers in the config.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Comparing architectures with one seed; seeds alone moved the loss by up to 0.015 here.
  • Swapping GELU for SwiGLU without narrowing the MLP; three matrices at full width add 50% to the MLP.
  • Forgetting to repeat the key and value heads to the query-head count, or repeating them in the wrong order.
  • Assuming every model is untied, or tied; Qwen2.5-7B is not tied, the smaller ones are.
  • Reading max_position_embeddings as a guarantee that the model uses all of that context well (Chapter 18).
  • Sizing a deployment by weights alone; without GQA, a 32k-token Qwen2.5-7B cache would be 12 GiB per request.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What has changed in transformer blocks between GPT-2 and models like Llama or Qwen?

How a strong answer sounds
The overall structure is the same pre-norm decoder block, but nearly every part was replaced. Learned position embeddings became rotary embeddings applied to queries and keys. LayerNorm became RMSNorm, which drops the mean subtraction and bias. The GELU MLP became SwiGLU, a gated MLP with three matrices, made about two thirds as wide to keep parameters level. Biases were mostly removed. Key-value heads were shared across groups of query heads, grouped-query attention, which shrinks the KV cache several times. Vocabularies grew, and very large models untied input and output embeddings. In my own small ablation on TinyStories, RoPE and SwiGLU gave clear gains, about 0.05 and 0.03 in loss; RMSNorm and removing biases were within seed noise; and GQA cost about 0.02 for a cache three times smaller.

Deeper: Why is grouped-query attention used everywhere now if it slightly hurts quality?

How a strong answer sounds
Because the expensive resource at inference is memory, not parameters. During generation each layer stores keys and values for every previous token, and decode speed is limited by reading that cache plus the weights. With 7 query heads per key-value head, Qwen2.5 stores a seventh as much: 57 KB per token for the 7B model instead of 401 KB, or 1.75 GiB for a 32k context instead of 12.3 GiB. That means more concurrent requests per GPU, longer contexts and faster memory-bound decoding. The quality cost is small, about 0.02 in loss in my small experiment, and it can be recovered with slightly more training, so the trade is overwhelmingly worth it for anything that is served.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Modern open models keep GPT-2's pre-norm decoder structure but replace most parts.
  • A Qwen2.5 layer rebuilt from scratch matched the real one to 2 × 10⁻⁶.
  • RMSNorm drops the mean and bias; SwiGLU gates the MLP with three matrices at two thirds the width.
  • In a small ablation, RoPE (−0.053) and SwiGLU (−0.030) mattered; RMSNorm and no-bias were within noise.
  • GQA shares key-value heads: a 7× smaller cache for Qwen2.5, at a small cost in loss.
  • The MLP holds 88% of a Qwen2.5-0.5B layer's parameters.
