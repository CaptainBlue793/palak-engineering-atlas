Probability for Language Models

A language model never outputs a word. It outputs a probability distribution over its whole vocabulary, and everything you will do with it, from generating text to measuring how good it is, is a probability operation on that distribution: sampling from it, multiplying probabilities along a sentence, taking logs, averaging, exponentiating.

This chapter covers the five you need, each measured on GPT-2: how spread out a next-token distribution really is, how many samples it takes before sampling looks like the model, why a sentence's probability must be kept as a log (a product of probabilities hit zero after 33 tokens in fp32), what entropy and perplexity mean, and why one forward pass can score a whole sentence.

  • Compute a sequence's log-likelihood, and explain why it is never stored as a product
  • Turn a loss into perplexity and bits, and compare models with them
  • Apply the chain rule of probability to sequences

A distribution over tokens

For any text so far, the model gives every token in its vocabulary a probability, and those 50,257 probabilities (for GPT-2) add up to 1. Some contexts leave almost nothing to chance; others leave a great deal. Measured on GPT-2:

Two things stand out. First, "obvious" answers are often not where the probability is: after "Paris is the capital of", " France" gets only 4.1%, because "the capital of the …" and "a …" are also common ways for that sentence to continue. Second, even confident distributions have a long tail: after "404 means Not", " Found" takes 59%, yet it takes 59 different tokens to cover 90% of the probability. That tail is what makes sampling interesting and sometimes dangerous (Chapter 9).

Entropy measures how spread out a distribution is: H = −Σ p · log₂ p bits. It is the average number of yes/no questions you would need to identify the next token. A distribution with all its mass on one token has entropy 0; a uniform distribution over GPT-2's vocabulary has log₂ 50,257 = 15.6 bits. The code completion above needs half a bit; the HTTP status code needs over seven.

Sampling from it

Generating text means drawing the next token from that distribution, appending it, and repeating. In PyTorch one draw is torch.multinomial(probs, 1), which picks token i with probability probs[i]:

probs = torch.softmax(logits.double(), -1)                        # (V,) the next-token distribution
g = torch.Generator(device="cuda").manual_seed(0)                # seeded, so the draws can be repeated
draws = torch.multinomial(probs.float(), 10_000, replacement=True, generator=g)   # (10000,) token ids

Ten thousand draws after "The server returned HTTP status code" produced 811 different tokens. " 200" came up 16.1% of the time against its probability of 15.9%, and 67.6% of draws were outside the top six tokens, against 68.1% predicted. With only 10 draws the estimates were useless: " 200" appeared once (10%), and " 1", " 0" and " 204" not at all. The error of a frequency estimated from n samples shrinks like 1/√n, so judging a model from a handful of generations, as people often do, tells you very little about its distribution.

Log-likelihood, and why it lives in logs

The probability of a whole sequence is the product of the probability of each token given the ones before it. For "The server returned HTTP status code 200 and the request completed in 35 ms." that product, over 14 predicted tokens, is 2.6 × 10⁻²⁴. Products like this shrink geometrically with length, so they underflow. For a 296-token passage about how a web request travels, the running product of GPT-2's token probabilities reached exactly zero at token 33 in fp32 and at token 210 in fp64, the most precise float a GPU normally handles. The passage's true probability is about 10⁻⁴⁵⁸.

The fix is to never form the product. The log of a product is the sum of the logs, so the log-likelihood of the passage is simply the sum of the per-token log-probabilities: −1,055.2 nats, a perfectly ordinary number. This is why models return log-probabilities, why the loss is a negative log-likelihood, and why every comparison of sequences in this course is done in log space.

logp = torch.log_softmax(logits[:-1].double(), -1)                   # (T-1, V) log-probabilities at each position
token_logp = logp.gather(1, ids[1:, None]).squeeze(1)              # (T-1,) log p(actual next token)
seq_logp = token_logp.sum()                                        # −1,055.2 for the 296-token passage
mean_nll = -token_logp.mean()                                      # 3.577: the cross-entropy loss

Entropy, cross-entropy and perplexity

The mean negative log-likelihood per token is exactly the cross-entropy loss from P3. Its exponential is the perplexity: perplexity = e^(mean NLL). It reads as "the model was, on average, as uncertain as if it were choosing uniformly between this many tokens". A perplexity of 22 means about 22 equally likely choices per token; a model that knows nothing about the text scores the vocabulary size, 50,257. Dividing the loss by ln 2 gives bits per token, the same quantity in base 2.

Structured text is the most predictable: JSON keys, brackets and Python syntax follow rules the model has seen millions of times. The log line is hard because its timestamp and IDs are close to random. Shuffling the same words multiplies perplexity by 47, which is how much of the predictability came from word order rather than vocabulary. And random tokens score 2.06 million, about 41 times worse than a model that predicts uniformly. GPT-2 is confident about what usually comes next, so when something improbable comes instead, it has given that token far less than 1/50,257. Perplexity punishes confident mistakes hardest. These are short texts, so treat the exact values as rough; Chapter 10 measures perplexity properly, on held-out data.

The chain rule of probability

The chain rule says the probability of a sequence is the product of each token's probability given everything before it: P(w₁, …, wₙ) = P(w₁) · P(w₂ | w₁) · … · P(wₙ | w₁, …, wₙ₋₁). A language model is exactly a machine for the factor on the right, and nothing else.

Generation has to evaluate those factors one at a time, because each depends on the token chosen before it. Scoring a sentence that already exists does not: a causal model (Chapter 4) computes every position's prediction in a single forward pass, each position seeing only the tokens before it. I checked both ways on the 15-token sentence: one forward pass, and 14 separate steps feeding one token at a time with a cache of earlier keys and values (Chapter 16). The per-token log-probabilities agreed to within 5.4 × 10⁻⁵, rounding error in fp32. This is why training is fast: a batch of sequences gives a prediction and a loss at every position at once, thousands of training examples per forward pass.

Use case: Shannon's guessing game, 1951

Claude Shannon measured the entropy of English by asking people to guess the next letter of a passage, one letter at a time, and counting how many guesses they needed. From that he estimated that printed English carries roughly one bit per letter rather than the 4.7 bits that 27 equally likely symbols would allow. A language model plays the same game at the level of tokens, and its cross-entropy in bits is the modern version of Shannon's estimate: the fewer bits a model needs per character of text, the better it predicts it. Chapter 10 uses exactly this unit, bits per byte, to compare models fairly.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Multiplying token probabilities to score a sequence; the product reached zero after 33 tokens in fp32.
  • Judging a model's distribution from a few samples; with 10 draws, three of the six likeliest tokens never appeared.
  • Comparing perplexities across different tokenisers or test sets, where the same text is split into different numbers of tokens (Chapter 10).
  • Reading the top token as the model's answer when it holds 4% of the probability, as " France" did.
  • Mixing natural logs and log base 2 when converting between nats, bits and perplexity.
  • Computing a sentence's probability with a separate forward pass per token when one causal pass gives all of them.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is perplexity, and what does it mean that a model scores 22 on one text and 60 on another?

How a strong answer sounds
Perplexity is the exponential of the average negative log-likelihood per token, which is the cross-entropy loss. It reads as the number of equally likely tokens the model was effectively choosing between at each step. GPT-2 scored 22 on a JSON response body and 60 on an English sentence: on JSON it was as uncertain as a choice among 22 tokens, on prose among 60, because JSON's structure is more predictable. Lower is better, a model that knows nothing scores the vocabulary size, and the comparison is only fair on the same text with the same tokeniser, because a different tokeniser splits the same text into a different number of tokens.

Deeper: GPT-2's perplexity on a string of random tokens was about 2 million, though its vocabulary has only 50,257 tokens. How can perplexity be larger than the vocabulary?

How a strong answer sounds
A perplexity equal to the vocabulary size corresponds to predicting uniformly, giving every token 1/50,257. A trained model does not predict uniformly: it concentrates probability on tokens that usually follow, which leaves very little for unusual ones. On random tokens every actual next token is unusual, so the model assigns each of them far less than 1/50,257, and the average negative log-probability exceeds ln(50,257). Perplexity therefore punishes confident mistakes more heavily than ignorance. It is the same reason a confidently wrong model can have a worse loss than an untrained one, and why cross-entropy on out-of-distribution data can blow up.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • A model outputs a full distribution; even confident ones have long tails (59 tokens to cover 90% after "404 means Not").
  • Entropy measures spread: 0.51 bits for an obvious code completion, 7.3 bits for an HTTP status code, 15.6 bits for uniform.
  • Sampling estimates shrink their error like 1/√n; 10 draws missed half the likeliest tokens.
  • Keep sequence probabilities as sums of logs: the product hit zero at token 33 in fp32 and 210 in fp64.
  • Perplexity is e^(mean loss): 22 on JSON, 60 on prose, 2.06 million on random tokens, which is worse than uniform.
  • The chain rule lets one causal forward pass score every position at once; it matched step-by-step scoring to within 5 × 10⁻⁵.
