Long Context

A model's context length is not a hard wall like GPT-2's 1,024 position rows. With rotary positions, a model can be fed any number of tokens; the questions are whether it still predicts well past the length it was trained on, whether it can find a fact buried deep inside a long prompt, and what a long prompt costs.

This chapter measures all three on Qwen2.5-0.5B, whose configuration says 32,768 tokens. Its loss stayed level far past that, to about 98,000 tokens, and rose only in the last quarter of a 131,072-token window, from 2.53 to 2.76; YaRN scaling brought that last quarter down to 2.60. Asked for a number hidden in a document, it found it every time up to 8,000 tokens, but at 30,000 it missed 3 of 20, all in the first half of the document. And a 32,768-token prompt took __PREFILL32__ to read before the first word of an answer.

  • Use RoPE scaling (position interpolation, YaRN) and know what it trades
  • Run a needle-in-a-haystack test and read its results
  • Recognise the "lost in the middle" effect
  • Estimate the time and memory a long prompt costs

Where the length limit comes from

Three things limit context. Positions: a learned table like GPT-2's simply has no row past its end (Chapter 5), while RoPE has no table but produces rotation angles the model never saw in training. Training: a model only learns to use long-range information if it was trained on long sequences, usually in a final stage after most pretraining at a shorter length. Memory and time: attention's cost grows with the square of the length and the cache with the length (Chapters 16 and 17). Qwen2.5-0.5B's config gives max_position_embeddings 32,768 and a RoPE base of 1,000,000. That number is what it was trained for, not a limit the code enforces.

Measuring beyond it needed one fix first. Transformers asked PyTorch for grouped-query attention without a mask, which on this Windows build has no fast kernel, so attention fell back to the naive path and tried to allocate 224 GiB at 64k tokens (Chapter 17). Telling transformers to repeat the key-value heads itself let the memory-efficient kernel run: a 131,072-token forward pass then used 5.4 GB.

Without any scaling, the model's loss was essentially level from 4k to 96k tokens, three times past its configured length, and rose by 0.23 only in the last quarter. The large RoPE base keeps distant angles in a range the model can cope with for a long way. The positions in this test are not all equally hard: WikiText is many short articles joined together, so most of the predictability comes from the last few thousand tokens; level loss here means the model was not disrupted by long positions, which is different from proving it uses them. The needle test below asks that question.

RoPE scaling and interpolation

RoPE scaling changes the rotation angles so that long positions look like trained ones. Position interpolation divides every position by a factor, squeezing 128k positions into the 32k range, but also blurs nearby positions. YaRN (2023) is smarter: it leaves the fast-rotating pairs that encode nearby order almost untouched, stretches the slow pairs that encode long distances, and adjusts the attention temperature. In transformers it is a config change:

model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained("Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B", rope_parameters={
    "rope_type": "yarn", "factor": 4.0,                                  # stretch to 4 × 32,768
    "original_max_position_embeddings": 32768, "rope_theta": 1e6})

The measurements show the trade exactly. Factor 2 cost up to 0.02 inside the trained range, lowered the loss beyond it (2.40 against 2.50 at 48k–64k), and brought the last quarter from 2.76 to 2.66. Factor 4 cost more at short range, up to 0.08 at 16k–32k, and was best at the very end, 2.60. Scaling buys reach with some short-range precision, and works best with a little training at the new length, which is how Qwen2.5's larger models reach 128k. Pick the smallest factor that covers the lengths you need.

Needle-in-a-haystack tests

The standard test of whether a model can use its whole context hides a fact, the needle, inside unrelated text, the haystack, and asks for it. Here the needle was "The special magic number for this document is 48213." with a random five-digit number, placed at five depths inside Wikipedia text of four lengths, four trials each, asked of Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct:

A model with half a billion parameters found a single sentence in 30,000 tokens 85% of the time. That is impressive, and also the limit of what this test shows: retrieving an exact, oddly phrased sentence is the easiest long-context task, because the needle stands out. Harder variants hide several needles, require combining them, or use a needle that resembles the haystack, and models score far lower on those. Treat a perfect needle chart as a minimum, not a guarantee.

Lost in the middle

The misses were not spread evenly. At 30,000 tokens every needle at the start, at 75% and at the end was found, while the ones at 25% and 50% were missed 3 times in 8. The 2023 paper "Lost in the Middle" found the same U shape for many models on multi-document question answering: information at the beginning and end of the context is used best, the middle worst. Part of the cause is visible in Chapter 4's attention sinks and in models' strong focus on recent tokens. With 4 trials per cell these counts are small, but the pattern is the one larger studies report. The practical rule follows: put the most important material at the start or end of a long prompt, and the question last.

Cost of long prompts

Every token of a prompt is paid for on every request. The instruct model was timed reading prompts of 1,024 to 32,768 tokens (prefill, median of 3 runs) and then generating one token against the resulting cache (median of 10).

Up to 4k tokens prefill grew in step with the prompt, about 18 µs a token, because the matrix multiplies in the MLP and projections dominate. Past that, attention's quadratic term takes over: 32 times the tokens took 81 times as long, and a user waits 1.5 seconds before the first word of the answer. The cache grew exactly linearly, 12,288 bytes a token (Chapter 16's figure for this model), while the peak was about four times the cache: during prefill each layer briefly holds activations for every prompt token at once, the widest being the MLP's 4,864-wide intermediate, about 0.32 GB per tensor at 32k. Generating each further token got 3.8 times slower from 1k to 32k. Part of that is reading a longer cache; part is this setup: the workaround at the top of the script repeats the 2 key-value heads to 14 before attention, on every step, and a kernel built for long queries rather than one. A decode kernel that reads the grouped cache directly (Chapter 17) avoids the copy. Either way, the prompt's length is a cost on every request, not a one-off.

Use case: long context or retrieval?

Million-token context windows are now advertised by several model providers, and it is tempting to paste whole codebases or document sets into every request. The measurements here say when that is worth it. A long prompt is paid for on every request in prefill time and cache memory, and retrieval from its middle is the weakest. Retrieval-augmented generation (the RAG course) instead selects the few passages that matter and puts them in a short prompt, where the model attends to them best. In practice teams combine them: retrieve generously, place the best evidence at the start or end, and use long context for the cases where the whole document truly matters, such as summarising it or following an argument across it. Prompt caching (Chapter 16) makes repeated long prefixes much cheaper.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Treating max_position_embeddings as a wall, or as a guarantee; here loss held well past it, retrieval weakened before it.
  • Turning on a large RoPE scaling factor for short prompts; factor 4 cost up to 0.08 inside the trained range.
  • Trusting a perfect needle test as proof the model reasons over the whole context.
  • Burying the key instruction in the middle of a long prompt; the middle was found least.
  • Forgetting the cost: prefill time and cache memory grow with every token of every request.
  • Not checking the attention kernel at long lengths; a fallback tried to allocate 224 GiB here.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How do models extend their context window beyond what they were trained on?

How a strong answer sounds
With rotary positions there is no position table to run out of, but positions beyond the training length produce rotation angles the model has never seen. RoPE scaling maps long positions back into the familiar range: position interpolation divides positions by a factor, which blurs nearby order; NTK-aware scaling and YaRN stretch the slow, long-wavelength pairs more than the fast ones, keeping local detail. A large RoPE base helps too. Usually this is followed by a short fine-tune on long sequences. On Qwen2.5-0.5B, configured for 32k, the loss stayed level to about 96k without scaling and rose in the last quarter of 128k; YaRN factor 2 brought that quarter from 2.76 to 2.66 at a cost of up to 0.02 inside 32k.

Deeper: A model passes a needle-in-a-haystack test at its full context length. Is it ready for long-document question answering?

How a strong answer sounds
Not necessarily. Single-needle retrieval is the easiest case: one distinctive sentence, asked for verbatim. Real tasks need several facts, combined, often phrased like the surrounding text, and performance drops sharply on multi-needle, multi-hop and distractor variants. Models also show a U-shaped "lost in the middle" pattern: even in my small test at 30k tokens, all misses were at 25% and 50% depth. I would test on the real task with realistic documents, vary the position of the key evidence, compare against a retrieval pipeline that puts only relevant passages in a short prompt, and include the cost: prefill time and cache memory grow with the prompt on every request.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Context limits come from positions, training and memory; with RoPE the configured length is a target, not a wall.
  • Qwen2.5-0.5B's loss held level to about 96k tokens, three times its configured 32k, then rose.
  • YaRN trades a little short-range loss for better long-range loss: the last quarter went from 2.76 to 2.66 (factor 2) and 2.60 (factor 4).
  • The needle was found every time up to 8k, 85% of the time at 30k.
  • Misses were in the middle: put key material at the start or end, the question last.
  • A 32k prompt took 1.5 s to read, 81 times a 1k prompt, and made each generated token 3.8 times slower.
