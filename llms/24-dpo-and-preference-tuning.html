DPO & Direct Preference Tuning

Chapter 23's RLHF needed a reward model, a sampling loop, a KL penalty and three attempts before it learned. Direct preference optimisation (DPO, 2023) gets to the same objective with none of the machinery: a little algebra turns "maximise reward while staying close to the reference model" into a loss that trains directly on preference pairs, like supervised fine-tuning.

This chapter derives that loss, writes it in a few lines, and trains Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct four ways on the same 4,000 UltraFeedback pairs, judged by Chapter 23's gold reward model. SFT on the chosen replies did not beat the untuned model (it won 46.9% of comparisons). DPO at β = 0.1 won 60.9%, and IPO, a variant, scored highest, 5.80 against 5.00, at the same reply length. With 64 test prompts those differences are suggestive rather than decisive, and the chapter shows how to tell.

From RLHF to DPO

RLHF maximises the reward minus β times the KL divergence from a reference model πref, usually the SFT model. That objective has an exact solution: the best policy reweights the reference by the exponentiated reward,

π*(y | x) = πref(y | x) · exp(r(x, y) / β) / Z(x)

where Z(x) depends only on the prompt. Turn it around and the reward is written in terms of the policy: r(x, y) = β · log(π(y | x) / πref(y | x)) + β · log Z(x). Put that into Chapter 23's Bradley–Terry loss, which only uses the difference between the rewards of two replies to the same prompt, and the awkward Z(x) cancels. What is left is a loss on the policy alone, with no reward model and no sampling: the policy is its own reward model. The quantity β · log(π / πref) is called its implicit reward.

The DPO loss

def preference_loss(policy, batch, beta, kind="dpo"):
    lp = seq_logp(policy, batch.chosen + batch.rejected)          # sum of reply-token log-probs
    with torch.no_grad(), policy.disable_adapter():               # the reference: same weights, no LoRA
        ref = seq_logp(policy, batch.chosen + batch.rejected)
    n = len(batch.chosen)
    h = (lp[:n] - ref[:n]) - (lp[n:] - ref[n:])                   # how much more than the reference it prefers chosen
    if kind == "dpo":
        return -F.logsigmoid(beta * h).mean()
    return ((h - 1 / (2 * beta)) ** 2).mean()                     # IPO: aim for a fixed margin

Two details make this cheap. With LoRA, the reference model needs no second copy in memory: switching the adapter off gives back exactly the starting weights. And the loss at the start is exactly ln 2 = 0.693 (both replies equally preferred), which the training curves confirmed. The gradient of the DPO loss weights each pair by σ(−β·h): pairs the policy still gets wrong, relative to the reference, count most, and pairs it already separates widely count almost not at all.

Training it

Every run used the same 4,000 pairs (each under 640 tokens, chosen strictly better), one epoch of 500 steps of 8 pairs, a rank-16 LoRA adapter on all seven linear layers, learning rate 5 × 10⁻⁵ with a cosine schedule. The SFT run trained on the chosen replies only. Each model then answered 64 held-out prompts greedily, and Chapter 23's gold reward model (Qwen2.5-1.5B, 77.8% accurate) scored every reply. The judge never saw those prompts, but 836 of the 4,000 training pairs (20.9%) were among the 8,000 it was trained on, so it shares part of the training signal; the comparison between methods is fairer than the absolute scores.

Measured against SFT

SFT on the chosen replies made the replies shorter, 108 tokens against 134, but did not make them better by the judge: it won 46.9% of comparisons, no better than a coin. It never sees the rejected replies, so it learns to sound like the chosen ones without learning what made them better than the alternatives. Its implicit reward even ranked held-out pairs worse than chance, 44.2%: raising the probability of replies like the chosen ones raised the rejected ones, often written in a similar style, more.

DPO learned the difference. Its implicit reward picked the chosen reply in 67.2% of held-out pairs, close to the 72.9% of Chapter 23's separately trained 0.5B reward model, and its replies won 60.9% of comparisons. Part of its gain may be length: its replies grew by 10 tokens, and Chapter 23 showed that judges favour length. A stronger tie to the reference, β = 0.5, kept the length and the win rate at the untuned model's level, though its implicit accuracy (66.2%) was nearly as good: it learned to rank pairs but changed its own replies less.

How sure is this? Each win rate is measured on 64 prompts, so its standard error is about √(0.25 / 64) ≈ 6 points. A win rate needs to pass about 62% to be two standard errors above 50%, and 60.9% does not quite. These results point the same way as the DPO paper's and many replications, but on their own they would justify a larger evaluation, not a launch decision. Preference-tuning results are often reported on a few hundred prompts with no error bars; ask for them.

Variants: IPO, KTO, ORPO

IPO (identity preference optimisation) replaces the sigmoid with a squared error that aims for a fixed margin, 1/(2β) = 5 here. DPO's loss keeps falling as the margin grows, so on pairs where one reply is always preferred it can push the probabilities to extremes; IPO stops pushing once the target is reached. Its loss started at exactly 25 = 5², as expected. Here it had the highest gold score, 5.80, with replies the same length as the untuned model's (135 against 134), so its gain was not length. Its implicit accuracy was lower, 61.2%, consistent with a loss that stops separating pairs once they reach the margin.

Two other variants were not measured here. KTO needs no pairs at all, only replies labelled good or bad, which is what a thumbs-up button collects; it weighs losses more than gains, after prospect theory. ORPO drops the reference model, adding a penalty on the odds of the rejected reply to the ordinary SFT loss, so preference tuning and SFT happen in one run. Both trade some of DPO's grounding for data or compute that is easier to get.

Use case: preference tuning without an RL team

DPO made preference tuning routine. Many open instruction-tuned models, starting with Hugging Face's Zephyr in 2023, were aligned with DPO or one of its variants on public preference sets like UltraFeedback, and libraries such as TRL run it in a few lines on one GPU. For a team with comparison data, say pairs of support replies that agents ranked, it is usually the first thing to try after SFT: no reward model to train and serve, no sampling loop, and far fewer ways to fail than RL. Its limits come from the same simplicity. It only learns from the pairs it is given, rather than exploring new replies and having them scored, and online methods that do explore often do better when a good reward model exists.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Assuming SFT on the chosen replies is enough; it won 46.9% here and ranked pairs worse than chance.
  • Reporting win rates without error bars; on 64 prompts one standard error is about 6 points.
  • Ignoring length; DPO at β = 0.1 added 10 tokens a reply, and judges favour length.
  • Judging with a model trained on the same pairs without saying so; 20.9% overlapped here.
  • Keeping a second full copy as the reference when a LoRA adapter can simply be switched off.
  • Reading a high implicit accuracy as better replies; β = 0.5 ranked pairs at 66.2% but did not beat the untuned model.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is DPO, and why did it replace RLHF in many pipelines?

How a strong answer sounds
RLHF maximises reward minus β times the KL to a reference model. That objective's optimal policy is the reference reweighted by exp(reward / β), so the reward can be written as β log(π / π_ref) plus a term that depends only on the prompt. Substituting into the Bradley–Terry preference loss cancels that term, giving a classification-style loss on pairs: −log σ(β[(log π − log π_ref)(chosen) − (log π − log π_ref)(rejected)]). No reward model, no sampling, no value function, so it is simpler, cheaper and more stable. In my runs on Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct it trained on its first settings in about 8 minutes, its implicit reward ranked 67.2% of held-out pairs correctly, and its replies beat the untuned model's 60.9% of the time, against 46.9% for SFT on the same chosen replies.

Deeper: Your DPO model wins 61% of comparisons against the base model. What else do you check before you believe it?

How a strong answer sounds
First the sample size: on 64 prompts the standard error is about 6 points, so 61% is not two standard errors above 50%; I would evaluate on several hundred prompts. Second length: my DPO model's replies were 10 tokens longer, and judges, human or model, favour length, so I would compare at matched length or use a length-controlled win rate. Third the judge: whether it was trained on overlapping data (20.9% of my pairs were in its training set), and whether it agrees with people. Fourth regressions on capabilities the preference data does not cover. I would also try IPO or a larger β; in my runs IPO scored highest with no length increase, and β = 0.5 changed little.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • DPO rewrites RLHF's KL-regularised objective as a loss on preference pairs; the policy is its own reward model.
  • With LoRA, the reference is the same model with the adapter switched off.
  • SFT on the chosen replies did not beat the untuned model (46.9%); DPO at β = 0.1 won 60.9%.
  • IPO scored highest by the judge (5.80 against 5.00) with no increase in length.
  • DPO's implicit reward ranked 67.2% of held-out pairs, near a separately trained reward model's 72.9%.
  • On 64 prompts a win rate's standard error is about 6 points: report error bars and length.
