Looking Inside: Interpretability

Every measurement so far looked at a model from the outside: its loss, its answers, its speed. Interpretability looks at the computation itself, asking what information is in the activations, where it is computed, and which parts of the network are responsible for a behaviour.

This chapter applies four standard methods to GPT-2 small and Chapter 8's tiny GPT. A linear probe read a city's country from GPT-2's activations with 87% accuracy, against 12.5% chance and 4 to 12% for a shuffled-label control. Activation patching located the indirect-object answer ("When Emma and Paul went to the store, Paul gave a drink to" → Emma) moving from the repeated name to the last token between layers 6 and 8, and singled out the same attention heads the original 2022 study found. On a repeated random sequence, GPT-2's loss fell from 13.0 to 0.39 on the second copy, carried by a handful of induction heads; the tiny GPT barely had any.

  • Read intermediate predictions with the logit lens, and attention patterns per head
  • Train linear probes, with the control that makes them meaningful
  • Use activation patching to find where and in which components a behaviour is computed
  • Detect induction heads and measure what they do
  • Know what each method can and cannot show

The logit lens

Every block of a transformer adds its output to the residual stream (Chapter 6), and the final layer norm and output matrix turn the stream into next-token logits. The logit lens applies that same final step to the stream after each block, showing what the model would predict if it stopped there. Chapter 6 did this for "The Eiffel Tower is located in the city of": in GPT-2, " Paris" became the top prediction only after 11 of the 12 blocks, while earlier layers favoured generic continuations. It is the quickest way to see at which depth an answer forms. Its limit is that intermediate layers were never trained to be read this way, so an early layer can contain an answer the lens cannot decode; tuned lenses that learn a small map per layer read them better.

Attention patterns

Each head's attention weights say which earlier tokens it reads from. Chapter 4 found three recognisable kinds in GPT-2: a previous-token head (layer 4, head 11) that put 99.97% of its weight on the token just before; heads that dump most of their weight on the first token, an "attention sink", rising from 3% in layer 0 to 67% in layer 7; and an induction head (layer 5, head 5) that attends to the token that followed an earlier copy of the current one. Patterns are suggestive but not proof of function: a head can attend somewhere and write almost nothing, and the information it reads can be the result of earlier processing at that position. The next two methods test claims instead of just looking.

Linear probes

A probe asks whether some information can be read from the activations by a simple classifier. GPT-2 read "I recently travelled to {city}" for 96 cities in 8 countries, and a logistic-regression probe was trained on the residual stream at the city's last token, at each layer, to predict the country, using 9 cities per country for training and testing on the other 3 (averaged over 4 splits):

Chance is 12.5%. The country of a city it had never been trained on could be read off a straight line through the activations, at 69% from the token embedding alone and above 80% after two blocks. The control matters: with 768 dimensions and 72 training points, a probe can fit almost anything, so the same probe was trained on shuffled labels and stayed at or below chance on held-out cities. A probe shows the information is present in a linearly readable form; it does not show the model uses it. For that, change the activations and watch the output.

Activation patching

The indirect-object task is a standard test case: "When Emma and Paul went to the store, Paul gave a drink to" should continue with " Emma", the name that was not repeated. Swapping which name is repeated, "…, Emma gave a drink to", flips the answer to " Paul". On 24 such prompt pairs GPT-2 preferred the right name by a logit difference of +2.65 on the clean prompts and −2.98 on the swapped ones. Activation patching runs the swapped prompt but copies in one activation from the clean run, and measures how much of the clean answer comes back, from 0 (no effect) to 1 (fully restored).

def patch(module, inputs, output):                          # forward hook on one block
    h = output[0].clone()
    h[0, pos] = clean_cache[layer][0, pos]                   # one position, from the clean run
    return (h,) + output[1:]
recovered = (patched_diff - corrupt_diff) / (clean_diff - corrupt_diff)

Patching the residual stream after each block, one position at a time, gave a clear picture. Up to layer 6 only one position mattered, the repeated name: restoring it there brought back 95% to 100% of the answer, and restoring any other position did nothing. From layer 8 on only the last position mattered (95% to 100%). In between, at layer 7, both carried part of it (69% and 41%). The information about which name was repeated is computed at that name and moved to the end of the sentence in layers 7 and 8. Patching each attention head's output at the last position then asked which heads do the moving:

Head (layer.head)8.108.67.99.97.310.0
Share of the answer restored29%27%25%18%14%12%

These are the heads that Wang et al. described in 2022 as GPT-2's indirect-object circuit: 7.3, 7.9, 8.6 and 8.10 are its "S-inhibition" heads, which carry the signal "this name was repeated" to the last position, and 9.9 and 10.0 are among its "name mover" heads, which copy the remaining name into the output. Twenty-four prompts and a 50-line script recovered the published circuit. Patching shows causal importance, but only for the behaviour and the corruption chosen; a different corruption can implicate different parts.

Induction heads

An induction head completes patterns: having seen "… A B … A", it predicts B. The clean test is a sequence of 60 random tokens repeated twice, which no model could have memorised. On the first copy every token is unpredictable; on the second, a model with induction heads can copy.

GPT-2 small (124M)13.030.395.5 (0.93), 6.9 (0.92), 5.1 (0.92), 7.10 (0.92), 7.2 (0.84)

The score is the attention a head pays, from each token in the second copy, to the token after its earlier occurrence. GPT-2's best heads put over 90% of their attention exactly there, and its loss on the second copy fell 33-fold: it reproduced a random sequence it had seen once. These heads, 5.1, 5.5, 6.9, 7.2 and 7.10, are the induction heads reported for GPT-2 in the literature. The tiny GPT, trained on 98 million tokens of children's stories where long exact repeats are rare, had none to speak of. Induction heads are thought to underlie much of in-context learning, and in larger models they appear abruptly during training, together with a sudden improvement at using long contexts.

Use case: debugging and auditing a model's behaviour

Interpretability is moving from research into practice. Model developers use probes to check whether a model represents things like truthfulness or a user's apparent age, activation patching and its scaled-up descendants to trace why a model gives a particular answer, and sparse autoencoders (Anthropic, OpenAI and Google DeepMind have all published large ones) to break activations into many human-readable features, some of which can be switched up or down to steer behaviour. For an engineer the same tools answer concrete questions: is a fine-tune using the feature you intended or a shortcut, which layer would a targeted edit need to touch, and does a safety behaviour depend on one fragile component. The cautions measured here apply to all of them: probes need controls, and patching results hold for the task and corruption tested.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Probing without a control; with 768 dimensions a probe can fit noise, so train one on shuffled labels too.
  • Reading "the information is there" as "the model uses it"; only an intervention shows use.
  • Treating attention weights as explanations; a head can attend and write little.
  • Generalising one patching result; it holds for the behaviour and corruption tested.
  • Testing induction on natural text; repeated random tokens separate copying from knowledge.
  • Patching prompts of different lengths; positions must line up between the clean and corrupted runs.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is activation patching, and what does it tell you that a probe does not?

How a strong answer sounds
A probe is a simple classifier trained on activations; if it works, the information is linearly present, and it needs a shuffled-label control because high-dimensional activations can fit noise. It does not show the model uses that information. Activation patching is an intervention: run a corrupted input that changes the answer, copy in one activation from the clean run, and measure how much of the clean answer comes back. On GPT-2's indirect-object task, "When Emma and Paul went to the store, Paul gave a drink to", patching the residual stream showed the deciding information sat at the repeated name up to layer 6 and at the last token from layer 8, and patching heads picked out 8.10, 8.6, 7.9 and 7.3, the S-inhibition heads from the 2022 circuit paper. So patching tells you where and through which components a behaviour is computed.

Deeper: How would you show that a model has induction heads, and why do they matter?

How a strong answer sounds
Use an input where copying is the only way to do well: a random token sequence repeated twice. Measure the loss on each copy, and for every head the attention from each second-copy token to the token after its earlier occurrence. GPT-2 small's loss fell from 13.0 on the first copy to 0.39 on the second, and heads 5.5, 6.9, 5.1 and 7.10 put over 90% of their attention on exactly that position; a 13.9M-parameter model trained on short children's stories had essentially none, loss 11.7 to 10.6. To show they are causal I would ablate those heads and check the second-copy loss rises. They matter because they are a mechanism for in-context learning, using patterns earlier in the prompt, and they tend to form abruptly during training together with a jump in long-context performance.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • The logit lens and attention patterns are quick looks; they suggest, they do not prove.
  • A probe read a city's country at 87% (chance 12.5%); a shuffled-label control stayed at or below chance.
  • Probes show information is present; interventions show it is used.
  • Patching found the indirect-object signal moving from the repeated name to the last token in layers 7 and 8.
  • Head patching recovered the published circuit's heads: 8.10, 8.6, 7.9, 7.3, then 9.9 and 10.0.
  • GPT-2's induction heads cut the loss on a repeated random sequence from 13.0 to 0.39; the tiny GPT had none.
