Tokenisation: Byte-Pair Encoding from Scratch

Before a model sees any text, a tokeniser cuts it into pieces from a fixed vocabulary and replaces each with a number. Every count that matters, how long a context is, what a request costs, how fast generation runs, is counted in those pieces. And the tokeniser is not designed by hand: it is learnt from a sample of text by a simple greedy algorithm, byte-pair encoding, which you can write in under a hundred lines.

This chapter writes that algorithm, trains it on 4 MB of English and Python on this machine, and measures what it learnt. Its compression matched Hugging Face's own trainer to three decimal places. It packed English into 3.3 bytes per token and Hindi into 1.0, because there was no Hindi in its training text. And GPT-2's professionally trained tokeniser compressed Python worse than this small one did.

Why not characters or words

Two obvious choices both fail. Characters (or bytes) give a tiny vocabulary and never meet an unknown symbol, but every text becomes very long: the 200 KB of held-out English used below is 200,345 bytes, so 200,345 tokens at one byte each. Attention costs grow with the square of the length (P6), and each token is a separate step of generation (Chapter 1). Words give short sequences, but the vocabulary is unbounded: every name, typo, number and identifier is a new word, and any word not in the vocabulary cannot be represented at all.

Subword tokenisers sit in between: common words become single tokens, rarer words are spelt out of common pieces, and anything at all can still be written down as individual bytes. Byte-pair encoding (BPE), used by GPT-2, Llama, Qwen and most current models, learns those pieces from data.

The BPE merge loop

BPE training starts with every byte as its own token, a vocabulary of 256. It then counts every pair of adjacent tokens in the training text, replaces the most frequent pair everywhere with a new token, and repeats until the vocabulary reaches the size you asked for. The list of merges, in order, is the tokeniser.

def train(text: str, vocab_size: int) -> list[tuple[int, int]]:
    counts = Counter(pretokenise(text))                     # unique words (with their leading space) and how often each occurs
    words = [list(w.encode("utf-8")) for w in counts]            # each word as a list of byte ids, 0..255
    freq = [counts[w] for w in counts]
    merges = []
    while 256 + len(merges) < vocab_size:
        pairs = Counter()
        for w, f in zip(words, freq):
            for pair in zip(w, w[1:]):
                pairs[pair] += f                              # how often this adjacent pair occurs
        best = max(pairs, key=pairs.get)                     # the most frequent pair becomes a new token
        new_id = 256 + len(merges)
        merges.append(best)
        words = [replace_pair(w, best, new_id) for w in words]
    return merges

That version recounts every pair after every merge, which is clear but slow. The trainer measured here keeps the pair counts up to date incrementally, touching only the words that contain the merged pair, and learnt 3,840 merges (a vocabulary of 4,096) from 4 MB in 11 seconds; 7,936 merges took 26 seconds.

The first merges show what the training text was made of. With English and Python mixed, merge 1 was two spaces and merge 2 four spaces, Python's indentation. Among the next forty were " t", "in", " a", "he", a newline followed by four spaces, " the", " =" and "ing". Later merges are whole words: merge 1,000 was " names", merge 2,000 " wrote". Encoding new text applies the learnt merges in the order they were learnt: within each word, find the adjacent pair with the earliest merge, replace it, and repeat until no learnt pair remains. Decoding just concatenates the bytes of each token. On all five held-out texts below, decoding the tokens gave back exactly the original text.

Byte-level BPE

Starting from the 256 possible bytes, rather than from characters, is what makes a tokeniser byte-level. Any text in any language, including emoji and binary junk, is a sequence of bytes, so it can always be encoded: in the worst case, one token per byte. There is no "unknown token", which older word-level tokenisers needed and which silently destroyed information.

Before merging, text is pre-tokenised with a regular expression that splits it into words, numbers, punctuation and whitespace, keeping a word's leading space attached to it. Merges never cross those boundaries, so a token never spans two words. This is where a classic surprise comes from: "hello" and " hello" are different tokens. In GPT-2 they are ids 31373 and 23748, "Hello" is 15496, and "HELLO" is three tokens (HE · LL · O). To the model these are unrelated symbols until training teaches it they mean the same thing (Chapter 3).

Vocabulary size trade-offs

More merges mean longer tokens and shorter sequences. Compression is measured in bytes per token: higher is better. Trained on English and Python and measured on held-out text, including three languages it never saw:

Each doubling of the vocabulary bought less: English went from 2.42 to 3.30 to 3.78 bytes per token. A bigger vocabulary also costs parameters, because the model needs an embedding row and an output row for every token (Chapter 3), and the rarest tokens get few training examples. Even at 4,096 entries this tokeniser spent merges on names that happened to be common in its training text, such as " Ali" at merge 3,000.

Hugging Face's BPE trainer, given the same text and vocabulary size, compressed every test set to the same three decimal places, which is a good check that the from-scratch version is right. Hindi and Chinese stayed at about 1.0 byte per token at every size: there were no Devanagari or Chinese characters in the training text, so no merges for them were learnt, and every byte stayed its own token. German, which shares the Latin alphabet with English, gained some compression but far less. GPT-2, trained mostly on English web text, did better on Hindi but much worse on Python, at 2.18 bytes per token against 3.39 for this tokeniser with a twelfth of the vocabulary, because GPT-2 spends one token on every space of indentation. Qwen2.5, with a large vocabulary trained on multilingual text and code, compressed everything well.

The training text decides the tokeniser. Trained on English alone, the same 4,096-token setup managed only 1.68 bytes per token on Python; trained on Python alone, 2.54 on English. Every language or format missing from the tokeniser's training data costs more tokens for the rest of the model's life, and so more money, a smaller effective context and slower generation.

Numbers are the most consequential. GPT-2 splits them into whichever digit strings were frequent in its training text, so "12345" is "123" and "45" but other numbers split differently, and the model never sees digits lined up by place value. Qwen2.5 deliberately splits every digit into its own token, which makes sequences longer but gives arithmetic a consistent structure; Llama 2 did the same. Similarly, a model asked to count the letters in a word is working from tokens that hide the letters inside them. And the single GPT-2 token for " SolidGoldMagikarp" exists because that string, a Reddit username, was frequent in the tokeniser's training text even though it was rare in the model's (see the use case below).

Use case: the SolidGoldMagikarp glitch tokens

In early 2023, researchers Jessica Rumbelow and Matthew Watkins found tokens that GPT-2 and GPT-3 models could not repeat back: asked to say " SolidGoldMagikarp", a model would say something else entirely, or behave erratically. The cause was a mismatch between two training sets. The tokeniser had been trained on text where that string, a Reddit username, appeared often enough to earn its own token; the model was then trained on data where it almost never appeared, so its embedding row received almost no training and the model never learnt what the token meant (Chapter 3 measures where such tokens sit). The measurement on this page shows the token is real: GPT-2's tokeniser turns the whole string into a single token, while Qwen2.5's spells it out in five pieces.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Estimating token counts from English and applying them to code, other languages or numbers.
  • Training a tokeniser on a different mix of text from what the model will see; English-only training halved Python's compression.
  • Assuming "hello", " hello" and "Hello" are related to the model before training has taught it so.
  • Expecting a model to count letters or line up digits that its tokens hide.
  • Growing the vocabulary for compression alone, ignoring the parameters each token adds and the rare tokens that barely get trained.
  • Changing the tokeniser of a trained model; every token id then means something different to it.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How is a byte-pair encoding tokeniser trained, and how does it encode new text?

How a strong answer sounds
Start with a vocabulary of the 256 possible bytes, and split the training text into words with a pre-tokenisation pattern so merges never cross word boundaries. Count every pair of adjacent tokens across the text, merge the most frequent pair into a new token, update the counts, and repeat until the vocabulary is the size you want. The ordered list of merges is the tokeniser. To encode new text, split it into words the same way, turn each into bytes, and repeatedly apply whichever learnt merge appears earliest in the list until none applies; decoding concatenates each token's bytes. Because it starts from bytes, any input can be encoded and decoded exactly, with no unknown tokens. On 4 MB of English and Python, 3,840 merges took 11 seconds and compressed English to 3.3 bytes per token.

Deeper: Why do language models struggle with tasks like counting the letters in a word or adding long numbers, and what can the tokeniser do about it?

How a strong answer sounds
The model never sees letters or digits directly; it sees tokens. A word is usually one or two tokens whose spelling is not in the input, so counting its letters needs knowledge the model had to memorise about each token. Numbers in tokenisers like GPT-2's are split into frequency-based chunks of varying length, so the same digit position falls in different places in different numbers, and carrying is hard to learn. Tokenisers can help: many recent ones, Qwen2.5's among them, split every digit into its own token so place value lines up consistently, at the cost of longer sequences. Beyond the tokeniser, the robust fixes are giving the model a tool such as a code interpreter for arithmetic, or asking it to spell the word out first so the letters become separate tokens it can see.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Subword tokens balance sequence length against vocabulary size; BPE learns them by merging the most frequent adjacent pair, again and again.
  • Byte-level BPE starts from the 256 bytes, so any text encodes and decodes exactly, with no unknown tokens.
  • From scratch, 3,840 merges on 4 MB took 11 seconds and matched Hugging Face's trainer to three decimals.
  • Bigger vocabularies compress better with diminishing returns: English went from 2.4 to 3.3 to 3.8 bytes per token.
  • The training text decides the tokeniser: English-only training gave Python 1.68 bytes per token, and unseen scripts stayed at 1.0.
  • Leading spaces, case and number splitting make surprising tokens; Qwen2.5 splits digits individually on purpose.

Check your understanding

At each step of BPE training, which pair is merged?

Greedy and frequency-based: merge 1 here was two spaces, from Python's indentation.

Why does a byte-level tokeniser never need an "unknown" token?

In the worst case a character becomes several one-byte tokens, as Hindi did here, but it is never lost.

A tokeniser trained only on English text was measured on Python. How did it do compared with one trained on English and Python?

It never learnt merges for indentation or common code pieces, so Python cost twice as many tokens.

What is the main cost of a larger vocabulary?

Sequences get shorter, which is the benefit; every token costs a row in two large matrices (Chapter 3).

Why are "hello" and " hello" different tokens in GPT-2?

They are ids 31373 and 23748: unrelated to the model until training teaches it otherwise.
