Supervised Fine-Tuning

A pretrained base model continues text. Asked a question, it may answer, and then it keeps going: another question, another answer, a page of something else. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) teaches it to behave like an assistant by training on example conversations, a user turn and a good reply, until replying and stopping become what it predicts.

This chapter fully fine-tunes Qwen2.5-0.5B on 13,749 human-written instruction–reply pairs from Databricks' Dolly dataset, in about seven minutes on this GPU. Before fine-tuning, the base model never once ended its turn, 0 out of 40 times; after one epoch it ended 75% of the time with short, on-topic answers, and its loss on held-out replies fell from 2.43 to 2.04. Three epochs ended its turn more reliably but did worse on new replies and cost more general ability, and a ten-times-higher learning rate made the model worse than where it started.

Base vs instruction-tuned

A base model is trained only to predict the next token of internet text. It knows a great deal, but nothing tells it that a question should be followed by one helpful answer and then silence. Given the first test question, "Identify which car manufacturer is French or American: Aixam, Buick", the base model answered "Buick is American. Buick is a brand of car, and it is not French." and then repeated that sentence until it ran out of tokens. On another it answered, then wrote "Human:" and invented the user's next question. Qwen2.5's base model has seen enough chat-like text that many of its answers are reasonable; what it lacked completely was stopping, in 40 of 40 replies.

Qwen's own Instruct model, which went through SFT and then preference tuning (Chapters 23 and 24), ended its turn 68% of the time within 200 tokens. It also answered that first question wrongly, calling Aixam American and Buick French; instruction tuning teaches behaviour, not facts.

Instruction data

Dolly has 15,011 instruction–reply pairs written by Databricks employees in 2023 across categories like open questions, brainstorming, classification and summarising, released under a CC BY-SA licence. Each becomes a conversation in the model's own chat template, so the model learns the exact token pattern it will see at use: a user turn, an assistant turn, and the end-of-turn token <|im_end|>.

def encode(row):
    prompt = tok.apply_chat_template([{"role": "user", "content": row["instruction"] + context}],
                                    tokenize=False, add_generation_prompt=True)    # ends with "<|im_start|>assistant\n"
    p = tok(prompt).input_ids
    a = tok(row["response"]).input_ids + [IM_END]                         # the reply, then the stop token
    return p + a, [0] * len(p) + [1] * len(a)                           # mask: 1 where the reply is

Conversations over 512 tokens were dropped, leaving 13,749 for training (2.05 million tokens, 45% of them reply) and 300 held out. Qwen's template also inserts a default system message, "You are a helpful assistant.", which the model sees in every example.

Masking the prompt in the loss

The usual advice is to compute the loss only on the reply: the model should learn to answer, not to write questions. In code it is a mask on the targets, and the logits only need computing where the mask is on, which with Qwen's 151,936-token vocabulary is most of the memory (Chapter 13):

h = model.model(input_ids=x, attention_mask=att).last_hidden_state[:, :-1]
tgt = y[:, 1:]                                    # y = token ids, with -100 on the prompt when masking
keep = tgt != -100
loss = chunked_cross_entropy(h[keep], model.lm_head, tgt[keep])   # logits only where they count

Measured, it made little difference here. One epoch with the reply-only loss reached a held-out reply loss of 2.038 and ended its turn 75% of the time; scoring every token reached 2.033 and 80%, and the two models wrote near-identical answers to the sample questions. With prompts this short and this varied, learning to predict them neither helps nor hurts much. Masking matters more when prompts are long and repetitive, such as a large system prompt or retrieved documents in every example: then most of the loss, and most of the gradient, would go into learning to copy them.

A full fine-tune

A full fine-tune updates all 494 million parameters. With AdamW's fp32 states that is about 8 GB before activations; gradient checkpointing and the reply-only chunked loss kept the peak at 10.2 GB. One epoch, 859 steps of 16 conversations at a learning rate of 10⁻⁵, took 7.2 minutes.

One epoch did what SFT is for: the model now answers in Dolly's style, briefly ("Buick is American, Aixam is French", correctly), and usually stops. It beat Qwen's Instruct model on held-out Dolly replies, which is no surprise, since it was trained on their style; the Instruct model writes longer, more general answers. Reply loss measures fit to this data, so the comparison that matters across models is behaviour on the task you care about (Chapter 30).

Forgetting what it knew

Fine-tuning moves the model towards the new data and away from everything else. The WikiText column measures that drift: bits per byte on Wikipedia text, which none of the fine-tuning touched. One epoch at 10⁻⁵ cost 0.023, about 2.5%, a little less than Qwen's own Instruct model gave up (0.033). Three epochs cost 0.12 and also made held-out replies worse: the model was memorising Dolly rather than learning to answer, a third pass over 13,749 examples being too many for a model this size. A learning rate of 10⁻⁴ for a single epoch did the most damage, 0.29 bits per byte, and ended up worse than the untouched base model on the very replies it was trained to write. Its answers show it: "The first step is to get a good sleep. You should not drink alcohol or alcohol with sugar."

The practical defaults follow: one to three epochs, a learning rate around 10⁻⁵ for a full fine-tune of a small model (lower for larger ones), a held-out set from the same data, and a general benchmark to watch for forgetting. When forgetting matters more, the next two chapters' parameter-efficient methods change far fewer weights.

Use case: a little data goes a long way

Meta's LIMA paper (2023) fine-tuned a 65-billion-parameter Llama on just 1,000 carefully chosen examples and got responses that people often preferred to those of models trained on far more instruction data. Their conclusion, the "superficial alignment hypothesis", was that a model's knowledge comes from pretraining and SFT mostly teaches it the format and style of an assistant. The measurements here fit: one epoch of 13,749 examples was enough to change the model's behaviour completely, and more training mostly cost general ability. For a team building a domain assistant, the lesson is to spend effort on a few thousand high-quality, representative examples in the exact format the model will see, rather than on volume.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Leaving the end-of-turn token out of the training replies; then the model never learns to stop.
  • Formatting training data differently from inference (a different template or system prompt).
  • Too many epochs; three made held-out replies worse and cost five times the general ability of one.
  • A learning rate for pretraining or LoRA in a full fine-tune; 10⁻⁴ left the model worse than the base.
  • Comparing models only by loss on your own data, which rewards matching its style.
  • Computing logits for every position with a large vocabulary; it ran out of memory here until only scored positions were kept.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is supervised fine-tuning, and what does it change in a model?

How a strong answer sounds
SFT continues training a pretrained model on example conversations, prompts paired with good replies, formatted in the chat template the model will be used with, including the end-of-turn token. It is the same next-token loss as pretraining, usually computed only on the reply tokens. It mainly changes behaviour: the model learns to answer in the expected format and style and then stop. On Qwen2.5-0.5B, one epoch over 13,749 Dolly examples took seven minutes, cut held-out reply loss from 2.43 to 2.04, and took the rate of properly ending a turn from 0% to 75%. It adds little knowledge: Qwen's Instruct model still got a simple fact wrong. The cost is some forgetting of general ability, 2.5% in bits per byte on Wikipedia after one epoch.

Deeper: Your fine-tuned model got worse at things it used to do. How do you diagnose and fix that?

How a strong answer sounds
That is catastrophic forgetting, and it scales with how far training moved the weights. I would measure it directly with a general held-out benchmark before and after, alongside the task's held-out set. The usual causes are too many epochs and too high a learning rate: in my runs three epochs raised Wikipedia bits per byte by 0.12 and a learning rate of 1e-4 by 0.29, versus 0.02 for one epoch at 1e-5, and both also hurt the task itself. Fixes, roughly in order: fewer epochs and a lower learning rate with early stopping on a held-out set, mixing some general or replayed pretraining-style data into the fine-tuning set, and parameter-efficient methods such as LoRA that change a small low-rank update instead of every weight.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • A base model continues text; SFT teaches it to answer in a format and then stop.
  • One epoch on 13,749 conversations took ending-the-turn from 0% to 75%, in seven minutes.
  • Train in the exact chat template used at inference, with the end-of-turn token.
  • Masking the prompt barely mattered with short prompts; it matters when prompts are long and repeated.
  • Three epochs or a 10× learning rate made held-out replies worse and cost far more general ability.
  • SFT changes behaviour more than knowledge: measure forgetting with a general benchmark.
