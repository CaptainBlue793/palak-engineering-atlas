Hallucination & Calibration

A language model always produces fluent text, whether or not it knows the answer. When it does not, the result is a hallucination: a confident, plausible and wrong statement. The useful question is not whether a model hallucinates, since every model does, but whether it can tell when it is likely to.

Asked 400 closed-book trivia questions, Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct answered 34% correctly and Qwen2.5-0.5B 15%; the rest were wrong, almost never "I don't know". The probability the model gave its own answer was a real signal: the 1.5B model's most confident tenth was 80% right. Asked how confident it was, it said 99% on average, which told nothing. A short fine-tune taught the 0.5B model to say "I don't know" and cut its wrong answers from 340 to 7, but it also gave up two thirds of its right ones.

  • Distinguish kinds of hallucination and where each comes from
  • Use token probability, stated confidence and sample agreement as confidence signals
  • Read calibration curves, expected calibration error and AUROC
  • Trade coverage for accuracy by answering only when confident
  • Train a model to abstain, and measure what it costs

Kinds of hallucination

Hallucinations come in two broad kinds. Factual ones state something false about the world: a wrong date, an invented citation, the wrong person. Faithfulness ones contradict or go beyond the text the model was given, as when a summary adds a claim the document never made. The first kind is about what the model knows; the second about whether it stays on its inputs, which is what retrieval and grounding (the RAG course) address. Both have the same root: a language model is trained to produce likely text, and a likely-sounding answer is produced just as fluently when the underlying knowledge is missing.

This chapter measures the factual kind, on 400 questions from TriviaQA's validation set, asked closed-book ("Answer the question with a short phrase only"), answered greedily, and counted right if the reply contains one of the answer's listed aliases. The 1.5B model was right on 34%, the 0.5B on 15%. Some of the wrong answers show how fluent a hallucination is:

Token probabilities as confidence

Three signals were tried for each answer. The token probability: the product of the probabilities of the answer's tokens, from the model's raw logits. Stated confidence: a second turn asking "How confident are you that your answer is correct? Reply with a number from 0 to 100." Sample agreement: the share of 5 samples at temperature 1 that gave the same answer as the greedy one. Two numbers describe each signal. AUROC is the probability that a random right answer gets a higher score than a random wrong one (0.5 is useless, 1.0 perfect). Expected calibration error (ECE) is the average gap between the stated confidence and the actual accuracy, across bins.

The model's own probabilities knew a good deal. On average they matched the accuracy closely (0.329 against 0.34 for the 1.5B model) and they ranked right answers above wrong ones 77% of the time. Asked in words, the models had no idea: the 1.5B model said 99% on average, gave nearly every answer the same number, and its stated confidence ranked answers no better than a coin. Sampling five times and checking agreement worked as well as token probability, which matters when probabilities are not available, as with many APIs.

Calibration curves

A model is calibrated if, among answers it gives 70% probability, 70% are right. Grouping the 1.5B model's answers by token probability: answers in the lowest bin (about 0.04) were right 12% of the time, those around 0.44 right 52%, and those around 0.97 right 87%. The curve rises steadily and sits close to the diagonal, a little above it at the low end (the model under-confident) and a little below at the top (over-confident, as with "Baseball" at 0.85). Instruction tuning and RLHF are known to make the probabilities of chat models less well calibrated than those of base models; these small models were still usefully calibrated on short factual answers. Stated confidence put 399 of 400 answers in the top bin, where 34% were right.

Calibration turns into a policy: answer only when confident. Ranking the 1.5B model's answers by token probability and answering only the top share:

Sampling consistency checks

Sample agreement is the idea behind SelfCheckGPT and related methods: if the model knows a fact, different samples tend to state it the same way; if it is guessing, samples scatter. Here it matched token probability as a signal (AUROC 0.78 against 0.77 for the 1.5B model) at the cost of five extra generations per answer. For long answers, the same check is applied claim by claim, asking whether each sentence of one answer is supported by the other samples. Its blind spot is the confident, consistent error: a model that has learnt a wrong fact repeats it every time, and agreement then signals confidence in a falsehood.

Simply asking for abstention did little. With "If you are not sure, reply 'I don't know'" added, the 1.5B model abstained on 7.5% of questions: 9.5% of those it would have got wrong and 3.7% of those it would have got right. Accuracy on the questions it did answer rose only from 34% to 36%.

Training to say "I don't know"

R-tuning (2023) teaches abstention directly. The 0.5B model answered 2,000 training questions; on the 338 it got right its answer was kept as the target, and on the 1,662 it got wrong the target became "I don't know." The model was then LoRA-tuned on those 2,000 examples for two epochs and tested on the same 400 held-out questions as before.

Wrong answers fell from 340 to 7, and when the tuned model did answer it was right 73% of the time (one more "I don't know" reply also named the right answer, and is counted as an abstention). It also abstained on two thirds of the questions it used to get right. The training targets were 83% "I don't know", so the model learnt to abstain on almost everything; it did not learn its own knowledge boundary precisely. That is the general trade: abstention can be tuned from "never" to "nearly always", and where to set it depends on whether a wrong answer or a missing one costs more. A balanced training set, or a confidence threshold like the coverage table's, gives finer control.

Use case: an assistant that knows when to look things up

A support or research assistant that answers from memory will sometimes invent a policy, a price or a citation. Products handle it in layers. A confidence signal, token probabilities where the model exposes them or agreement between a few samples where it does not, decides when an answer is risky; below a threshold the system retrieves sources (the RAG course), asks a clarifying question, or says it is not sure. Thresholds are set on a labelled sample of real questions, exactly like the coverage table here, for an accuracy the business can live with. Asking the model how confident it is, as these measurements show, is not one of the layers.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Asking the model how sure it is; stated confidence averaged 99% and ranked answers like a coin (AUROC 0.52).
  • Ignoring the token probabilities; the most confident tenth was 80% right against 34% overall.
  • Trusting agreement between samples completely; a consistently wrong model agrees with itself.
  • Expecting "say I don't know if unsure" to work; the 1.5B model abstained on only 9.5% of its wrong answers.
  • Training abstention on unbalanced targets; 83% "I don't know" targets cost two thirds of the right answers.
  • Judging calibration from one number; look at the curve and the coverage trade-off.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Why do language models hallucinate, and can they tell when they are doing it?

How a strong answer sounds
A model is trained to produce likely text, and a plausible answer is produced just as fluently when the fact is missing, so wrong answers come out confidently in tone. Whether the model "knows" it is guessing can be measured. On 400 closed-book trivia questions Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct was right 34% of the time; the probability it assigned its own answer had an AUROC of 0.77 for separating right from wrong and matched the accuracy on average, so its most confident tenth was 80% right. Agreement between five samples worked as well. But asking it in words was useless: it reported 99% confidence on almost every answer. So the information is in the probabilities, not in what the model says about itself.

Deeper: You want a model to say "I don't know" instead of guessing. How would you do it, and what would you watch?

How a strong answer sounds
Cheapest first: threshold a confidence signal, token probability or sample agreement, and abstain or retrieve below it, choosing the threshold from an accuracy-coverage curve on labelled questions. Prompting the model to abstain barely helped in my test. Training can: with R-tuning I relabelled the questions a 0.5B model got wrong as "I don't know" and LoRA-tuned it, and its wrong answers on held-out questions fell from 340 to 7 with 73% accuracy when it answered, but it abstained on two thirds of the questions it used to get right, because 83% of its targets were abstentions. I would watch both sides, wrong answers and lost right answers, balance the training data or add a threshold, and re-check on new questions, since the boundary of what the model knows shifts with every update.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Closed-book, the 1.5B model was right on 34% of trivia questions, the 0.5B on 15%; almost every miss was a confident guess.
  • Token probability was a real confidence signal: AUROC 0.77, mean close to accuracy, top tenth 80% right.
  • Stated confidence was useless: 99% on average, AUROC 0.52.
  • Agreement among 5 samples matched token probability (0.78), at five times the generation.
  • Prompting for abstention barely helped; R-tuning cut wrong answers from 340 to 7 but lost two thirds of the right ones.
  • Where to set abstention is a business choice; set it from an accuracy-coverage curve.
