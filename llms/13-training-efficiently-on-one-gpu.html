Training Efficiently on One GPU

A training run's cost is its tokens divided by its tokens per second. Chapter 11 fixed the tokens; this chapter is about the other half. A handful of standard techniques change how fast the same model trains, and how big a batch fits, without changing what it learns.

Each was measured on GPT-2 small, 124 million parameters with a 1,024-token context, on this 16 GB GPU. Together, bf16 mixed precision, a fused optimiser and torch.compile took training from 25,000 to 70,000 tokens per second, from 21% to 58% of the GPU's measured peak. Gradient accumulation gave the same gradients as a four-times-larger batch to within 3 × 10⁻⁸ while using a quarter of the memory. And on Windows, running out of GPU memory did not fail at all: it made training 28 to 47 times slower.

  • Use bf16 mixed precision and know what it saves
  • Speed up a training step with fused optimisers and torch.compile
  • Reach a large effective batch with gradient accumulation
  • Trade compute for memory with activation checkpointing
  • Measure tokens per second and model FLOPs utilisation (MFU)

Measuring the right thing

Every result below is GPT-2 small trained on real web text for 12 timed steps after a warm-up, with batches of 1,024-token sequences. Two numbers summarise a setup. Tokens per second is what sets the cost of a run. Model FLOPs utilisation (MFU) is the share of the GPU's peak arithmetic doing useful work: tokens per second times the FLOPs the model needs per token, about 6 × parameters plus attention, here 798 million per token (Chapter 11), divided by the peak. The peak used is the best bf16 matrix multiply measured on this GPU in P6, 96.6 trillion operations per second, so MFU here is relative to what this card has been seen to do, not to a datasheet.

bf16 mixed precision

The baseline does everything in fp32: 25,018 tokens per second, 21% MFU (measured, like every MFU here, against the bf16 peak, which fp32 arithmetic cannot reach), 12.1 GB at a batch of 8 sequences. Two one-line changes speed it up without touching the model.

torch.backends.cuda.matmul.allow_tf32 = True                 # TF32: fp32 matmuls on tensor cores, 10-bit mantissa

with torch.autocast("cuda", dtype=torch.bfloat16):             # bf16 mixed precision
    _, loss = model(x, y)                                   # matmuls in bf16, sensitive ops (softmax, norms, loss) in fp32
loss.backward()                                            # weights, gradients and optimiser stay fp32

TF32 lets fp32 matrix multiplies use the tensor cores with a shorter mantissa: 34,076 tokens per second, 36% faster, same memory. bf16 autocast runs the matrix multiplies in bfloat16, which has fp32's range but only 8 bits of mantissa (P1), while keeping the master weights, gradients and optimiser state in fp32: 50,189 tokens per second, twice the baseline, and 9.6 GB because activations stored for the backward pass are half the size. The loss after 15 steps was identical to three decimals, 8.097 in all three. bf16 needs no loss scaling, unlike fp16, because its range matches fp32's; that is why it became the default for training.

Fused optimisers and torch.compile

Not all of a step is matrix multiplies. AdamW updates 124 million parameters with a dozen element-wise operations each, and a plain implementation launches a separate small GPU kernel for each operation on each tensor. fused=True does the whole update in one kernel: 53,274 tokens per second, another 6%. torch.compile goes further for the model itself: it traces the forward and backward pass and generates fused kernels, so chains like add-bias, GELU and dropout read and write memory once instead of three times.

opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=3e-4, fused=True)   # one kernel for the whole update
model = torch.compile(model)                                       # first steps are slow while it compiles

Compiled, the step ran at 70,136 tokens per second, 58% MFU, and used less memory, 7.3 GB, because fused kernels keep fewer intermediate tensors. (On Windows this needs the community triton-windows package; on Linux it works out of the box.) The full ladder at a batch of 8:

2.8 times faster in total, with the same model, data and learning. Training this model on Chinchilla's 2.5 billion tokens would take 28 hours in fp32 and 10 hours with all three. Large training runs across thousands of GPUs report MFU of roughly 40–50%; the remaining gap to 100% is memory traffic, kernel launches and the non-matmul work that no technique fully removes.

Where the memory goes

Measured at a batch of 8 in bf16, P7's accounting holds: 0.48 GB of fp32 weights, 0.48 GB of gradients and 0.97 GB of AdamW's two moment estimates, together the fixed 16 bytes per parameter of mixed-precision training. The rest of the 8.7 GB peak is activations, 5.1 GB of tensors saved in the forward pass for the backward pass, and they grow with the batch. At this vocabulary size a large part of that is the output: the logits are batch × 1,024 × 50,257 numbers, about 0.8 GB in bf16 at a batch of 8, plus a 1.5 GB fp32 copy for the loss.

Gradient accumulation

When the batch you want does not fit, split it. Run several micro-batches forward and backward, letting the gradients add up, and step the optimiser once. With each micro-batch's loss divided by the number of micro-batches, the result is mathematically the same gradient as the big batch.

opt.zero_grad()
for micro in range(4):                         # 4 micro-batches of 8 = an effective batch of 32
    x, y = batch(8)
    with torch.autocast("cuda", dtype=torch.bfloat16):
        _, loss = model(x, y)
    (loss / 4).backward()                      # gradients accumulate in .grad
opt.step()

Checked on a small model in fp32, the accumulated gradient matched the single big batch to within 3 × 10⁻⁸. On GPT-2 small, 8 × 4 accumulated ran at 67,711 tokens per second in 7.8 GB, where a real batch of 32 needed far more memory than the card has. Accumulation costs a little speed, since each small batch uses the GPU less fully, and it is not quite free with batch normalisation, but transformers use layer norm, so for them it is exact.

Running out of memory on Windows

The first time these measurements were run, two setups needed more than the card's 16 GB: fp32 at a batch of 16 and compiled bf16 at a batch of 32. Neither failed. Both "succeeded" with peaks of 22.8 and 24.3 GB, at 590 and 2,451 tokens per second, 47 and 28 times slower than they should have been. On Windows, NVIDIA's driver by default lets the GPU borrow system RAM when its own memory is full ("sysmem fallback"), over a PCIe link many times slower than the card's own memory. The fix used for the memory measurements here is one line, which makes PyTorch raise an out-of-memory error instead:

torch.cuda.set_per_process_memory_fraction(15 * 2**30 / torch.cuda.get_device_properties(0).total_memory)

If a training run on a Windows machine is mysteriously slow, check its peak memory against the card's size first.

Use case: reproducing GPT-2 on a budget

In 2024, Andrej Karpathy's llm.c project reproduced GPT-2 small, trained from scratch on 10 billion tokens of FineWeb, in about 90 minutes on one node of eight H100 GPUs for around $20, a model that had cost OpenAI far more in 2019. Almost none of the saving came from new ideas about language models: it came from the techniques on this page, bf16, fused kernels, FlashAttention (Chapter 17), large batches and careful measurement of MFU, plus faster hardware. The same arithmetic applies here. At 70,000 tokens per second, those 10 billion tokens would take this single 16 GB card about 40 hours; at the fp32 baseline, 111.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Training in fp32 by default; bf16 autocast alone doubled throughput here.
  • Using fp16 without a loss scaler; bf16 has fp32's range and needs none.
  • Timing the first steps of a compiled model; they include compilation.
  • Forgetting to divide the loss by the number of accumulation steps, which multiplies the effective learning rate.
  • Raising the batch until it "still runs" on Windows; past the card's memory it ran 28 to 47 times slower instead of failing.
  • Reporting speed without MFU; 70,000 tokens per second means little without the model's size.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How would you make a single-GPU training run faster?

How a strong answer sounds
Measure tokens per second and MFU first. Then: bf16 autocast for the matrix multiplies, with fp32 master weights; a fused optimiser so the update is one kernel; torch.compile to fuse element-wise operations; FlashAttention through scaled_dot_product_attention; and a batch large enough to keep the GPU busy. On GPT-2 small on a 16 GB card that went from 25,000 tokens per second in fp32, 21% MFU, to 70,000, 58% MFU, with identical losses. If the batch I want does not fit, use gradient accumulation, which gives the same gradients, and activation checkpointing if activations are the limit, accepting extra recomputation.

Deeper: What uses the memory when training a transformer, and how do you reduce each part?

How a strong answer sounds
With AdamW in mixed precision, about 16 bytes per parameter are fixed: fp32 weights, fp32 gradients and two fp32 moment estimates; for GPT-2 small that measured 0.48, 0.48 and 0.97 GB. Those shrink with 8-bit optimisers, sharding across GPUs (ZeRO, FSDP) or parameter-efficient fine-tuning. The rest is activations, which grow with batch size, sequence length and depth; 5.1 GB at a batch of 8 here. They shrink with activation checkpointing, which recomputes them during the backward pass, with smaller micro-batches plus gradient accumulation, and with FlashAttention, which never stores the attention matrix. With a large vocabulary, the logits are a big share: batch × sequence × 50,257, plus an fp32 copy for the loss, which a chunked or fused cross-entropy avoids.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • bf16 autocast doubled throughput and cut memory, with the same loss; it needs no loss scaling.
  • Fused AdamW and torch.compile took GPT-2 small from 50,000 to 70,000 tokens per second, 58% MFU.
  • Weights, gradients and AdamW states cost 16 bytes per parameter; activations grow with the batch.
  • Gradient accumulation gave the same gradients as a 4× batch to 3 × 10⁻⁸, at a quarter of the memory.
