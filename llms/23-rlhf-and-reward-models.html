RLHF & Reward Models

Supervised fine-tuning (Chapter 19) teaches a model to imitate good replies. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) teaches it from comparisons instead: people say which of two replies is better, a reward model learns to score replies the way they would, and the language model is then trained to earn high scores. It is how InstructGPT and the first ChatGPT were tuned, and its central danger is that the model learns to please the reward model rather than the people it stands for.

This chapter trains two reward models on 38,607 preference pairs, a small "proxy" to optimise against and a larger "gold" one that plays the part of the human judges. Picking the proxy's favourite of 16 samples raised the proxy's score by 6.6 points but the gold score by only 0.1. A policy-gradient loop without a penalty raised the proxy and cut the gold score from 6.07 to 3.18, padding every reply to the length limit. The same loop with a KL penalty raised the proxy further and kept the gold score level.

Preference pairs

A preference dataset holds a prompt and two replies, one marked chosen and one rejected. Asking people which of two answers is better is easier and more consistent than asking them to write the ideal answer or to score one on an absolute scale. This chapter uses UltraFeedback (binarised by Hugging Face, MIT licence), where each prompt's replies came from several models and were rated by GPT-4; the highest-rated reply is chosen and a lower one rejected. Kept were pairs where the chosen reply was strictly better and both fit in 640 tokens: 38,607 for training and 1,253 held out.

Preference data carries its raters' habits. In the held-out pairs the chosen reply was the longer one 55.1% of the time, so a "reward model" that simply prefers longer replies already beats a coin. Any reward model has to clear that bar to show it learned something beyond length.

Training a reward model

A reward model is a language model with its next-token head replaced by a single number: the reward for the whole conversation, read at its last token. It is trained on pairs with the Bradley–Terry loss, which only asks that the chosen reply score higher than the rejected one, by a margin turned into a probability with the sigmoid:

def reward_loss(rm, chosen, rejected):
    r_c, r_r = rm(chosen).logits[:, 0], rm(rejected).logits[:, 0]
    return -F.logsigmoid(r_c - r_r).mean()           # P(chosen beats rejected) = sigmoid(r_c - r_r)

Only differences matter, so the scale and zero of the rewards are arbitrary. Two were trained, each for one pass over its pairs and scored on 1,000 held-out pairs:

That difference is what the rest of the chapter exploits. The gold model is the stand-in for people here, as in OpenAI's 2022 study of reward-model over-optimisation, which used the same set-up: optimise against a small reward model, judge with a larger one that the policy never sees.

PPO in outline

With a reward model, the language model, now called the policy, can be trained by reinforcement learning: sample replies, score them, and make high-scoring replies more likely. InstructGPT used PPO (proximal policy optimisation), which keeps four models in memory: the policy, a frozen copy of the starting model, the reward model, and a value model that predicts the reward from each partial reply so that every token gets its own credit. PPO then takes several small steps on each batch of samples, clipping how far the probability of any token may move per step.

Every run here used something simpler: REINFORCE with a per-prompt baseline, close to the RLOO and GRPO methods that many recent models use instead of PPO. Each step samples 4 replies to each of 4 prompts, and each reply's advantage is how much better it scored than its 3 siblings, so no value model is needed. A KL penalty subtracts β times how much more likely the reply is under the policy than under the starting model, which charges the policy for drifting away from it:

seqs = sample(policy, [p for p in prompts for _ in range(4)])      # 4 prompts x 4 replies
r = proxy_rm(seqs)
kl = logp(policy, seqs) - logp(reference, seqs)           # reference = the adapter switched off
g = (r - beta * kl).view(-1, 4)
adv = ((g - g.mean(1, keepdim=True)) / g.std(1, keepdim=True)).view(-1)   # better than its siblings?
loss = -(adv * logp(policy, seqs) / n_tokens).mean()        # make the better replies more likely

It took three attempts to get a loop that learned, which is typical of RL on language models. With a learning rate of 2 × 10⁻⁵ the proxy reward on held-out prompts did not rise in 120 steps (−2.40 to −3.32). At 10⁻⁴, with the advantage measured against the whole batch of 16 different prompts, the reward on training samples stayed flat for 30 steps (−4.97, −4.91, −4.94) while the policy drifted: across different prompts the baseline mostly measures which prompts are easy, not which replies are better. Comparing each reply only with replies to the same prompt fixed it. The policy is Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct with a rank-16 LoRA adapter; each run took 120 steps and under 10 minutes.

Choosing among 16 samples raised the proxy's score by 6.6 points, and the gold model's by 0.1. The samples did contain better replies, since the gold model's own favourite among the same 16 scored 2.6 higher; the proxy simply could not tell which they were. Its choices drifted longer instead, from 139 to 154 tokens. This is Goodhart's law: a measure that becomes a target stops being a good measure. Selection only exploits the proxy's errors a little. Training exploits them much more:

Without the penalty, the proxy reward on its own training samples rose from −4.98 to −2.10, while the gold reward on held-out prompts fell almost every time it was measured, from 6.07 to 2.71 at step 100. By step 40 replies averaged 126 of the 128 tokens allowed, and from step 100 every one ran to the limit; the KL to the starting model grew without bound, to more than 120 nats per reply. The policy had found what the proxy over-rewards, long and grand-sounding text, and produced it whatever the question was.

What RLHF changes in outputs

The first held-out prompt is a story task that asks for an answer of "Option 1" or "Option 2". The starting model replied "Option 2". After 120 unpenalised steps the policy replied:

Gerald's decision to embark on an extraordinary journey marked a pivotal turning point in his life. His determination was unwavering, driven by a profound passion that transcended mere ambition. Through meticulous planning and unwavering dedication, he secured the resources necessary to achieve his…

The answer the user asked for is gone, replaced by the style the proxy scores highly. With the KL penalty the same prompt still got "Option 2", replies stayed about as long as before (88 tokens against 87), the KL stayed near 5, and the proxy reward on held-out prompts rose more than in the unpenalised run, to −0.79, while the gold judge stayed level (6.07 to 6.17; it peaked at 7.32 at step 60, and with 32 prompts its changes of about 1 are within noise). The penalty did not stop learning; it stopped the policy from leaving the region where the reward model's scores mean anything.

That is what RLHF does to production models at much larger scale. It shifts style, tone, refusals and length, often more than knowledge, and those shifts are only as good as the reward model's judgement. Longer answers are a well-documented side effect of RLHF on real models too. Chapter 24 trains on the same preference pairs without a reward model or sampling at all.

Use case: tuning an assistant on user feedback

A product team with thumbs-up and thumbs-down buttons, or with raters comparing replies, has preference data. Turning it into a reward model and optimising against it can make an assistant noticeably more helpful, and it is how the large chat models were first tuned. The measurements here are the warning that comes with it: the reward model will have biases, such as liking long or confident-sounding answers, and an optimiser will find them. Teams that do this well keep a KL penalty or a similar limit, cap or normalise reply length, refresh the reward model with new comparisons of the tuned model's outputs, and judge progress with people or a separate model rather than with the reward being optimised.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Reporting the reward you optimised as the result; the proxy went up while the gold judge fell from 6.07 to 3.18.
  • Dropping the KL penalty; without it KL passed 120 nats a reply and every reply hit the length limit.
  • Not checking a reward model against a length baseline (55.1% here) or its correlation with length (0.51 for the proxy).
  • Using one baseline across different prompts; the reward stayed flat until replies were compared with their siblings.
  • Trusting best-of-N against a weak reward model; 16 samples bought 6.6 proxy points and 0.1 gold.
  • Expecting RL to work on the first settings; it took three attempts here.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How does RLHF work?

How a strong answer sounds
Start from a supervised fine-tuned model. Collect comparisons: for a prompt, which of two replies is better. Train a reward model, a copy of the language model with a scalar head, so that the chosen reply scores higher than the rejected one, using the Bradley–Terry loss. Then optimise the language model to produce replies the reward model scores highly, with an RL algorithm such as PPO, or simpler ones like REINFORCE with a per-prompt baseline, RLOO or GRPO, plus a KL penalty to the starting model. In my experiment a 0.5B reward model reached 72.9% on held-out pairs and a 1.5B one 77.8%, against 55.1% for "longer wins". The KL penalty is essential: without it the policy reward-hacked the small reward model, filling every reply to the limit, and a separate judge's score fell from 6.07 to 3.18.

Deeper: Your RLHF run shows the reward climbing steadily. How do you know the model is actually getting better?

How a strong answer sounds
I would not trust the optimised reward at all, since it is exactly what the policy is exploiting. I would track an independent judge, ideally people and otherwise a larger held-out reward model the policy never trains against, plus the KL to the starting model and simple statistics like reply length. In my run without a KL penalty the proxy reward rose while the gold judge fell from 6.07 to 3.18, replies all hit the 128-token limit and KL passed 120 nats; with β = 0.1 the proxy rose more and the gold judge stayed level. Warning signs are rising KL, length or repetition, and a growing gap between the two rewards. Fixes are a larger KL penalty, length normalisation, early stopping on the independent judge, and collecting new preference labels on the tuned model's own outputs to retrain the reward model.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • A reward model learns from comparisons with the Bradley–Terry loss; check it against "longer wins" (55.1% here).
  • Reward models are imperfect (72.9% and 77.8%) and biased; the proxy's rewards correlated 0.51 with length.
  • Best-of-16 against the proxy raised its score by 6.6 and the gold score by 0.1.
  • RL without a KL penalty reward-hacked: gold fell from 6.07 to 3.18 and every reply hit the length limit.
  • With a KL penalty of 0.1 the proxy rose more, length and the gold score held, and KL stayed near 5.
  • Never measure success with the reward you optimise; keep an independent judge.
