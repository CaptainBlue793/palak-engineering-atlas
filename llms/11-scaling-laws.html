Scaling Laws

Training a large model is expensive enough that you get one attempt. Scaling laws are how labs choose its size, its data and its expected loss in advance: train many small models, fit a smooth curve to how loss falls with size, data and compute, and extend the curve to the run you cannot afford to repeat.

This chapter does it at small scale on this GPU: 30 models from 0.45 to 77 million parameters, trained on Chapter 8's TinyStories data at four compute budgets, 1 × 10¹⁴ to 3 × 10¹⁵ operations, 28 minutes of training in all. At every budget there was a best size, and it grew steadily with compute. A law fitted on the three smaller budgets predicted the losses of the seven models at the largest budget to within 0.03 on average, before they were looked at. It also showed where the simplest version of the law goes wrong.

  • Count training compute as 6 × parameters × tokens
  • Read IsoFLOP curves and find the compute-optimal model size
  • Fit a power law and a Chinchilla-style loss law
  • Test a scaling law's prediction on a budget it was not fitted to
  • Use a fit to plan a run, and know when to break the "optimal" rule

Loss against parameters, data and compute

Three quantities describe a training run: the number of parameters N, the number of training tokens D, and the compute C. Each parameter takes part in one multiply-add per token on the way forward (2 operations) and about two more on the way back (4 operations), so C ≈ 6 N D (P6). A fixed budget of compute can buy a big model trained briefly or a small one trained long. Which is better?

Counting N needs care at small scale. Kaplan and colleagues at OpenAI (2020) counted only the parameters inside the blocks; the Chinchilla paper from DeepMind (2022) counted all of them. Here the difference is huge: in the smallest model, 2 layers of width 48, the blocks have 55,296 parameters and the tied output layer, 8,192 × 48, has 393,216. Every token passes through that output matrix, so it costs compute like any other layer. A first version of this sweep counted only block parameters, and at the smallest budget the smallest model came out best, with no optimum in range. Counting the output layer, as Chinchilla does, fixed that, and is what every number below uses.

N = 12 * layers * d**2 + vocab * d            # blocks + tied output layer (Chapter 7)
for C in [1e14, 3e14, 1e15, 3e15]:                 # compute budgets, in operations
    for layers, d, heads in SHAPES:                    # 10 shapes, 0.45 M to 77 M parameters
        D = C / (6 * N)                              # the tokens this budget buys at this size
        if 1.5e6 <= D <= 3e8:
            train(layers, d, heads, tokens=D)         # Chapter 8's loop, cosine schedule fitted to D

Each run uses Chapter 8's training loop with its learning-rate schedule stretched to its own length, which matters: a model stopped part-way through a longer schedule looks worse than it is, and this is one of the things Chinchilla corrected in the earlier work. Four of the 30 runs needed more tokens than the 117-million-token training set holds and saw it up to 2.3 times; Chapter 12 discusses what repetition costs.

The optimum moved up with every budget, and the ratio of tokens to parameters at the optimum stayed between 11 and 15. Chinchilla found about 20 for web text at far larger scale. The exact number depends on the data and the setup, but the shape of the answer is the same: grow parameters and data together, roughly in proportion. Missing the optimum is costly. At 3 × 10¹⁵, the 77-million-parameter model trained on 6.5 million tokens reached a loss of 2.55, worse than the best model at 3 × 10¹⁴, which had a tenth of the compute.

Fitting a power law

On log-log axes, the optimal size against compute is close to a straight line, a power law N_opt = k · Ca. Fitted on the three smaller budgets only, the exponent was a = 0.56, so compute four times larger buys a model about 2.2 times larger trained on about 1.8 times the data. Chinchilla's exponent was about 0.5; Kaplan's earlier work had 0.73, which favoured much bigger models trained on less data, partly because of how parameters were counted and how runs were stopped.

Where the curve bends

The pure power law for loss, L = k · C−0.136, was too optimistic by 0.08. It assumes loss can fall towards zero forever, but no model can predict a story perfectly: some of what comes next is genuinely unpredictable. Chinchilla's form builds that in:

L(N, D) = E + A / Nα + B / Dβ

E is the irreducible loss of the text itself, and the two other terms are what a finite model and finite data add to it. Fitted on the 23 runs of the three smaller budgets, it gave E = 1.24, α = 0.62 and β = 0.45, matched those runs to 0.05, and then predicted all seven runs of the unseen budget with a mean error of 0.030, from the 1.8-million-parameter model trained on 272 million tokens (predicted 1.963, measured 1.994) to the 77-million one trained on 6.5 million (2.529, 2.555). As compute grows, loss approaches E rather than zero, so each tenfold increase buys less: on a log-log plot the straight line bends. Plenty of real curves bend in less predictable ways too, when data runs out and is repeated, when a learning rate that suited small models is too high for large ones, or when a capability appears that smooth loss does not reflect.

Using it to plan a run

Given a compute budget, the fit gives a size, a token count and an expected loss; given a target loss, it gives the compute needed. For this data, at 10¹⁶ operations the Chinchilla-form fit suggests about 8.3 million parameters and 200 million tokens, a run of around six minutes here (and 1.7 passes over the data), and a loss near 1.70. That is an extrapolation over one more factor of three, the same distance as the test above. Further out the uncertainty grows, which is why labs fit across many orders of magnitude before a large run.

"Compute-optimal" also answers only one question: the best loss for a fixed training budget. A model that will serve billions of requests is cheaper overall if it is smaller and trained far past the optimum, since every token it generates costs less. Meta trained Llama 3's 8-billion-parameter model on over 15 trillion tokens, nearly 2,000 per parameter, a hundred times the Chinchilla ratio, for exactly this reason. The fitted law still says what that costs: here, the 1.84-million-parameter model trained on 148 tokens per parameter reached 1.994 at the largest budget, against 1.832 for the optimal size, about 0.16 worse for a model nearly four times smaller to run.

Use case: Chinchilla and the right size for a budget

In 2022, DeepMind's Chinchilla paper trained over 400 models and concluded that the large models of the time were too big for their data: parameters and tokens should grow together. To test it, they trained Chinchilla, 70 billion parameters on 1.4 trillion tokens, with the same compute as their earlier Gopher, 280 billion parameters on 300 billion tokens. Chinchilla was better on nearly every benchmark, and, at a quarter of the size, much cheaper to run. Since then, scaling-law fits like this chapter's, done at small scale first, have been how labs size their large runs, and inference costs have pushed models deliberately past the optimum, as with Llama 3. The fit is not only for giants: here it took 28 minutes of small runs to predict a larger run to within a few hundredths.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Leaving the output layer out of N for small models; it was most of the compute here, and the first sweep found no optimum without it.
  • Comparing runs stopped part-way through one long schedule; each run needs its schedule fitted to its own length.
  • Fitting a pure power law to loss; it ignored the floor and was 0.08 too optimistic.
  • Trusting a fit you never tested on a budget it was not given.
  • Treating 20 tokens per parameter as a constant; it was 11–15 here and depends on data and setup.
  • Optimising training compute alone for a model that will be served heavily; smaller and longer-trained can be cheaper overall.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What did the Chinchilla paper change about how models are sized?

How a strong answer sounds
For a fixed compute budget, roughly 6 × parameters × tokens, there is a model size that gives the lowest loss, found by training several sizes at the same budget and picking the bottom of the valley. Chinchilla showed that the optimal parameter count and token count should grow at about the same rate, around 20 tokens per parameter for their data, while earlier practice had built much larger models trained on too little data. Their 70B Chinchilla trained on 1.4T tokens beat the 280B Gopher at the same compute. I reproduced the method on a small scale: across four budgets the optimum moved from 1 to 6 million parameters at 11 to 15 tokens per parameter, and the optimal size grew as compute to the power 0.56.

Deeper: You have a budget for one big training run. How would you use scaling laws to plan it, and how would you know the prediction is trustworthy?

How a strong answer sounds
Run a sweep of small models over several compute budgets, each with its learning-rate schedule matched to its length, and count all parameters that cost compute, including embeddings or the output layer at small scale. Fit the IsoFLOP valleys to get the optimal size against compute, and fit a loss law with an irreducible term, L = E + A/N^α + B/D^β, because a pure power law ignores the floor; mine was 0.08 too optimistic, while the Chinchilla form was 0.04 off. Crucially, hold out the largest budget and predict it before looking: my fit on three budgets predicted seven unseen runs to a mean error of 0.03. Then extrapolate only as far as the evidence supports, and adjust for inference cost if the model will be served heavily, by choosing a smaller model trained on more tokens.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Training compute is about 6 × parameters × tokens; a budget buys many combinations of size and data.
  • At each budget there is a best size; here it grew from 1.0 M to 6.1 M as compute grew thirtyfold.
  • The optimum was 11–15 tokens per parameter; the optimal size grew as C0.56.
  • Count every parameter that costs compute; at small scale the output layer dominates.
  • A law with a floor, E + A/Nα + B/Dβ, predicted an unseen budget to 0.03; a pure power law was 0.08 off.
  • Compute-optimal is about training cost; heavily served models are often trained far past it.
