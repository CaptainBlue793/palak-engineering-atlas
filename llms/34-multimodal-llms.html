Multimodal LLMs

A language model reads tokens. To read an image, a multimodal model cuts it into small square patches, turns each into a vector with a vision encoder, and passes those vectors, through a small projector, into the language model as if they were word embeddings. From then on the image is just more context, with the same costs and limits as text.

This chapter measures that pipeline in Qwen2-VL-2B-Instruct, on images drawn so that every answer is known. A 448-pixel image became 1,024 patches and 256 tokens, exactly as the processor reported. Doubling the image's width and height quadrupled its tokens: 2,304 at 1,344 pixels, taking a full second to read. Where it failed was telling: it counted 1, 2 or 3 circles perfectly and almost never 4 or more, and text 8 pixels high was read half the time at full resolution and never at half.

  • Estimate image tokens, prefill time and memory from resolution
  • Know how audio is handled the same way
  • Test the known weak spots: counting, small text and resolution

Patches as tokens

The Vision Transformer (2020) showed that an image can be handled like a sentence: cut it into fixed squares, flatten each into a vector, and run a transformer over the sequence. Qwen2-VL uses 14 × 14-pixel patches. For a 448 × 448 image that is a 32 × 32 grid of 1,024 patches, each a vector of 14 × 14 × 3 = 588 numbers:

x = image.permute(2, 0, 1)                                   # (3, 448, 448)
patches = x.unfold(1, 14, 14).unfold(2, 14, 14)            # (3, 32, 32, 14, 14)
vectors = patches.permute(1, 2, 0, 3, 4).reshape(1024, 588)   # one row per patch

The model's processor reported the same grid, 1 × 32 × 32, and the prompt it built contained 256 image tokens, not 1,024: after the vision encoder, each 2 × 2 block of neighbouring patches is merged into one token, which cuts the language model's work by four. So one image token covers 28 × 28 pixels. Qwen2-VL also gives each image token a two-dimensional position (row and column) instead of a place in a line, so the language model can tell what is above what.

Vision encoders and projectors

A multimodal model of this kind has three parts. The vision encoder, a vision transformer pretrained on images and captions, turns patches into vectors that describe their content in context. The projector, here an MLP that also does the 2 × 2 merge, maps those vectors into the language model's embedding space. The language model then reads image and text tokens together. Counting Qwen2-VL-2B's parameters:

Vision encoder631 M29%
Projector (merger)34 M1.5%

Training usually proceeds in stages: first only the projector, on image-caption pairs, so the language model learns to "see" without forgetting language; then the projector and the language model, often the encoder too, on instruction data with images. LLaVA (2023) showed that a simple projector trained this way was enough to connect a good vision encoder to a good language model, which is why the design spread so quickly.

Image tokens per picture

Because each token covers a fixed patch of pixels, an image's cost grows with its area. Measured on Qwen2-VL-2B, one image plus a short question, prefill only (median of 5):

Tokens follow width × height ÷ 784 exactly, and time grows faster than tokens at the top, as attention's quadratic term takes over (Chapter 18). A single large photo cost as much as a 2,300-token document, on every request that includes it. Other designs fix the cost instead: SmolVLM's processor turned every square image here, from 224 to 1,344 pixels, into the same 1,088 tokens (it resizes to a grid of tiles plus a downscaled overview), and the 16:9 one into 832. Fixed tiling makes cost predictable; native resolution keeps detail in large images and saves tokens on small ones. Either way, resizing images before sending them is the first cost lever.

Audio in outline

Speech goes the same way, with a different encoder. Audio is turned into a spectrogram, its energy at each frequency over short time windows, and an audio encoder such as Whisper's turns it into a sequence of vectors, typically one per 20 to 40 ms; a projector maps them into the language model. Qwen2-Audio and similar models are built like this, and models that also speak add a decoder that turns generated tokens back into sound. The trade-offs carry over directly: tokens per second of audio set the cost, and the encoder's resolution limits what can be heard. Audio was not measured here.

Where it fails

Three tests on images drawn so that every answer is known, at 448 × 448 unless stated. Counting: 1 to 9 coloured circles at random positions, six images for each number, "How many circles are in the image?":

Circles123456789
Right, of 6666101000
Typical answer123345677–8

Perfect up to 3, then almost always one short. The errors were not random guesses: the model consistently undercounted by about one, as if it recognised small groups at a glance and estimated larger ones. Counting is a known weakness of vision-language models; it needs the model to keep track of many separate objects, which a single pass over merged patch tokens does poorly. Reading: a random six-letter code in black on white, at six font sizes, ten codes each, at full resolution and with the image halved to 224 pixels:

Text height8 px10 px12 px16 px24 px40 px
Read exactly, 448-pixel image5/109/1010/1010/1010/108/10
Same image halved to 224 pixels0/101/105/109/109/109/10

Small text failed as resolution fell, and the failures were confident: "XVWOYB" was read as "XWOWYS", "BPCLJV" at the lower resolution as "Bolli". An 8-pixel letter at half resolution is 4 pixels high, a seventh of one 28-pixel token. The misses at large sizes were different: "CIIQLR" read as "CIQLR", doubled letters merged, the kind of error that comes from the language model's prior about plausible strings rather than from the pixels. Spatial relations were easy at this scale: asked whether a red square was left or right of a blue circle, it was right 40 times in 40, with no bias to either answer.

Use case: reading documents and screenshots

Teams use vision-language models to extract fields from invoices, read charts, describe screenshots for support, or let agents operate a graphical interface. The measurements here are the practical guide. Resolution is a cost and an accuracy dial at once: small print needs enough pixels per letter, but every doubling of width and height quadruples the tokens and more, so crop to the region that matters rather than sending a whole page at high resolution. Do not trust counts above a handful, or exact strings in small text, without a check; where text matters, a dedicated OCR step or asking the model to quote and verify reduces errors. And because the image is context, a picture can carry instructions too: text inside an image is a prompt-injection channel like any document (Chapter 20).

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Sending full-resolution images by default; 1,344 pixels cost 2,304 tokens and a second of prefill.
  • Downscaling images with small text; 8-pixel text went from 5 in 10 read to none.
  • Trusting counts above three; from 4 circles up the model was almost always one short.
  • Treating a confident reading as correct; "BPCLJV" became "Bolli".
  • Assuming every model's image cost is the same; fixed tiling spent 1,088 tokens even on a 224-pixel image.
  • Forgetting that images are context; text in an image can inject instructions.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How does a language model read an image?

How a strong answer sounds
The image is cut into small square patches, 14 × 14 pixels in Qwen2-VL, and each is flattened into a vector; a vision transformer encodes the patches in context; a small projector maps the result into the language model's embedding space, here also merging each 2 × 2 block into one token; and the language model reads those image tokens alongside the text, with two-dimensional positions so it knows the layout. In Qwen2-VL-2B a 448-pixel image became 1,024 patches and 256 tokens, and the parameters split 631 million for the vision encoder, 34 million for the projector and 1.5 billion for the language model. Models like LLaVA showed that training mainly the projector, then fine-tuning on image instructions, is enough to connect a vision encoder to a language model.

Deeper: A multimodal feature is slow and misses small details. What do you look at?

How a strong answer sounds
Tokens per image first. With native-resolution models the cost grows with area, about one token per 28 × 28 pixels: I measured 64 tokens at 224 pixels and 2,304 at 1,344, with prefill rising from 41 ms to over a second, so I would resize or crop to the region that matters, and cache repeated images. For missed details, check pixels per detail: 8-pixel text was read half the time at full resolution and never after halving the image, so the fix may be a higher resolution on a crop rather than the whole image. Some failures are not resolution at all: the model counted three objects perfectly and four or more almost never, so for counting or exact text I would add a dedicated tool or a verification step rather than trusting the model's answer.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Images become patch tokens: a vision encoder, a projector, then the language model reads them like text.
  • Qwen2-VL: 14-pixel patches merged 2 × 2, so one token per 28 × 28 pixels; 448 pixels → 256 tokens.
  • Cost grows with area: 2,304 tokens and a second of prefill at 1,344 pixels; fixed tiling spends a constant 1,088.
  • Counting failed above three, almost always one short.
  • Small text needs pixels: 8-pixel text read 5/10 at full resolution, 0/10 at half.
  • Crop and resize deliberately; images are context, with context's costs and risks.
