The LLM Interview Playbook

Interviews for roles that train, tune or serve language models use four formats: concept questions ("why does the KV cache help?"), back-of-envelope maths ("how much memory to fine-tune a 7B model?"), debugging scenarios ("the loss is great but the model is useless") and design rounds ("build an assistant for our support team"). Each rewards the same habit: a mechanism, a number, and a way to check it.

This chapter is a playbook for each format, built from the measurements in this course, with links back to where each was made. Its centre is a design framework of eight decisions; the panel shows what a strong candidate would decide for four common briefs, and the evidence behind it. A calculator does the arithmetic interviewers expect you to do in your head.

Concept questions

Concept questions test whether you can explain a mechanism simply and know its limits. A reliable pattern is the definition, the mechanism, a measured consequence, and the remedy or trade-off. "Why do we use a KV cache?" → each new token's attention needs the keys and values of every earlier token; caching them turns decoding from recomputing the whole prefix into one small step; at 896 tokens GPT-2 took 2.6 ms per token cached against 6.0 uncached; the cost is memory, 36,864 bytes per token for GPT-2, which is why grouped-query attention exists (16). The questions that come up most, by where this course measured them:

  • What does a tokeniser do, and why do some languages cost more tokens? (2, P5) · How does attention work, and why scale by √d? (4) · How do models know token order? (5)
  • Pre- or post-norm, and why residual connections? (6) · What changed from GPT-2 to modern open models? (15) · What do scaling laws say about model size against data? (11)
  • Greedy, temperature, top-p, beam: when each? (9) · Why does long context cost so much, and how is it extended? (17, 18)
  • SFT, LoRA, QLoRA: which and why? (19, 21, 22) · RLHF against DPO? (23, 24) · Distillation? (29)
  • Quantisation: what is lost and when is it faster? (25) · Continuous batching and paged attention? (26) · Speculative decoding: when does it pay? (27)
  • How do you evaluate an LLM, and why are benchmark numbers fragile? (30) · Why do models hallucinate, and can they tell? (31) · What is a mixture of experts? (33)
  • How do images become tokens? (34) · How are models taught to refuse, and how is it undone? (35) · What does more thinking at answer time buy? (36)

Debugging scenarios

"Something is wrong with our model. What do you do?" Answer with a method: reproduce it on a small case, compare against a known-good baseline, read the signals and samples, change one thing at a time. Typical scenarios and the first thing to check:

  • Training loss excellent, model useless: targets not shifted (0.0017 training loss, 25.6 validation) or padding counted in the loss (37).
  • NaN at the first steps: fp16 weights or optimiser state; Adam's ε rounds to zero (37, P1).
  • Loss stuck high, activations huge: learning rate or initialisation; read the update-to-weight ratio (37, 14).
  • Out of memory only at long sequences: full-vocabulary logits or an attention fallback to the math kernel (17, 19).
  • Replies run on into invented turns: the stop token, or a mismatched chat template (20).
  • Greedy output loops: decoding, not the model; sample or penalise repetition (9).
  • Reward climbs, users complain: the policy is gaming the reward model; check an independent judge and the KL (23).
  • Benchmark jumped after a data refresh: contamination; rotate the options or test on fresh questions (30).
  • Quantised model fine on perplexity, broken on a task: evaluate the task itself; small models are fragile at 4 bits (25).
  • Same prompt, different answers at temperature 0: batch-dependent bf16 rounding, not a bug (26).
  • Structured output always parses but fields are wrong: test the grammar itself (28).

Design prompts

A design round gives a vague goal and 45 minutes. Spend the first five clarifying: who uses it, what a correct output is and how a program could check it, the latency and volume, and what is sensitive. Then work through the decisions in order, simplest option first, and say how you would measure each. Use case: preparing for the loop

Interview loops for LLM roles, whether research engineering, applied ML or inference infrastructure, usually combine these formats with a coding round (implement attention, a sampler or a training step; Chapters 4, 7 and 9 have from-scratch versions). Practise each format aloud: explain one concept a day with a number from this course, do one back-of-envelope estimate without a calculator and check it with the one above, and run one design brief end to end against the rubric in the mock-interview room. The labs have published guidance that reads like design-round answers, from model cards that state evaluation and limitations to engineering posts on serving and evaluation; reading a few shows what "measure every choice" looks like in practice.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Answering a design brief with a model choice before asking how success is checked.
  • Reaching for fine-tuning first; prompting and retrieval are cheaper to try and to change.
  • Quoting peak hardware numbers; plan with measured utilisation and batch size.
  • Treating a benchmark or judge score as ground truth without error bars or bias checks.
  • Debugging by changing several things at once or without a baseline run.
  • Forgetting safety and the supply chain: refusals, injection, pickles and poisoned data.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How much GPU memory does it take to fine-tune a 7B model, and how would you fit it on one 16 GB card?

How a strong answer sounds
A full fine-tune keeps fp32 weights, gradients and two Adam moments, about 16 bytes per parameter, so 7 billion parameters need about 112 GB before activations: impossible on one card. LoRA freezes the base, so only the adapters need gradients and optimiser state, but the frozen weights still take 2 bytes each in bf16, 14 GB; when I tried, Qwen2.5-7B's weights alone took 14.19 GB and one 512-token training step ran out of memory under a 15 GB cap. QLoRA stores the frozen weights in 4-bit NF4, about 4.13 bits each with the scales, which brought them to 5.18 GB because the embeddings stay in 16 bits, and training fit at a peak of 11.8 GB with gradient checkpointing and 512-token sequences. I would also budget the fp32 cast that peft applies to non-quantised layers, about 2 GB for that model.

Deeper: Design an assistant that answers employees' questions from internal documents. Walk me through it.

How a strong answer sounds
First the success check: a set of a few hundred real questions with answers written by experts, scored for correctness and for citing the right document, with error bars. Then the simplest system: retrieval of relevant passages into the prompt of a capable model, rather than fine-tuning facts in, because documents change and retrieval cites its sources; put the best evidence at the start or end of the prompt, since models miss the middle of long contexts. Hallucination control: abstain or say so when retrieval finds nothing or the model's confidence is low, using token probabilities or sample agreement rather than asking it how sure it is. Fine-tune only for style or format, with LoRA. For serving, size the KV cache and batch for the expected concurrency, and check cost against an API. Finally guard against prompt injection in the documents and keep the evaluation running on every change.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Answer concept questions with a definition, the mechanism, a measured number and the trade-off.
  • Know the formulas: 12Ld² + Vd, 2N and 6ND, 16 bytes per parameter, 2·L·kv·hd·bytes per token.
  • Debug with a method: reproduce small, compare to a baseline, read signals and samples.
  • In design rounds, start from the success check and the simplest option, then size and measure.
  • Name what you measured, what you estimated, and what would change your answer.
