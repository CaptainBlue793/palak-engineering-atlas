GPU Memory Budgets

On one GPU, memory decides what you can do long before speed does. A 7-billion-parameter model needs 14 GiB just to hold its weights in bf16; training even a half-billion-parameter one with a normal optimiser needs most of a 16 GB card before a single example goes through it. Knowing where the bytes go lets you decide, before anything runs, whether a model fits, what batch size will work, and which trick to reach for when it does not.

This chapter builds the budget piece by piece and checks each piece on a 16 GB RTX 5070 Ti: weights that load to within 1% of the arithmetic, AdamW's 8 bytes per parameter, activations that grow by about 1 MiB per token of batch, a KV cache of 36 KiB per generated token, and the out-of-memory error you get when the sum is too big.

  • Compute a model's weight memory from its parameter count and dtype
  • Add up what training needs: weights, gradients, optimiser state and activations
  • Estimate how memory grows with batch size and sequence length
  • Read torch.cuda statistics and nvidia-smi and know why they differ
  • Respond to an out-of-memory error with the right fix

Bytes per parameter

A model's weights take parameters × bytes per parameter: 4 in fp32, 2 in bf16 or fp16, 1 in int8, half a byte at 4 bits. That arithmetic is exact enough to plan with. Loaded onto the GPU and measured with torch.cuda.memory_allocated():

The few extra MiB are small buffers that are not parameters. By the same arithmetic, a 7B model in bf16 needs 14.2 GiB for weights alone, leaving under 2 GiB of this 15.9 GiB card for everything else; that is the main reason quantisation (Chapter 25) exists.

Weights, gradients and optimiser state

Training needs much more than the weights. Backward fills a gradient for every parameter, and AdamW keeps two running averages per parameter (P2). With the common recipe used on this page, fp32 master weights and bf16 arithmetic through torch.autocast, each parameter costs:

# per parameter, mixed-precision training with AdamW
weights   = 4    # fp32 master copy
grads     = 4    # fp32, from backward() until the optimiser step
adam_m    = 4    # fp32 running mean of gradients
adam_v    = 4    # fp32 running mean of squared gradients
total     = 16   # bytes per parameter, before a single activation

Measured after a training step, with gradients freed (zero_grad(set_to_none=True)), GPT-2 held 12 bytes per parameter of weights and optimiser state, 1.4 GiB, and Qwen2.5-0.5B held 5.5 GiB. At 16 bytes per parameter, Qwen2.5-1.5B would need 23 GiB and a 7B model 113 GiB before any data goes in. On a 16 GB card, full fine-tuning stops at well under a billion parameters, which is the problem LoRA (Chapter 21) and QLoRA (Chapter 22) solve by training only a small number of extra parameters.

Activations

The fourth cost depends on the data. Autograd keeps the intermediate values of the forward pass for the backward pass (P2), and there are a lot of them: every layer's inputs, attention scores, MLP activations, for every token of every sequence. Measured on GPT-2 with 1,024-token sequences:

Saved activations grew by about 0.95 MiB per token, and the peak by about 1.43 MiB per token. The difference is temporary tensors in the loss: the output layer produces 50,257 logits per token, and the cross-entropy works on an fp32 copy of them (P3). For a batch of 8 × 1,024 tokens one fp32 copy of the logits is 1.5 GiB, and the backward pass needs another of the same size. A line fitted through the four autocast runs, 1,734 MiB plus 1.43 MiB per token, predicted the batch-2 and batch-4 peaks to within 0.5%. Qwen2.5-0.5B, with a 151,936-token vocabulary, cost about 4 MiB per token: 512-token sequences peaked at 9.4 GiB with a batch of 1 and 15.4 GiB with a batch of 4.

Activations are also the part you can trade. Halving the batch halves them, and gradient accumulation (Chapter 13) gets the same effective batch over several smaller steps. Activation checkpointing throws most of them away and recomputes them during backward, trading about a third more compute for much less memory.

The KV cache

Inference has no gradients or optimiser state, but generation adds a memory cost of its own. To avoid recomputing earlier tokens, a model stores each layer's keys and values for every token it has seen (Chapter 16). Per token, that is 2 (keys and values) × layers × KV heads × head size × bytes:

def kv_bytes_per_token(layers, kv_heads, head_dim, bytes_per=2):
    return 2 * layers * kv_heads * head_dim * bytes_per

kv_bytes_per_token(12, 12, 64)     # GPT-2: 36,864 bytes; measured 37.6 MiB after 1,024 tokens (36 MiB predicted)
kv_bytes_per_token(24, 2, 64)      # Qwen2.5-0.5B: 12,288 bytes
kv_bytes_per_token(28, 2, 128)     # Qwen2.5-1.5B: 28,672 bytes
kv_bytes_per_token(28, 4, 128)     # Qwen2.5-7B: 57,344 bytes; 32,768 tokens of context is 1.75 GiB per sequence

GPT-2 needs more cache per token than Qwen2.5-1.5B, a model twelve times its size, because GPT-2 keeps keys and values for all 12 of its attention heads while Qwen shares them between groups of heads (grouped-query attention, Chapter 15). For a server holding many conversations at once, the KV cache, not the weights, is usually what runs out first (Chapter 26).

Reading the numbers

Three numbers describe GPU memory, and they disagree on purpose. torch.cuda.memory_allocated() is the bytes held by live tensors. torch.cuda.memory_reserved() is what PyTorch's caching allocator has taken from the driver, including freed blocks it keeps for reuse. nvidia-smi shows what the driver has handed out, including the CUDA context itself. After loading GPT-2 in bf16 they read 243.5 MiB allocated, 256 MiB reserved, and 505 MiB more in nvidia-smi than before the process started, of which 251 MiB was the CUDA context, measured before any tensor existed. Use torch.cuda.max_memory_allocated() to find a step's peak; nvidia-smi only shows a moment, and on Windows it cannot attribute memory to a single process.

Out-of-memory errors

When the sum does not fit, PyTorch raises torch.OutOfMemoryError. Training Qwen2.5-0.5B with 16 sequences of 1,024 tokens gave: CUDA out of memory. Tried to allocate 896.00 MiB. GPU 0 has a total capacity of 15.89 GiB of which 0 bytes is free. The "tried to allocate" figure is only the request that failed, not the whole shortfall, so do not plan from it. Work out the budget instead, then fix the part that is too big: activations with a smaller batch, gradient accumulation or checkpointing; optimiser state with an 8-bit optimiser or LoRA; weights with bf16 or quantisation.

Use case: ZeRO and the 16 bytes per parameter

Microsoft's 2019 ZeRO paper (the basis of DeepSpeed) started from exactly this budget: with mixed-precision Adam, it counted 16 bytes per parameter of weights, gradients and optimiser state, 120 GB for a 7.5-billion-parameter model, far more than one GPU held. Its answer was to stop every GPU keeping a full copy: ZeRO splits the optimiser state, then the gradients, then the weights themselves across the GPUs in a job, so each holds only its share. PyTorch's Fully Sharded Data Parallel (FSDP) is the same idea. On one 16 GB GPU there is nothing to split across, which is why this course reaches for LoRA and quantisation instead.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Budgeting only the weights for training; AdamW alone adds 8 bytes per parameter and gradients 4 more.
  • Forgetting the logits: with a 50,000 to 150,000-token vocabulary, the loss's fp32 logits can be gigabytes per batch.
  • Planning from the "tried to allocate" number in an out-of-memory error, which is only the last request.
  • Reading nvidia-smi once and calling it the peak; use torch.cuda.max_memory_allocated().
  • Ignoring the KV cache when serving long contexts or many users at once.
  • Keeping gradients alive between steps instead of zero_grad(set_to_none=True).

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Can you fully fine-tune a 7-billion-parameter model on one 24 GB GPU? Show the arithmetic.

How a strong answer sounds
No, not with a standard setup. Mixed-precision training with AdamW costs about 16 bytes per parameter before activations: 4 for fp32 master weights, 4 for gradients and 8 for Adam's two moments. For 7 billion parameters that is about 112 GB, nearly five times the card, and activations come on top. Even the bf16 weights alone are 14 GB. Options: train only small adapters with LoRA, so gradients and optimiser state exist only for a few million parameters; quantise the frozen base weights to 4 bits with QLoRA, which brings the weights to about 4 GB; use an 8-bit optimiser; or shard everything across several GPUs with ZeRO or FSDP. Activation memory is then controlled with a small micro-batch, gradient accumulation and activation checkpointing.

Deeper: Your training run fits at batch size 4 but runs out of memory at 8. The weights are under 1 GB. Where is the memory going, and what do you do?

How a strong answer sounds
If doubling the batch breaks it, the problem is the part that scales with batch × sequence length, not the weights or optimiser state. That is the saved activations, about 1 MiB per token for a GPT-2-sized model, plus the large temporary tensors around the loss: the output layer produces one logit per vocabulary entry per token, and the cross-entropy works on an fp32 copy of those, 1.5 GiB for 8 × 1,024 tokens with a 50,000-token vocabulary, and the backward pass needs another. I would confirm with torch.cuda.max_memory_allocated() and the memory snapshot tool, then keep the effective batch of 8 by running batch 4 twice with gradient accumulation, add activation checkpointing if I need longer sequences, and check that the loss is computed in a way that does not materialise extra copies of the logits, for example by computing it in chunks.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Weights cost parameters × bytes: measured loads matched to within 1% (GPT-2 475 MiB in fp32, Qwen2.5-0.5B 950 MiB in bf16).
  • Mixed-precision AdamW training costs about 16 bytes per parameter before activations: 23 GiB for a 1.5B model.
  • Activations scale with batch × tokens: about 1 MiB per token for GPT-2, plus the loss's fp32 logits.
  • The KV cache costs 2 × layers × KV heads × head size × bytes per token; GPT-2 needs more than Qwen2.5-1.5B because it has no grouped-query attention.
  • allocated ≤ reserved ≤ what nvidia-smi shows; the CUDA context alone was 251 MiB. Measure peaks with max_memory_allocated.
  • On out-of-memory errors, fix the part that is too big: batch and checkpointing for activations, LoRA or 8-bit optimisers for state, quantisation for weights.

Check your understanding

About how much memory do a 7.6-billion-parameter model's weights need in bf16?

2 bytes × 7.6 billion ≈ 15.2 GB = 14.2 GiB, almost all of a 16 GB card.

With fp32 master weights and AdamW, how many bytes per parameter does training need before activations?

4 (weights) + 4 (gradients) + 8 (Adam's two moments). Measured after a step with gradients freed, 12 bytes per parameter remained.

GPT-2's training peak grew from 3.1 GiB at batch 1 to 13.1 GiB at batch 8. What grew?

About 1.43 MiB per token at the peak: 0.95 MiB of saved activations plus fp32 logits over a 50,257-token vocabulary.

Why does GPT-2 need more KV cache per token (36 KiB) than the much larger Qwen2.5-1.5B (28 KiB)?

GPT-2: 2 × 12 layers × 12 heads × 64 × 2 bytes. Qwen2.5-1.5B: 2 × 28 layers × 2 KV heads × 128 × 2 bytes.

An out-of-memory error says "Tried to allocate 896.00 MiB". What does that number tell you?

Work out the budget from its parts instead; the failing request is just where the sum crossed the limit.
