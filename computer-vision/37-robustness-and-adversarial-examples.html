Robustness & Adversarial Examples

In 2013 researchers found that a tiny, carefully chosen change to an image, invisible to a person, could make a state-of-the-art classifier say anything. These adversarial examples are not rare accidents: they can be computed for almost any input in milliseconds, and the same weakness shows up more mildly as fragility to ordinary noise, blur and lighting. Any vision system facing someone with a motive, or simply a dirty lens, has to account for it.

This chapter writes the two standard attacks, FGSM and PGD, and turns them on a ResNet-18 trained on CIFAR-10 here, then trains a second ResNet-18 on adversarial examples and measures what that buys and what it costs. A change of 2 grey levels per pixel, out of 255, cut the standard model from 92.8% to 10% accuracy, and 4 grey levels to 0.15%. Adversarial training held 45.8% at 8 grey levels, at the cost of 4.7 times the training time and 18 points of clean accuracy, and it made the model far more robust to noise and far less robust to low contrast.

  • Generate adversarial examples with FGSM and PGD
  • Measure robust accuracy against the perturbation budget
  • Train adversarially and account for its cost
  • Test common corruptions as well as attacks
  • Know what physical-world attacks and certified defences mean in practice

Attacks: FGSM and PGD

Training adjusts the weights to lower the loss on an image. An attack does the opposite with the image: it adjusts the pixels to raise the loss, keeping the weights fixed, under a budget ε on how far any pixel may move (the L∞ norm). The fast gradient sign method (FGSM, 2014) takes one step: compute the gradient of the loss with respect to the pixels and move every pixel by ε in the direction of its sign. Projected gradient descent (PGD, 2017) takes many small steps from a random start, clipping back into the ε-box after each, and is the standard strong attack.

def pgd(model, x, y, eps=8 / 255, steps=10, alpha=2 / 255):
    d = torch.empty_like(x).uniform_(-eps, eps)                        # random start inside the budget
    for _ in range(steps):
        d.requires_grad_(True)
        loss = F.cross_entropy(model((x + d).clamp(0, 1)), y)
        g, = torch.autograd.grad(loss, d)
        d = (d.detach() + alpha * g.sign()).clamp(-eps, eps)          # step uphill, project back into the box
    return (x + d).clamp(0, 1)
fgsm = lambda m, x, y, eps: pgd(m, x, y, eps, steps=1, alpha=eps)       # (FGSM starts at zero, not at random)

Why does it work so well? A network is close to linear in its input over small regions, and an image has thousands of pixels. Moving each by a tiny ε in the direction that increases the loss adds up across all of them: the change in a linear score is ε times the sum of the absolute gradient, which grows with the number of pixels. High-dimensional inputs make small per-pixel budgets powerful.

Half a grey level, a change no screen can display, cost the standard model 19 points. At 4 grey levels PGD left it right on 3 images out of 2,000. Adversarial training

The most reliable empirical defence is to train on attacks: at every step, generate PGD examples against the current model and train on those instead of the clean images. It solves a min–max problem, minimise over the weights the loss maximised over perturbations, and it is expensive, because every training step now includes several forward and backward passes for the attack.

Adversarial training bought robustness at a steep price on every other axis: 4.7 times the training time (each step adds five attack passes), and 18 points of clean accuracy at this short schedule. Robust accuracy at the trained budget went from 0 to 46%, close to published CIFAR-10 numbers for ResNet-18, around 50% with longer training; no known method makes the gap disappear. The trade-off between clean and robust accuracy is real and has a theoretical basis: robust models must ignore small but genuinely predictive pixel patterns that standard models use.

Transfer attacks show what each model relies on. PGD examples crafted on the standard model and fed to the robust one left it at 72.3%, barely below its clean 74.6%: the perturbations that fool a standard network are aimed at features the robust one ignores. In the other direction, examples crafted on the robust model cut the standard one from 92% to 70.5%, because they change features both models use. An attacker without access to your model can craft attacks on a substitute and often succeed this way, which is why secrecy is not a defence.

Common corruptions

Worst-case attacks are one end of robustness; everyday degradation is the other. Benchmarks such as ImageNet-C and CIFAR-10-C apply 15 corruptions (noise, blur, weather, digital artefacts) at five severities. Three are tested here on all 10,000 test images.

Robustness is not one property. Adversarial training made the model dramatically more robust to random noise (60% against 16% at σ = 0.16) and somewhat more to blur, because it learned to ignore small high-frequency pixel changes, which is what noise mostly is. It made the model much less robust to reduced contrast (33% against 84% at 40% contrast): squashing every pixel towards the mean is a large, structured, low-frequency change, and the robust model, having learned to rely on coarse, high-contrast shape and colour, lost its signal. The standard model, which leans on fine texture, was the reverse. A defence for one threat can be a weakness for another, so test the corruptions you expect in deployment.

Physical attacks and certified defences

Attacks survive the physical world: printed stickers that make a stop sign read as a speed limit, patterned glasses that defeat face recognition, adversarial patches that hide a person from a detector. They are designed to work across viewing angles, distances and lighting (“expectation over transformation”), so they are larger and visible, but still innocuous-looking. On the defence side, many published defences were later broken by adaptive attacks that targeted them specifically, often because they merely masked the gradient. Certified defences, such as randomised smoothing, give a mathematical guarantee that no perturbation within a radius changes the prediction, at a significant cost in clean accuracy and only for small radii. For most applications the practical stance is: assume a determined attacker with model access will succeed; use adversarial training where it matters, limit what an attacker can learn about the model, and do not let a single classifier make an irreversible decision alone.

🧾
Use case: content filters and self-driving perception

Upload filters that block prohibited images face adversaries who iterate: blur a corner, add a frame, overlay noise, re-upload. Production filters combine adversarially trained models, perceptual hashes that match known images regardless of small changes, and ensembles, and they measure robustness to the transformations attackers actually use, not only L∞ budgets. A self-driving stack treats the camera classifier as one sensor among several: a stop sign misread because of stickers should be contradicted by the map and by lidar geometry, which is why safety cases rest on redundancy rather than on any single network being robust.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Evaluating a defence only against FGSM or a weak attack: use PGD with restarts, and adaptive attacks against the defence itself.
  • Gradient masking: a defence that breaks gradients (non-differentiable preprocessing, randomness) can look robust to gradient attacks and fall to transfer or gradient-free ones.
  • Attacking in normalised space with ε meant for pixels: budgets are defined on [0, 1] or [0, 255] pixels; normalise inside the model.
  • Leaving the model in train mode during the attack, so BatchNorm statistics change (Chapter 6).
  • Assuming adversarial robustness gives corruption robustness or the reverse: adversarial training raised noise accuracy from 16% to 60% but cut low-contrast accuracy from 84% to 33%.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is an adversarial example, and how is one made?

How a strong answer sounds
It is an input changed by a small, usually invisible amount that makes the model predict something wrong. To make one you compute the gradient of the loss with respect to the input pixels rather than the weights, and move the pixels in the direction that increases the loss, within a budget such as every pixel changing by at most 8 grey levels. FGSM takes one step of size ε along the sign of the gradient; PGD takes several smaller steps from a random start, projecting back into the budget each time, and is much stronger. It works because networks are nearly linear locally and images have thousands of dimensions, so tiny per-pixel changes add up. On a CIFAR-10 ResNet-18 I measured PGD taking accuracy from 92.8% to 10% at 2 grey levels and to 0.15% at 4.

Deeper: What does adversarial training cost, and is it worth it?

How a strong answer sounds
Every training batch is replaced by PGD examples against the current model, so each step costs roughly (attack steps + 1) forward-backward passes, several times normal training. It lowers clean accuracy and makes the model rely on more shape-like, perceptually aligned features. In exchange, robust accuracy at the trained budget goes from near zero to a meaningful fraction, and attacks transferred from other models largely fail. It is worth it where an adversary exists and errors are costly, combined with system-level defences; for a photo app it usually is not. In my run it cost 4.7 times the training time and 18 points of clean accuracy (92.4 to 74.6%), for 46% robust accuracy at ε = 8 instead of 0, and it changed corruption robustness in both directions: much better against noise, much worse against low contrast.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • An attack ascends the loss with respect to the pixels within a budget ε; FGSM takes one step, PGD many.
  • Measured on a standard ResNet-18: PGD at 2 grey levels → 10% accuracy, at 4 → 0.15%; FGSM is much weaker and can overshoot.
  • PGD adversarial training: 46% robust accuracy at ε = 8 instead of 0, for 4.7× training time and −18 points clean.
  • Robustness is not one property: the robust model was far better against noise and far worse against low contrast.
  • Evaluate defences with strong, adaptive attacks; certified defences give guarantees only for small radii.
