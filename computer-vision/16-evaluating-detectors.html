Evaluating Detectors: mAP from Scratch

“51.3 mAP” is the number every detection paper leads with, and few people could compute it by hand. It averages precision over 101 recall levels, 10 IoU thresholds and 80 classes, with special rules for crowds, small objects and the 100 most confident detections per image. Each rule changes the number, and each one hides something a product might care about.

This chapter implements COCO’s mean average precision from scratch in about 60 lines, runs it on Faster R-CNN’s detections for 500 COCO images, and matches pycocotools, the reference implementation, to every printed digit at all ten IoU thresholds. Then it takes the number apart: per class, from 100 down to 0; by IoU threshold, from 70.8 at 0.5 to 7.0 at 0.95; and by score threshold, where the same model drops 10 points.

  • Match detections to ground truth greedily by score at an IoU threshold
  • Build a precision–recall curve and compute interpolated average precision
  • Compute COCO AP@[.5:.95], AP50, AP75 and AP by object size
  • Handle crowd annotations and the per-image detection limit correctly
  • Explain what mAP hides, and what to report alongside it

Step 1: match detections to ground truth

For one class in one image, sort the detections by score. Go down the list; each detection is a true positive if its IoU with some not-yet-matched ground-truth box is at least the threshold (it takes the one it overlaps most), otherwise a false positive. Each ground-truth box can be matched once, so a duplicate detection of an already-matched object is a false positive. Ground-truth boxes never matched are false negatives. Two COCO rules complicate this. Boxes marked iscrowd (a crowd of people annotated as one region) are “ignore” regions: a detection matching one is neither right nor wrong, and crowd regions can absorb many detections. And when computing AP for one size range (small: area below 32², medium, large: above 96²), objects outside it are ignored rather than counted as misses.

Step 2: precision–recall and average precision

Pool all detections of the class across all images, sort them by score, and walk down the list keeping running counts: after each detection, precision = TP / (TP + FP) and recall = TP / (number of ground-truth objects). This traces the precision–recall curve, from high precision and low recall (only the most confident detections) to low precision and high recall. Precision is then made monotonic: at each recall it is replaced by the best precision achieved at that recall or higher. Average precision is the mean of that interpolated precision at 101 recall levels, 0, 0.01, …, 1.0, and is 0 at recall levels never reached.

def ap_one_class(scores, is_tp, is_ignored, n_gt):           # per detection, pooled over images
    order = np.argsort(-scores, kind="mergesort")            # stable sort, as pycocotools
    tp = np.cumsum(is_tp[order] & ~is_ignored[order])
    fp = np.cumsum(~is_tp[order] & ~is_ignored[order])
    recall, precision = tp / n_gt, tp / np.maximum(tp + fp, np.spacing(1))
    precision = np.maximum.accumulate(precision[::-1])[::-1]    # best precision at this recall or beyond
    idx = np.searchsorted(recall, np.linspace(0, 1, 101), side="left")
    return np.mean([precision[i] if i < len(precision) else 0 for i in idx])

# COCO AP = mean over 10 IoU thresholds (0.50:0.05:0.95) and over classes with any ground truth,
# with at most 100 detections per image (by score), crowd regions ignored, and per-size variants.

Matching pycocotools exactly

On Faster R-CNN v2’s 15,950 detections for the first 500 COCO validation images, the from-scratch implementation and pycocotools agreed at every IoU threshold to the two decimals pycocotools prints:

Getting there required every detail: the stable sort, matching each detection to the highest-IoU unmatched box, letting crowd regions be matched repeatedly with IoU computed over the detection’s area, sorting ignored ground truths last so real ones are preferred, the 100-detection cap per image and class, and interpolating at 101 points. Small-object AP (32.99) and large-object AP (63.19) matched too. Implementations that skip any of these give numbers that are “mAP” but not comparable with anyone else’s.

What mAP hides

Taking the 51.28 apart shows how much one number averages away:

  • Localisation. AP fell from 70.8 at IoU 0.5 to 57.2 at 0.75 and 7.0 at 0.95. COCO’s average rewards precise boxes heavily; a task that only needs “roughly where” (counting, alerting) should look at AP50.
  • Classes. Per-class AP ranged from 100 (toothbrush, a single instance) and 91.3 (stop sign) to 19.2 (book) and 0 (scissors). Common, distinctive objects score high; small, cluttered and rare ones low. The mean hides which is which.
  • Size. Small objects scored 33.0 against 63.2 for large ones.
  • The operating point. AP integrates over every score threshold, but a product runs at one. Keeping only detections above 0.5 cut AP from 51.3 to 49.0 (fewer detections means less recall to integrate over) while leaving 9.5 boxes per image instead of 31.9; precision and recall at that threshold are what users see.
  • The detection cap. Allowing only 1 detection per image and class gave 35.5 AP, 10 gave 50.6, 100 gave 51.3: crowded scenes need the high cap, and a system that keeps only the top few detections is not measured by the standard number.

Use case: VOC’s 11-point AP and COCO’s 101-point AP

The PASCAL VOC benchmark originally computed AP at 11 recall levels (0, 0.1, …, 1) and at a single IoU of 0.5; it later switched to using every recall point. COCO introduced averaging over IoU thresholds 0.5 to 0.95 and 101 recall levels, which rewards tight boxes and gives smoother numbers. The same detector can therefore have three or four different “mAP” values depending on the definition, and papers have been caught comparing a VOC-style number with a COCO-style one. Whenever you see an mAP, find out which IoU thresholds, which interpolation and which dataset split it used.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Writing your own mAP without matching pycocotools on a known case; small rule differences change the number.
  • Counting detections that match crowd regions as false positives (or ignoring crowd annotations entirely).
  • Applying a high score threshold before computing AP: it truncates the curve (51.3 → 49.0 at 0.5).
  • Comparing AP50 with AP@[.5:.95], or VOC 11-point AP with COCO AP.
  • Reporting the mean only, when the classes or sizes that matter score far lower.
  • Evaluating on a subset and comparing with full-set numbers (500 images gave 51.3 against a published 46.7).

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How is COCO mAP computed?

How a strong answer sounds
For each class and each IoU threshold from 0.50 to 0.95 in steps of 0.05: in every image, keep the top 100 detections, sort by score, and greedily match each to the highest-IoU unmatched ground truth above the threshold (true positive) or mark it a false positive; crowd regions are ignored. Pool across images, sort by score, and compute running precision and recall. Make precision non-increasing by taking, at each recall, the best precision at that recall or beyond, and average it at 101 recall levels: that is AP for the class and threshold. Average over thresholds and over classes. AP50 and AP75 fix one threshold; APs, APm, APl restrict to object sizes. Implemented from scratch on 500 COCO images, this matched pycocotools to two decimals at every threshold.

Deeper: Your detector’s mAP improved by 2 points but users see no difference. How can both be true?

How a strong answer sounds
mAP averages over things users do not experience. The gain might be at strict IoU thresholds (tighter boxes at 0.85–0.95), which matter for COCO’s average but not for an application that only needs approximate locations; or at low score thresholds that the product never uses; or in classes, sizes or crowded images that are rare in production. Here AP ranged from 70.8 at IoU 0.5 to 7.0 at 0.95, and keeping only detections above 0.5 already changed it. I would compare the two models at the deployed operating point (precision and recall at the chosen threshold, IoU 0.5 or whatever the product needs), per class for the classes that matter, on a sample of real production images, and check whether the differences are larger than run-to-run noise.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Match detections greedily by score to unmatched ground truth above an IoU threshold; duplicates are false positives, crowds are ignored.
  • AP = mean interpolated precision at 101 recall levels; COCO AP also averages IoU 0.50:0.05:0.95 and classes.
  • A from-scratch implementation matched pycocotools exactly (51.28) once every rule was copied.
  • The same model: 70.8 at IoU 0.5, 7.0 at 0.95; small 33.0 vs large 63.2; per-class from 100 to 0.
  • Report AP50/AP75, AP by size, per-class AP and precision/recall at the deployed threshold alongside mAP.

Each threshold gets its own 101-point AP per class; the reported AP is the mean of all of them.

What does interpolating precision in the PR curve do?

It makes the curve non-increasing, so AP does not depend on small wiggles in the ranking.

Keeping only detections scoring above 0.5 changed Faster R-CNN’s AP how?

AP integrates over all thresholds; truncating the list removes recall it could have earned.

How are detections that overlap a crowd (iscrowd) region treated by COCO evaluation?

A crowd region marks many unannotated people; detections inside it are ignored, and one crowd region can absorb several.
