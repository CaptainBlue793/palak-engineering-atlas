Masked Autoencoders & DINO

Contrastive learning (Chapter 22) needs negatives and leans heavily on hand-designed augmentations. Two later ideas dropped one or both. Masked autoencoders (MAE) hide most of an image’s patches and train a ViT to paint them back, the vision version of BERT’s fill-in-the-blank. DINO trains a student network to match a slowly moving teacher on different crops of the same image, with no negatives at all.

This chapter runs both with Meta’s released ViT-B/16 weights. MAE fills in a photo with 75% and 90% of it hidden. Then the frozen features of a supervised ViT, DINO and MAE are tested on pet breeds and satellite land use, where the methods rank very differently; and DINO’s attention heads turn out to segment objects without ever having seen a mask.

Masked image modelling

In language, BERT hides 15% of the words and predicts them; to do that it must understand grammar and meaning. The same task on images is harder to set up because neighbouring pixels are so redundant: hide 15% of the patches and the network can fill most of them by blurring their neighbours, learning nothing about objects. MAE (2021) answers with a very high masking ratio. It cuts the image into 196 patches of 16 × 16, hides a random 75% of them, and asks the model to reconstruct the hidden pixels from the 49 that remain. With three quarters of the dog missing, interpolation no longer works; the model has to know what dogs look like.

The architecture is deliberately lopsided. The encoder, a full ViT-B, sees only the visible 25% of patches, no mask tokens at all, so pre-training costs about a quarter of a normal ViT pass. A small decoder (8 thin blocks) receives the encoded visible patches plus a learned mask token at each hidden position, and predicts the pixels of every hidden patch. The loss is mean squared error on hidden patches only, with each target patch normalised by its own mean and variance, which makes the model care about structure rather than brightness. After pre-training the decoder is thrown away.

Running the released model on four photos at four masking ratios, the average reconstruction loss on hidden patches (in normalised-pixel units) was:

The reconstructions are blurry, which is expected and harmless: with MSE the best guess for an uncertain patch is the average of the possibilities. MAE is not an image generator; the reconstruction is only the exercise that forces the encoder to learn.

DINO: self-distillation with no labels and no negatives

DINO (self-DIstillation with NO labels, 2021) has two copies of the same network. The student sees several crops of an image, two large “global” crops and several small “local” ones; the teacher sees only the global crops. Both output a probability distribution over 65,536 made-up “prototype” classes, and the student is trained with cross-entropy to match the teacher’s distribution for a different crop. Matching a small local crop to the teacher’s view of the whole image teaches “local to global” correspondence: a patch of fur should mean the same as the whole cat. The teacher is not trained by gradients; its weights are a moving average of the student’s (momentum 0.996 rising to 1), as in MoCo.

Without negatives, there is an obvious cheat: both networks output the same distribution for every image and the loss is zero. This is collapse. DINO prevents it with two opposing operations on the teacher’s output. Centring subtracts a running mean of the teacher’s outputs, which stops one prototype dominating for all images, but on its own pushes towards a uniform output. Sharpening, a low softmax temperature for the teacher (0.04 against the student’s 0.1), pushes towards a confident output. Balanced, the only way to keep the loss low is to give different images different, confident outputs.

# one DINO step (simplified): s = student, t = teacher (EMA of s), C = running centre
g1, g2, locals_ = multicrop(x)                            # 2 global crops (224) + 6 local crops (96)
with torch.no_grad():
    pt = [((t(g) - C) / 0.04).softmax(-1) for g in (g1, g2)]  # centred and sharpened targets
ps = [(s(v) / 0.1).log_softmax(-1) for v in (g1, g2, *locals_)]
loss = mean(-(pt[i] * ps[j]).sum(-1).mean() for i in (0, 1) for j in range(8) if j != i)
loss.backward(); opt.step()
t.params = 0.996 * t.params + 0.004 * s.params                 # momentum teacher
C = 0.9 * C + 0.1 * t(cat(g1, g2)).mean(0)                         # update the centre

Frozen features, three ways of pre-training

Same architecture (ViT-B/16), three ways to pre-train it on ImageNet: with its labels (supervised), with DINO, and with MAE, neither of which saw a label. Their frozen features on two tasks unlike ImageNet in different ways: 37 cat and dog breeds (Oxford Pets, close to ImageNet’s content but fine-grained) and 10 land-use classes from satellite images (EuroSAT, a different world).

DINO, which never saw a label, matched or beat supervised pre-training on three of the four columns and was clearly best on satellite images (94.8% kNN against 89.4%). The supervised model kept a small lead on Pets kNN, which is not surprising: ImageNet’s labels include over a hundred dog breeds and several cats, so its features were trained on almost this exact task. MAE is the outlier, and in an instructive way. Its frozen features were competitive on EuroSAT, where colour and texture largely decide land use, but on fine-grained pet breeds its linear probe was 12–15 points lower and its kNN collapsed to 29–38%. MAE features hold a great deal of information, but not arranged so that images of the same breed sit close together; reconstruction does not ask for that, while DINO’s and supervised training do. The MAE paper reports the same pattern on ImageNet (68% linear probe, 83.6% after fine-tuning): MAE is pre-training for fine-tuning, not for frozen use.

DINO’s attention segments objects

The DINO paper’s most striking figure showed that the class token’s attention in the last block of a DINO ViT outlines the main object, although the model never saw a mask or a label. The panel’s second view shows six of the twelve heads for four photos. The pattern held, roughly. Averaged over heads, attention sat on the truck’s body and the church building rather than the sky and road, and on the dog rather than the floor. Individual heads specialise: one of the dog heads put 60% of its attention on 5 patches at the top of the head, and one truck head picked out the wheels and lower body. The masks are coarse, because each cell is a 16-pixel patch (the paper’s cleanest maps come from ViT-S/8, with 8-pixel patches), and each map has some stray background patches. Compare Chapter 19, where the supervised ViT-B’s last-block class-token attention went mostly to a few blank background patches: self-distillation across crops produced attention that follows objects, which is why DINO features are used for unsupervised segmentation and object discovery.

Use case: one frozen backbone for many products

A company with a dozen vision features (product search, duplicate detection, content moderation, auto-tagging) can run one strong self-supervised backbone and train only small heads on its frozen features, rather than fine-tuning and serving a dozen networks. DINO-family features (DINOv2 is the current workhorse) are popular for exactly this because they work out of the box with kNN and linear heads, and their patch features support dense tasks such as segmentation and depth. MAE-style pre-training is the better choice when the plan is to fine-tune the whole model on a large labelled set, or for domains like medical scans where masked reconstruction on in-domain unlabelled data is cheap and effective. The frozen-feature measurement in this chapter is the habit to copy: test frozen features on your data with a kNN and a linear probe before choosing.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Judging MAE features with a linear probe only: its frozen kNN on pet breeds was 29–38% while DINO’s was 90%; MAE is meant to be fine-tuned.
  • Masking too little: at 25% the task is solved by interpolation and teaches little; MAE uses 75%.
  • Treating MAE reconstructions as a generative model: they are blurry averages by design.
  • Removing DINO’s centring or sharpening: either one alone collapses training.
  • Assuming the best ImageNet method transfers best everywhere: rankings changed between Pets and EuroSAT here.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How does a masked autoencoder learn, and why does it mask so much?

How a strong answer sounds
MAE cuts an image into patches, hides a random 75% of them, encodes only the visible quarter with a ViT, and uses a small decoder with mask tokens to reconstruct the hidden pixels, with the loss on hidden patches only. Images are very redundant, so with a low ratio the model can fill gaps by interpolating neighbours and learns little; at 75% it has to model objects and scenes. Because the encoder never processes masked patches, pre-training is also about four times cheaper per image. Afterwards the decoder is discarded and the encoder is fine-tuned or probed. Running the released model, the loss on hidden patches rose from 0.11 at 25% masking to 0.24 at 75% and 0.44 at 90%, and the reconstructions are blurry by design.

Deeper: DINO has no negatives. Why does it not collapse to a constant output?

How a strong answer sounds
A constant output would make student and teacher agree trivially. DINO counters it with two operations on the teacher’s output: centring, subtracting a running mean so that no single dimension dominates for every image, and sharpening with a low temperature so the teacher’s distribution is confident rather than uniform. Centring alone collapses to uniform, sharpening alone to one-hot on the same dimension; together the only low-loss solution is image-dependent, confident outputs. The teacher is a momentum average of the student, which gives stable targets, and multi-crop training forces local crops to match the global view. In practice the frozen DINO ViT-B/16 I tested beat or matched supervised ImageNet features on pet breeds and satellite land use, and its last-block attention concentrates on the objects in a photo without ever seeing a mask.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • MAE hides 75% of patches, encodes only the visible ones and reconstructs the rest; the high ratio makes the task need understanding.
  • MAE’s loss on hidden patches rose from 0.11 at 25% masking to 0.44 at 90%; reconstructions are blurry by design.
  • DINO matches a momentum teacher across crops with no negatives; centring plus sharpening prevent collapse.
  • Frozen DINO features matched or beat supervised ones (EuroSAT kNN 94.8% vs 89.4%); frozen MAE features failed at kNN on pet breeds (29–38%) and need fine-tuning.
  • DINO’s last-block attention roughly outlines objects with no labels; individual heads pick out parts, at 16-pixel resolution.

Check your understanding

Why does MAE mask 75% of patches rather than 15% like BERT?

At 25% masking the loss was 0.11: the gaps are easy to fill from neighbours. A high ratio forces the encoder to model content.

What does MAE’s encoder process during pre-training?

Mask tokens are added only before the small decoder; the large encoder works on 25% of the tokens.

How are DINO’s teacher weights updated?

Momentum close to 1 gives a slowly changing, stable teacher, as in MoCo’s key encoder.

What prevents DINO from collapsing to the same output for every image?

Centring discourages one dominant dimension, sharpening discourages a uniform output; together they force image-dependent outputs.

What is the main lesson of the frozen-feature comparison?

The order changed between datasets and between linear and kNN evaluation.
