Semantic Segmentation: FCN & U-Net

Semantic segmentation labels every pixel: this one is road, that one is pedestrian, this one is sky. The output is an image-sized map of classes, so the network must do two things a classifier never does: keep spatial detail all the way to the output, and still see enough context to know what each region is.

This chapter trains four segmenters from scratch on 3,680 photos of cats and dogs with pixel labels (pet, background, border), changing one thing at a time: upsampling once at the end (an FCN), a U-Net decoder without skip connections, with them, and with a different upsampling layer. The skips were worth 3.6 points of mean IoU and almost all of it at the edges. It also measures three pretrained segmenters on COCO, and why pixel accuracy is the wrong number to report.

  • Turn a classification network into a fully convolutional per-pixel classifier
  • Upsample with transposed convolutions or interpolation, and know the trade-off
  • Explain why U-Net’s skip connections recover detail
  • Use dilated convolutions to keep resolution without losing context
  • Evaluate with mean IoU and boundary accuracy instead of pixel accuracy

Per-pixel classification

A classifier squeezes a 224 × 224 image down to a 7 × 7 map and then to one vector. A fully convolutional network (FCN, 2015) drops the final pooling and dense layers and keeps the 7 × 7 map, replacing the classifier with a 1 × 1 convolution that predicts class scores at every one of the 49 positions; then it upsamples the score map back to the input size. Cross-entropy is applied per pixel, averaged over all pixels. Every pixel is a training example, which is why segmentation can learn from a few thousand images.

class FCN(nn.Module):                                          # encoder: 4 conv blocks, each halving H and W
    def forward(self, x):                                        # x (N, 3, 128, 128)
        H = x.shape[-1]
        for e in self.enc: x = F.max_pool2d(e(x), 2)              # -> (N, 256, 8, 8)
        scores = self.out(self.mid(x))                              # (N, 3, 8, 8): one score per class per cell
        return F.interpolate(scores, size=(H, H), mode="bilinear")  # (N, 3, 128, 128), upsampled x16 in one go
loss = F.cross_entropy(model(x), y)                            # y (N, 128, 128): a class index per pixel

Upsampling one 8 × 8 map by sixteen times gives blobs: every 16 × 16 patch of output comes from the same few numbers, so edges are blurred and thin structures vanish. Two ideas fix that. Learned upsampling: a transposed convolution (stride 2) spreads each input value over a 2 × 2 (or larger) output area with learned weights, doubling resolution in a trainable way; alternatively, plain bilinear interpolation followed by a convolution (a 1 × 1 one in the experiment below), which avoids the checkerboard artefacts transposed convolutions can produce. And skip connections.

U-Net: an encoder, a decoder, and skips

U-Net (2015, for biomedical images) pairs the downsampling encoder with a mirror-image decoder that upsamples step by step, and at every resolution concatenates the encoder’s feature map from the same resolution onto the decoder’s. The encoder’s early maps know exactly where edges are but not what they belong to; the decoder knows what but has lost where. The skip connection gives each decoder stage both.

Four segmenters, measured

Oxford-IIIT Pet’s “trimaps” label each pixel as pet, background or border (a thin band around the outline). All images at 128 × 128; same encoder for every model; 30 epochs, AdamW, flips; 3,680 training and 3,669 test images. Mean IoU (mIoU) is the IoU between predicted and true pixels of each class, averaged over classes; boundary accuracy is pixel accuracy on the pet and background pixels within 3 pixels of the border band: the pixels that decide where the outline goes. Three lessons. A decoder that upsamples gradually beat one-shot upsampling by 3.8 points, and adding skips gained another 3.6, almost all of it on the thin border class (46.2 → 53.2 IoU) and the boundary (79.3% → 84.3%): exactly the detail that skips carry. The two upsampling layers were within half a point, so choose by artefacts and speed. And pixel accuracy is misleading: predicting “background” everywhere scored 57.7% pixel accuracy, because background is 58% of the pixels, while finding no pets at all; its mIoU, 19.2, says so. Small and thin classes barely move pixel accuracy, which is why segmentation results are reported as mIoU.

Keeping resolution: dilated convolutions

The alternative to an encoder–decoder is not to downsample so much in the first place. DeepLab replaces the strides of ResNet’s last stages with dilated convolutions (Chapter 2), keeping the feature map at 1/8 of the input instead of 1/32 while the receptive field still grows, and adds parallel branches at several dilation rates (atrous spatial pyramid pooling) to see context at several scales. On 300 COCO images with VOC classes, three pretrained torchvision models:

Thin and small objects stayed hard for all three (bottle 12–42, bird about 40). With only 300 images some per-class numbers are noisy: FCN beat DeepLabV3 on boat, 64.7 to 37.9, so the overall mIoU is the figure to trust. The mobile LR-ASPP model was five times faster and lost ten points, most of it on small objects (bottle 11.9, cow 13.5), the same pattern as mobile detectors in Chapter 15.

Use case: U-Net in medical imaging

U-Net was introduced for segmenting cells in microscopy images with only a few dozen annotated examples, and it remains the default architecture for medical segmentation: organs in CT and MRI, tumours, retinal vessels. Its strengths are exactly those measured here: skip connections preserve the fine boundaries clinicians care about, and per-pixel training extracts a lot of signal from few images. Clinical evaluations report Dice or IoU per structure and boundary distance metrics rather than pixel accuracy, because a tumour can occupy 1% of a scan and a model that predicts “healthy” everywhere would be 99% accurate (Chapter 9 and P7’s accuracy paradox, per pixel).

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Reporting pixel accuracy: “background everywhere” scored 57.7% and found nothing.
  • Resizing label masks with bilinear interpolation, which invents classes between labels; use nearest (P4).
  • Upsampling once from 1/16 or 1/32 and wondering why edges are blurry: use a decoder with skips or dilation.
  • Mismatched crops or flips between image and mask in augmentation.
  • Averaging IoU over pixels instead of classes (that is close to pixel accuracy); compute IoU per class, then average.
  • Using BatchNorm with batch 2 on high-resolution segmentation (Chapter 6).

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Why does U-Net have skip connections, and what do they buy?

How a strong answer sounds
The encoder downsamples to gain context, which throws away precise positions; the decoder upsamples back, but it can only reconstruct detail from the coarse map, so boundaries come out blurred. A skip concatenates the encoder’s feature map at each resolution onto the decoder’s at the same resolution, so each decoder stage has both the high-level meaning from below and the exact edges from the encoder. Measured on pet segmentation at 128 px, adding skips to the same encoder–decoder raised mIoU from 72.6 to 76.2, with most of the gain on the thin border class (46.2 to 53.2 IoU) and in boundary accuracy (79.3% to 84.3%). An FCN that upsampled the 8 × 8 scores straight to 128 × 128 scored 68.7.

Deeper: You segment tumours that cover 1% of each scan. Which loss and which metric?

How a strong answer sounds
With 99% background, plain per-pixel cross-entropy is dominated by easy background pixels, the same imbalance as dense detection (Chapter 15). Options are class-weighted cross-entropy, focal loss, or a Dice loss (1 − 2|P∩G| / (|P| + |G|)), which measures overlap of the small class directly and is insensitive to the amount of background; combining Dice with cross-entropy is a common, stable choice. For evaluation, never pixel accuracy: report Dice or IoU for the tumour class per scan and averaged, the detection rate of tumours (any overlap above a threshold), and a boundary metric such as the 95th-percentile Hausdorff distance, sliced by tumour size. I would also crop or sample patches around the region of interest during training so the model sees enough positives.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Segmentation is per-pixel classification: a fully convolutional network plus upsampling, with cross-entropy at every pixel.
  • One ×16 upsample gave 68.7 mIoU; a gradual decoder 72.6; U-Net’s skips 76.2, gained mostly at boundaries.
  • Transposed convolution and bilinear + conv upsampling were within half a point.
  • Dilated convolutions keep resolution: DeepLabV3 led the pretrained models at 65.3 mIoU on COCO/VOC classes.
  • Report mIoU, not pixel accuracy: “background everywhere” had 57.7% pixel accuracy and 19.2 mIoU.

Check your understanding

What makes a network “fully convolutional”?

The dense classifier is replaced by a 1 × 1 convolution, so every position of the final map gets its own class scores.

Where did U-Net’s skip connections help most in the pet experiment?

Border IoU 46.2 → 53.2 and boundary accuracy 79.3% → 84.3%: skips carry the precise positions the decoder had lost.

Predicting “background” for every pixel scored 57.7% pixel accuracy. What was its mean IoU?

IoU 57.7 for background and 0 for pet and border: averaged over three classes, 19.2.

How does DeepLab keep high-resolution features without an encoder–decoder?

Its output stays at 1/8 resolution instead of 1/32, and parallel dilation rates gather context at several scales.

How should a label mask be resized?

Labels are categories; interpolating between class 0 and class 2 invents a class 1. Nearest only copies existing labels.
