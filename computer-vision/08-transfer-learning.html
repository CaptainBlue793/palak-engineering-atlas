Transfer Learning & Fine-Tuning

Almost no one trains a vision model from random weights. A network trained on ImageNet’s 1.28 million photos has already learned edges, textures, parts and objects; a new task usually needs only to reuse those features and learn a little on top. That is transfer learning, and with small datasets it is the difference between a useless model and a good one.

This chapter takes a ResNet-50 and 3,680 photos of 37 cat and dog breeds. Trained from scratch, it reached 41.4%. Freezing the ImageNet weights and training only a linear layer on top took two seconds and reached 91.1%; fine-tuning everything reached 93.3%. With two photos per breed, fine-tuning still reached 71%. A satellite-image dataset, far from ImageNet’s photos, shows how far the features travel.

  • Explain why ImageNet features transfer, and to what
  • Train a linear probe on frozen features
  • Fine-tune part or all of a network, with suitable learning rates
  • Choose between probing, partial and full fine-tuning by data size and domain
  • Recognise when transfer helps less

Pretrained features

A classifier is a feature extractor followed by a linear layer. For ResNet-50 the extractor maps a 224 × 224 image to a 2,048-number vector (the global-average-pooled output of the last stage), and the ImageNet head maps that vector to 1,000 classes. Chapter 10 showed what the layers learn: edges first, then textures and parts, then whole objects. The early and middle layers are useful for nearly any photo; the last stage is more specific to ImageNet’s classes. Transfer learning keeps the extractor and replaces the head.

m = resnet50(weights=ResNet50_Weights.IMAGENET1K_V2)
m.fc = nn.Linear(2048, 37)                                  # new head for 37 breeds, randomly initialised

# 1. linear probe: freeze everything else, train only the head
for n, p in m.named_parameters(): p.requires_grad = n.startswith("fc")
# 2. last stage: also train layer4 (15 of 25.6 M parameters)
for n, p in m.named_parameters(): p.requires_grad = n.startswith(("layer4", "fc"))
# 3. full fine-tune: train everything, usually with a small learning rate
opt = torch.optim.SGD([{"params": backbone_params, "lr": 0.01},
                       {"params": m.fc.parameters(), "lr": 0.01}], momentum=0.9)

A linear probe can be even cheaper than the first option: run the frozen network once over every image, store the 2,048-number features, and train a logistic regression on them. Here that took 1.9 seconds for 200 epochs over the stored features (plus one pass of feature extraction), against minutes for any fine-tune.

Oxford-IIIT Pets: four ways to train

Oxford-IIIT Pets has 37 breeds (12 cats, 25 dogs), 3,680 training and 3,669 test photos, about 100 per breed: fine-grained, and small. All runs use ResNet-50 at 224 × 224 with random resized crops and flips, SGD with momentum, a one-cycle schedule and label smoothing 0.1, batch 64, bf16:

MethodTrainable parametersEpochsTest accuracy
From scratch (random init, LR 0.1)23.6 M3041.37%
Linear probe on frozen features75,813200 (on stored features)91.06%
Fine-tune layer4 + head (LR 0.01)15.0 M1092.97%
Full fine-tune (LR 0.01)23.6 M1093.32%
Full fine-tune, backbone at LR 0.001, head at 0.0123.6 M1092.26%
Full fine-tune, LR 0.123.6 M1091.96%

From scratch, 30 epochs on 3,680 images reached 41%: far too little data for 23.6 million parameters to learn edges, fur textures and breed differences from nothing. The frozen ImageNet features made the problem almost linear: a single linear layer on top reached 91%, because ImageNet already contains 120 dog breeds and several cats, so the features are close to what this task needs. Fine-tuning added the last two points. Two results are worth noticing because they run against common advice. A ten-times-lower learning rate for the backbone (“discriminative” rates) did slightly worse here than one rate for everything, and even a high rate of 0.1 did not wreck the features in ten epochs, it just did a little worse. With label smoothing, a warm-up and a short schedule, fine-tuning is robust; still, the safe default is a learning rate about ten times below what you would use from scratch, and checking a sweep, as Chapter 4 did.

How much data do you need?

The same comparison with only k training photos per breed (37k images in total), on the full test set:

Seventy-four labelled photos (two per breed) gave a 71%-accurate 37-way breed classifier. That is the practical meaning of transfer learning: tasks that would need tens of thousands of labels from scratch become feasible with tens. At very small sizes fine-tuning beat the probe here, by letting the crops and flips augment the few images through the whole network; from about 25 per class the two converged, and with the full set fine-tuning pulled ahead again. Which one wins at a given size depends on the task; both are cheap enough to try.

A distant domain: satellite images

EuroSAT is 27,000 Sentinel-2 satellite tiles of 64 × 64 pixels in ten land-use classes: forest, river, highway, residential, crops and so on. Nothing in ImageNet looks like a 10-metre-per-pixel overhead view. Using every second training image for the probe and the full 16,200 for fine-tuning:

EuroSAT, 5,400 test tilesTest accuracy
Linear probe on frozen ImageNet features94.91%
Full fine-tune, 5 epochs96.33%
From scratch, 5 epochs88.06%

Even on overhead images the frozen ImageNet features gave 94.9% with a linear layer, because edges, textures and colour statistics transfer across domains. Fine-tuning gained 1.4 points to 96.3%, and training from scratch reached 88.1% in the same five epochs (79 s each run). With 16,200 training images, a distant domain and ten coarse classes, scratch training gets much closer than on Pets, and a longer schedule would close more of the gap. The further the domain is from ImageNet and the more data you have, the less the pretrained start matters and the more fine-tuning (rather than probing) is needed.

Use case: medical imaging with a few thousand scans

Medical image datasets are often small because expert labels are expensive: a few thousand annotated scans is common. Studies of chest X-ray and retinal image classifiers have found that starting from ImageNet weights speeds training and helps most when labels are scarce, though the benefit shrinks as in-domain data grows, and that much of what transfers is low-level (edges, textures) rather than the high-level ImageNet categories. Teams therefore use ImageNet (or, increasingly, self-supervised or in-domain pretrained) backbones, fine-tune all layers at a low learning rate, and compare against a linear probe so they know how much the fine-tune is actually adding.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Training from scratch on a few thousand images (41% here) when pretrained weights are available.
  • Forgetting the pretrained model’s preprocessing (P1): ImageNet weights expect ImageNet normalisation.
  • Leaving BatchNorm statistics updating with tiny batches during fine-tuning (Chapter 6).
  • Skipping the linear probe, the two-second baseline that tells you what fine-tuning must beat.
  • Assuming one rule for learning rates (“always use discriminative rates”): measure on your task.
  • Tuning on the test set while choosing between methods; hold out validation images.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: You have 800 labelled images in 10 classes. How do you build a classifier?

How a strong answer sounds
Start from a backbone pretrained on ImageNet or a self-supervised model, never from scratch: on Oxford Pets a ResNet-50 from scratch reached 41% with 3,680 images, against 91% for a linear probe on frozen ImageNet features. First, extract features once and train a linear classifier; it takes seconds and is the baseline. Then fine-tune: the last stage first, then everything, with a learning rate about ten times below a from-scratch rate, augmentation (crops, flips), label smoothing, and early stopping on a held-out validation split, since 80 images per class is small. Here, fine-tuning added about two points over the probe with ~100 images per class. I would also check the preprocessing matches the pretrained weights and keep BatchNorm sensible for small batches.

Deeper: When does transfer from ImageNet help less, and what would you do then?

How a strong answer sounds
When the target domain is far from natural photos: satellite and aerial images, microscopy, medical scans, spectrograms, industrial X-rays. The low-level features (edges, textures) still transfer, so a pretrained start usually still beats random initialisation, but the high-level features are less useful, so a frozen probe falls further behind full fine-tuning and the gap to training from scratch shrinks as in-domain data grows. Options are: fine-tune all layers rather than probing; pretrain on the domain itself with self-supervised learning on unlabelled data (Chapters 22–23), which is often plentiful even when labels are not; or start from a foundation model trained on a broader mix (Chapter 24). Measure the probe, the full fine-tune and from-scratch on your own data before committing.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Pretrained features turn small datasets into easy problems: Pets went from 41.4% (scratch) to 91.1% (linear probe) to 93.3% (full fine-tune).
  • A linear probe on stored features takes seconds; always run it first as the baseline.
  • Two photos per breed gave 71% with fine-tuning; transfer is what makes tens of labels enough.
  • Fine-tuning with a modest LR was robust here; discriminative LRs did not help on this task. Measure, don’t assume.
  • Far-off domains (satellite tiles) still benefit, but the frozen features leave more on the table.

Check your understanding

What does a linear probe train?

The backbone is frozen; only a linear classifier on its 2,048-number features is trained. On Pets: 91.06% in 1.9 s.

Why did a ResNet-50 trained from scratch on 3,680 Pets photos reach only 41%?

Pretrained weights bring what 1.28 million ImageNet images taught; from scratch the model must learn edges and textures from 3,680 photos.

With two photos per breed (74 images), what did fine-tuning reach on the 37-breed test set?

71.11%; the linear probe reached 57.65%. Chance on 37 classes is 2.7%.

Why does ImageNet transfer so well to Oxford Pets in particular?

The images differ, but ImageNet’s 1,000 classes include 120 dog breeds and several cats, so fine-grained animal features are already learned.

In what order is it usually sensible to try transfer methods?

Each step costs more; the probe is a seconds-long baseline, and fine-tuning is worth it when it beats that baseline.
