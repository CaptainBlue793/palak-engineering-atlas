Instance Segmentation & Keypoints

Semantic segmentation (Chapter 17) paints every person the same colour. Counting people in a queue, picking one parcel off a pile or tracking one player needs instance segmentation: a separate mask for each object. The standard answer is to bolt a small mask predictor onto a two-stage detector (Chapter 14), and the same trick with a different head finds human joints.

This chapter runs torchvision’s Mask R-CNN on 500 COCO images and scores its boxes and its masks with the same AP machinery as Chapter 16, then asks what the mask adds over the box: scored as masks, the boxes alone reach 15.1 AP against the real masks’ 46.3. It runs Keypoint R-CNN on 300 images of people, and works through panoptic quality, the metric that joins semantic and instance segmentation.

Countable objects (people, cars, cups) are things; amorphous regions (sky, road, wall) are stuff. Instance segmentation handles things only, and its masks can overlap because each comes from a separate detection; panoptic segmentation covers both and assigns every pixel exactly once.

Mask R-CNN: a mask head on Faster R-CNN

Mask R-CNN (2017) adds one branch to Faster R-CNN. For each detected box, RoIAlign samples a 14 × 14 feature patch; four 3 × 3 convolutions and a stride-2 transposed convolution turn it into a 28 × 28 mask for each class; the mask of the predicted class is kept, resized to the box and thresholded at 0.5. Two design choices matter. The masks are trained with a per-pixel sigmoid for the box’s own class only, so classes do not compete inside the mask: the classifier decides what, the mask head only decides which pixels. And RoIAlign’s exact bilinear sampling replaced RoIPool’s rounding, because a mask is a pixel-level prediction and a half-cell misalignment shows up as a shifted outline (the paper reported RoIAlign improved mask AP by about 3 points at 50% overlap and much more at strict overlap).

from torchvision.models.detection import maskrcnn_resnet50_fpn_v2, MaskRCNN_ResNet50_FPN_V2_Weights
m = maskrcnn_resnet50_fpn_v2(weights=MaskRCNN_ResNet50_FPN_V2_Weights.COCO_V1).eval().cuda()
o = m([img])[0]                      # img (3, H, W) floats in [0, 1]
o["boxes"], o["labels"], o["scores"]  # (K, 4), (K,), (K,) as in Faster R-CNN
o["masks"]                            # (K, 1, H, W): soft masks already pasted into the image
binary = o["masks"][:, 0] > 0.5

Three readings. Mask AP is 5.6 points below box AP, because matching a mask is harder than matching a box: the gap is 2.6 points at IoU 0.5 but 6.1 at 0.75, and largest on small objects (36.6 → 28.4), where a 28 × 28 mask stretched over a 10-pixel object or squeezed onto a 400-pixel one is the weak link. Large objects lose only 1.6. Second, the mask head is doing real work: a filled-in box scored as a mask got 15.1 AP and only 7.6 at IoU 0.75, since most objects fill a fraction of their box. Third, these 500 images are easier than the full set: torchvision’s published figures for this model are 47.4 box AP and 41.8 mask AP on all 5,000. The model took 41.6 ms per image at batch 1 and has 46.4 M parameters.

Per class, the gap depends on shape. Compact objects that fill their box lose little or nothing: pizza scored 65.9 as boxes and 66.9 as masks, traffic lights 28.2 and 26.4. Thin, branching or hollow objects lose most: a giraffe’s box is easy (91.2 AP) but its neck and legs are not (68.0 as masks), and a bicycle, which is mostly air between spokes and frame, fell from 38.5 to 18.8. People went from 60.3 to 51.2, chairs from 38.8 to 29.1, ties from 41.5 to 29.2.

Keypoints: a heatmap per joint

Keypoint R-CNN swaps the mask head for a keypoint head: for each person box, RoIAlign takes a 14 × 14 patch, eight convolutions and two upsamplings produce a 56 × 56 map per keypoint (17 for COCO: nose, eyes, ears, shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips, knees, ankles), and each map is trained as a softmax over positions with the true location as the one correct class. The predicted joint is the peak of its map. Instead of IoU, keypoints are matched with object keypoint similarity:

OKS = mean over labelled keypoints of exp(−di2 / (2 s2 ki2))

where d is the distance between predicted and true joint, s the person’s scale (square root of their area) and k a per-joint tolerance measured from how much human annotators disagree: hips are hard to place and get a looser k than eyes. OKS is 1 for a perfect pose and plays the role of IoU in the usual AP computation. On 300 COCO images of people, Keypoint R-CNN scored 64.97 keypoint AP (87.5 at OKS 0.5, 71.3 at 0.75; 58.4 for medium-sized people and 74.9 for large ones; COCO does not score keypoints on small people), in line with torchvision’s published 65.0, at 29.5 ms per image. The panel draws its skeletons on one photo: hidden joints still get a position, and the keypoint score is how you tell them apart.

Panoptic quality

Panoptic segmentation is scored with PQ. Predicted and true segments of the same class match if their IoU exceeds 0.5; because panoptic segments cannot overlap, each true segment can match at most one prediction, so there is no need for confidence-ranked matching. Then

PQ = ΣTP IoU / (|TP| + ½|FP| + ½|FN|) = SQ (mean IoU of matches) × RQ (an F1 score of matches)

The split tells you what to fix: low SQ means outlines are poor, low RQ means objects are missed, merged or hallucinated. PQ is computed per class (things and stuff alike) and averaged. The panel’s last view lets you break a prediction in four ways and watch which factor drops.

Use case: robotic picking and sports analytics

A warehouse robot picking items from a cluttered tote needs one mask per item to choose a grasp point, and the masks must separate touching boxes of the same product: semantic segmentation would merge them into one blob. Instance segmentation models in the Mask R-CNN family, often fine-tuned on a few thousand labelled tote images, supply those masks. Broadcast sports graphics and coaching tools run keypoint models on every frame to get each player’s pose, then track the skeletons over time (Chapter 32); the metric that matters is keypoint accuracy on fast, partly occluded bodies, which is why they are evaluated with OKS-based AP per joint and not by eye.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Reporting box AP for a mask model and calling it segmentation accuracy: masks scored 5.6 points lower here.
  • Expecting fine detail from a 28 × 28 mask on a large object: hair, fingers and thin poles get smoothed. Use a higher-resolution head (PointRend-style refinement) or a semantic model for those.
  • Using raw instance masks as a panoptic output: they overlap, so you need a rule (highest score wins) to assign each pixel once.
  • Evaluating keypoints with pixel distance alone, which penalises large people and forgives small ones; OKS normalises by scale.
  • Forgetting the mask threshold: masks are probabilities, not booleans.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How does Mask R-CNN turn a detector into an instance segmenter?

How a strong answer sounds
It keeps Faster R-CNN unchanged and adds a third head. For each final box, RoIAlign samples a 14 × 14 feature patch, a few convolutions upsample it to a 28 × 28 mask per class, and the mask of the predicted class is resized into the box and thresholded. The mask is a per-pixel sigmoid for that class alone, so the classifier decides what the object is and the mask head only decides which pixels belong to it. RoIAlign matters because masks are pixel-accurate: rounding the box onto the feature grid, as RoIPool did, shifts the outline. Measured on 500 COCO images, the model scored 51.9 box AP and 46.3 mask AP; the same boxes filled in and scored as masks reached only 15.1, so the mask head is doing most of the segmentation work.

Deeper: Your instance segmenter’s mask AP is fine at IoU 0.5 but poor at 0.75 and on small objects. What would you look at?

How a strong answer sounds
That is the signature of mask resolution and alignment rather than recognition: objects are found, outlines are coarse. Measured here, the box-to-mask gap was 2.6 points at IoU 0.5 and 6.1 at 0.75, and small-object mask AP was 28.4 against 36.6 for boxes. I would check that RoIAlign (not RoIPool) is used and that masks are pasted with correct sub-pixel box coordinates; try a larger mask head or point-based refinement around the boundary, which spends computation where the outline is; increase input resolution or add a finer FPN level for small objects; and check the labels, because COCO polygons are themselves coarse, so at strict IoU the ceiling is annotation noise. I would confirm with a per-size breakdown and boundary IoU, not mask AP alone.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Semantic labels pixels, instance separates countable objects, panoptic does both with one label per pixel.
  • Mask R-CNN adds a 28 × 28 per-class sigmoid mask head on RoIAligned features to Faster R-CNN.
  • Measured: 51.9 box AP and 46.3 mask AP; the gap is largest at strict IoU and on small objects.
  • Filled-in boxes scored as masks got 15.1 AP: the mask head carries the segmentation.
  • Keypoints are 56 × 56 heatmaps per joint, matched with scale-normalised OKS; PQ = SQ × RQ.

Check your understanding

Two people stand touching. What does semantic segmentation output for them?

Semantic segmentation has a class per pixel but no notion of instances; separating them needs instance or panoptic segmentation.

Why does Mask R-CNN predict masks with a per-class sigmoid instead of a softmax over classes?

Decoupling “what” from “where” was one of the paper’s key findings; a softmax across classes did worse.

Where was the gap between box AP and mask AP largest in the measurement?

Small 36.6 → 28.4 and AP75 57.5 → 51.4, but large 64.7 → 63.1: a fixed 28 × 28 mask is the bottleneck for fine outlines.

What does OKS normalise the keypoint error by?

exp(−d² / (2s²k²)): s is the square root of the person’s area and k reflects how precisely annotators can place that joint.

A panoptic model finds every object but its outlines are rough. Which factor of PQ drops?

Matches still clear IoU 0.5, so RQ stays high; their average IoU falls, which is SQ.
