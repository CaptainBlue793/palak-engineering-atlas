Efficient Convolutions

A standard 3 × 3 convolution mixes space and channels in one step, and pays for both at once: K² × Cin × Cout multiply-adds per position. Mobile networks split it into cheaper parts, a per-channel spatial filter and a 1 × 1 channel mixer, and cut the arithmetic by eight or nine times.

Fewer operations is not the same as faster, and this chapter measures by how much. On this GPU a depthwise convolution with 64 times fewer FLOPs than a standard one ran only 3 times faster, and MobileNet v2, with a sixth of ResNet-18’s FLOPs, was slower than it at batch 1. On a single CPU core the picture flips. The lesson for every efficiency claim: count, then time it on the hardware you will ship on.

Grouped, depthwise and pointwise

A grouped convolution with g groups splits the channels into g independent sets: each output channel sees only Cin/g inputs, so cost and parameters fall by g. Taken to the limit, g = C, it is a depthwise convolution: each channel is filtered on its own with its own 3 × 3 kernel, no mixing between channels at all. A pointwise (1 × 1) convolution is the opposite: no spatial extent, only channel mixing. Put a depthwise and a pointwise together and you have a depthwise-separable convolution, the factorisation P5 used for blurs (2k instead of k²), applied to channels.

C = 64
standard  = nn.Conv2d(C, C, 3, padding=1, bias=False)                 # 36,864 params: 9·C·C
grouped   = nn.Conv2d(C, C, 3, padding=1, groups=8, bias=False)       #  4,608: 9·C·C/8
depthwise = nn.Conv2d(C, C, 3, padding=1, groups=C, bias=False)       #    576: 9·C, one 3x3 per channel
pointwise = nn.Conv2d(C, C, 1, bias=False)                            #  4,096: C·C, mixes channels only
separable = nn.Sequential(depthwise, pointwise)                          #  4,672: 9·C + C·C, ≈ 7.9x fewer

For C channels the saving of the separable version is 9C² / (9C + C²) = 9 / (1 + 9/C): 7.9× at 64 channels, 8.7× at 256. MobileNet v1 (2017) is essentially a stack of these. MobileNet v2 (2018) wraps them in an inverted residual: a 1 × 1 convolution that expands the channels six times, a depthwise 3 × 3 in that wide space, and a 1 × 1 that projects back down, with the residual connection between the narrow ends. It is ResNet’s bottleneck (Chapter 7) turned inside out: narrow–wide–narrow instead of wide–narrow–wide, because depthwise filtering is cheap enough to afford a wide middle.

The depthwise layer did 64 times less arithmetic and took a third of the time, because the GPU was not limited by arithmetic. Each depthwise output needs 9 multiply-adds, but every input and output value still has to be read from and written to memory, so the layer’s arithmetic intensity, FLOPs per byte moved, is tiny. Memory bandwidth sets its speed (P6 of the LLMs course calls this memory-bound), and tensor cores sit idle: 3.1 TFLOPS against 62.5 for the dense layer. The inverted residual is the extreme case: expanding 64 channels to 384 at 56 × 56 resolution creates a 384 × 56 × 56 tensor per image that is written and read three times, and the block took 12 times as long as a standard convolution.

On the GPU at batch 1, ResNet-18 was the fastest network here, 2.3 times faster than MobileNet v2 despite six times the FLOPs. A batch of one image cannot fill the GPU, so time is set by the number of layers and kernel launches (MobileNet v2 has 52 convolutions to ResNet-18’s 20, each with its own normalisation and activation) rather than by arithmetic. At batch 64 the GPU fills and MobileNet v3 pulled ahead, but only by 22%, nowhere near its 8× FLOP advantage. On one CPU core, where arithmetic is the bottleneck, the FLOP savings appeared: MobileNet v2 was 2.9 times faster than ResNet-18 and MobileNet v3 3.5 times. That is the hardware these networks were designed for. EfficientNet-B0, more accurate than MobileNet (Chapter 11), paid for its squeeze-and-excitation blocks and SiLU activations with more time per FLOP everywhere.

Does factorising cost accuracy?

Replacing every 3 × 3 convolution with a depthwise-separable pair (with BatchNorm and ReLU after each half) cut parameters 6.5 times and FLOPs 6.2 times, and cost 3.1 points of accuracy: 88.13% against 91.20%. Doubling the widths won back half the gap (89.60%) with still half the parameters and 60% of the FLOPs of the standard model. Training time went the other way: the separable models took longer to train on this GPU (47.6 s and 90.8 s against 43.5 s), for the memory-bound reasons above. Factorised convolutions are a good trade where arithmetic is the bottleneck and a poor one where it is not. (A first version of this run trained with the model accidentally left in evaluation mode, so BatchNorm never adapted and the separable nets diverged; the numbers here are from the corrected re-run.)

Use case: designing for the phone, then measuring on it

MobileNet v3 (2019) was not designed by counting FLOPs alone: its architecture was found by a search that measured latency on real Pixel phones, and the authors then hand-adjusted the parts that the measurements showed were expensive, such as the first and last layers, and replaced the swish activation with a cheaper “hard” version because it ran faster on mobile hardware. EfficientNet’s family was searched for FLOP efficiency and later found to be slower on GPUs than its FLOPs suggested, which motivated GPU-aware designs such as RegNet and EfficientNetV2. The pattern in this chapter’s numbers is the reason: efficiency is a property of a model on a device, not of the model.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Equating FLOPs with latency. 64× fewer FLOPs bought 3× on the GPU here.
  • Benchmarking a mobile network on a server GPU and concluding it is slow, or the reverse.
  • Timing at batch 64 for a service that runs batch 1. The ranking changed between the two.
  • Forgetting the expansion in inverted residuals when estimating memory: the wide middle tensor dominates.
  • Leaving out the nonlinearity and normalisation between depthwise and pointwise layers without testing it.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is a depthwise-separable convolution and how much does it save?

How a strong answer sounds
It splits a standard K × K convolution into two steps: a depthwise convolution that filters each channel separately with its own K × K kernel (K²·C weights), then a 1 × 1 pointwise convolution that mixes channels (C·C′ weights). The standard layer costs K²·C·C′ per position; the separable one K²·C + C·C′, a saving of roughly K² when the channel count is large: 7.9× for 3 × 3 at 64 channels, 8.7× at 256. The caveat is speed: on a GPU the depthwise step has so little arithmetic per byte that it is memory-bound; a 64-channel depthwise layer here ran at 3 TFLOPS against 62 for the standard convolution, so the separable pair was only 1.8× faster for 7.9× fewer FLOPs.

Deeper: Why can MobileNet v2 be slower than ResNet-18 on a GPU, and when would you still choose it?

How a strong answer sounds
At small batch on a GPU, time is dominated by kernel launches and memory traffic, not arithmetic. MobileNet v2 has many more, thinner layers than ResNet-18, and its depthwise and expansion layers are memory-bound, so at batch 1 it took 2.6 ms against 1.1 ms for ResNet-18 despite six times fewer FLOPs; at batch 64 it was only 17% faster. On a CPU or a mobile chip with less parallelism, arithmetic is the limit, and the FLOP savings show: on one CPU core it was 2.9 times faster. So I would choose MobileNet-style models for CPUs, phones and microcontrollers, or where memory and model size matter (3.5 M parameters against 11.7 M), and choose ResNet- or RegNet-style dense convolutions for GPU serving, after timing both on the target at the deployed batch size.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Grouped convs divide cost by g; depthwise (g = C) filters each channel alone; pointwise (1 × 1) mixes channels.
  • Depthwise-separable = depthwise + pointwise: 7.9× fewer FLOPs at 64 channels.
  • On the GPU those savings shrink: 64× fewer FLOPs ran 3× faster, because depthwise layers are memory-bound.
  • MobileNet v2 was slower than ResNet-18 on the GPU at batch 1 and 2.9× faster on one CPU core.
  • Inverted residuals expand channels 6× in the middle; their wide tensors cost time and memory.
  • Count FLOPs to shortlist; time on the target hardware at the real batch size to decide.

Check your understanding

How many weights does a depthwise 3 × 3 convolution on 64 channels have?

One 3 × 3 kernel per channel: 9 × 64. A standard 3 × 3 from 64 to 64 channels has 9 × 64 × 64 = 36,864.

A depthwise layer did 64× fewer FLOPs than a standard one but ran only 3× faster on the GPU. Why?

9 multiply-adds per output cannot keep tensor cores busy; the layer ran at 3.1 TFLOPS against 62.5 for the dense one.

Where did MobileNet’s FLOP savings turn into the biggest speed-up over ResNet-18?

9.5 ms against 27.6 ms on one core (2.9×). On the GPU at batch 1 MobileNet v2 was 2.3× slower.

What is “inverted” about MobileNet v2’s inverted residual?

A 1 × 1 expands channels (×6), a depthwise 3 × 3 filters the wide tensor, a 1 × 1 projects back; the residual joins the narrow ends.

What is the right way to choose between two architectures for a phone app?

Rankings changed between GPU and CPU and between batch sizes here. Only measurement on the target settles it.
