Classical Filtering: Blur, Edges & Kernels

Before networks learned their own filters, image processing ran on hand-made ones: a small grid of weights slid across the image, giving a weighted sum at each position. Blur, sharpen, find edges, remove noise: each is just a different 3 × 3 or 9 × 9 grid of numbers.

This matters for deep learning because a convolutional layer does exactly this, with weights it learns instead of weights you choose. This chapter writes the sliding sum by hand, checks it against OpenCV, SciPy and PyTorch, and measures the practical details that carry straight over to CNNs: correlation versus convolution, separable kernels, what happens at the border, and why the right filter depends on the noise.

  • Tell correlation from convolution, and know which one each library computes
  • Use separable kernels to cut the cost of large blurs from k² to 2k multiplies per pixel

A kernel sliding over an image

Take a small grid of weights, the kernel, say 3 × 3. Place its centre on a pixel, multiply each weight by the pixel under it, add the nine products, and write the sum to the same position in a new image. Move one pixel along and repeat. With all nine weights equal to 1/9 the output is the local average: a blur. With weights that are negative on the left and positive on the right, the output is large where brightness rises from left to right: a vertical-edge detector.

import numpy as np

def correlate(img: np.ndarray, k: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """out[y, x] = sum over (i, j) of k[i, j] * img[y + i - r, x + j - r], zeros outside the image."""
    r = k.shape[0] // 2
    p = np.pad(img, r)                                   # (H + 2r, W + 2r) zero border
    out = np.zeros_like(img)                            # (H, W)
    for i in range(k.shape[0]):                          # loop over the k*k weights, not the pixels:
        for j in range(k.shape[1]):                      # each step adds one shifted copy of the image
            out += k[i, j] * p[i:i + img.shape[0], j:j + img.shape[1]]
    return out

sobel_x = np.array([[-1, 0, 1], [-2, 0, 2], [-1, 0, 1]], float)
edges = correlate(gray, sobel_x)                          # (300, 451): + where it gets brighter to the right

Looping over the nine weights rather than the 135,300 pixels is the trick that makes this fast in NumPy: nine whole-image multiply-adds. On the cat photo in greyscale, this function and cv2.filter2D, scipy.ndimage.correlate and PyTorch’s F.conv2d (with padding=1) all agreed to the last bit: a maximum difference of 0.0.

The sum of the weights tells you what happens to flat regions. A sum of 1 keeps the brightness (blurs and sharpeners). A sum of 0 turns any flat area into 0 and responds only to change (edge detectors), so its output is signed: positive on one side of an edge, negative on the other. That is why edge maps are usually shown as an absolute value, or as the gradient magnitude √(gx² + gy²) from Sobel x and Sobel y together. On the cat photo the magnitude reached 533.5 and was above 100 for 11.4% of pixels.

Correlation or convolution?

Strictly, what the listing computes is cross-correlation. Mathematical convolution flips the kernel in both directions first. For symmetric kernels (box, Gaussian, Laplacian) the two are identical. For Sobel they differ by a sign: SciPy’s ndimage.convolve on the cat gave exactly the negative of the hand-written result (maximum difference from the negation: 0.0, from the original: 1,550).

Deep-learning “convolution” layers are all correlations. For learned kernels it makes no difference (the network simply learns the flipped weights), but it matters the moment you load a hand-designed kernel into a layer or port a filter from a signal-processing textbook.

Separable kernels

A k × k kernel costs k² multiplies per pixel. Some kernels are an outer product of a column and a row, and then the 2-D filter equals a 1-D pass down the columns followed by a 1-D pass along the rows: 2k multiplies. The box and Gaussian kernels are separable, and so is Sobel ([1, 2, 1] down, [−1, 0, 1] across, which is why Sobel is “smooth one way, differentiate the other”). In linear-algebra terms, a kernel is separable exactly when it has rank 1; the Laplacian has rank 2 and is not.

k1 = gauss1d(sigma=5)                                    # (31,) sums to 1
k2 = np.outer(k1, k1)                                     # (31, 31): 961 weights
a = cv2.filter2D(img, -1, k2)                             # 12.53 ms on a 1920 x 1080 float image
b = cv2.sepFilter2D(img, -1, k1, k1)                      # 1.51 ms, max difference 7.6e-05

What happens at the border

At the edge of the image part of the kernel hangs off the side, so something must be assumed about the missing pixels. The common choices are zeros (what F.conv2d’s padding does), repeating the edge pixel, mirroring the image, or wrapping round to the other side. A 9 × 9 box blur on the cat, comparing the mean of the leftmost output column with the true mean of the leftmost five columns (124.4):

CNNs use zero padding almost everywhere, and the network learns to cope, but it does learn it: features near the border behave differently, and a model can partly infer where in the image a patch sits from the padding. Noise decides the filter

Blurring removes noise because noise changes from pixel to pixel while the image mostly does not, so averaging cancels the noise faster than it destroys detail. But not every noise is the same. Each filter below was run on the full-size greyscale photo with noise added, and scored by PSNR (peak signal-to-noise ratio, 10·log₁₀(255² / mean squared error); higher is better, and +3 dB means the error energy has halved):

With Gaussian grain (σ = 20) the Gaussian blur did best (30.34 dB) and the median was worse (28.23). With 5% salt-and-pepper dots the 3 × 3 median won by a mile (33.92 dB against 28.07 for the Gaussian), because a dot is an outlier and a median simply ignores it, while an average smears it into a grey blob. The bilateral filter, which averages only over neighbours of similar brightness, kept edges for grain (29.98) but did badly on dots (21.37), because a dot has no similar-brightness neighbours to average with. The same reasoning, matching the operation to the corruption, comes back in robustness testing (Chapter 37).

Use case: Canny edges in a document scanner

Phone apps that crop a photographed receipt or page to its corners commonly run a classic pipeline: greyscale, Gaussian blur, Canny edge detection (Sobel gradients, thinning to one-pixel lines, then two thresholds), find the largest four-sided contour, and warp it flat with a homography (P4). The blur is not optional. On the cat photo, Canny with thresholds 100 and 200 marked 6.45% of pixels as edges; after a 5 × 5 Gaussian blur (σ = 1.4) only 1.03%, because the fur texture stopped producing edges and only the strong outlines remained. A document finder wants exactly that: the page boundary, not the paper grain.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Filtering uint8 images with signed kernels, so negative edge responses are clipped to 0 and half the edges vanish. Use a float or CV_16S output.
  • Assuming every “convolve” flips the kernel. OpenCV and PyTorch correlate; SciPy’s convolve flips.
  • Using a 2-D kernel when a separable one exists, paying k² instead of 2k per pixel.
  • Forgetting the border, so a blurred image gets a dark frame from zero padding.
  • Using a mean or Gaussian filter on impulse noise, where a median is far better.
  • Running edge detection without a pre-blur, and getting texture instead of outlines.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What does a 3 × 3 Sobel kernel compute, and why do its weights sum to zero?

How a strong answer sounds
Sobel x is [−1, 0, 1] across the columns, weighted [1, 2, 1] down the rows: it takes the right column minus the left column, with the middle row counted twice. That is a finite-difference estimate of the horizontal brightness gradient, smoothed vertically so that single-pixel noise matters less. Because the weights sum to zero, a flat region gives zero output; only a change in brightness gives a response, positive when the image gets brighter to the right and negative when it gets darker. Sobel y is the same kernel transposed. Together they give a gradient vector at every pixel, whose length √(gx² + gy²) is the edge strength and whose angle is the edge direction. It is also separable, so it costs 6 multiplies per pixel instead of 9.

Deeper: How does a hand-made filter relate to the first layer of a CNN, and what do CNNs do about borders?

How a strong answer sounds
A convolutional layer computes the same sliding weighted sum, a cross-correlation, but with many kernels at once, each spanning all input channels, and with weights learned by gradient descent instead of chosen by hand. Trained first layers end up looking like the classic filters: oriented edge detectors similar to Sobel and Gabor filters, colour blobs and blurs. The difference is that the network picks whatever filters reduce its loss, and stacks layers so later filters combine earlier responses. For borders, CNNs almost always zero-pad so the output keeps its size; that introduces the dark-frame effect, and networks learn to work with it, but it means features near the edge differ from those in the middle, which can leak position information and hurt translation equivariance near the border. Reflect padding is available (padding_mode="reflect") and is common in image-to-image models such as super-resolution and style transfer, where edge artefacts are visible.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • A filter is a small grid of weights slid over the image: a weighted sum at every position.
  • Weights summing to 1 keep brightness (blur, sharpen); summing to 0 respond only to change (edges), with signed outputs.
  • OpenCV’s filter2D and PyTorch’s conv2d compute correlation; true convolution flips the kernel. For Sobel the two differ by a sign.
  • Rank-1 kernels are separable: 2k instead of k² multiplies. A 31 × 31 Gaussian ran in 1.51 ms instead of 12.53 ms.
  • Zero padding darkens the border (68.7 against a true 124.4 for a 9 × 9 blur); reflect or replicate avoid it.
  • Match the filter to the noise: Gaussian blur for grain (30.3 dB), median for salt and pepper (33.9 dB).

Check your understanding

A kernel’s weights sum to 0. What is its output on a perfectly flat region of the image?

Every pixel is the same value v, so the output is v × (sum of weights) = 0. Such kernels (Sobel, Laplacian) respond only to change.

Which of these computes a true convolution, flipping the kernel?

SciPy’s convolve flips the kernel; it gave exactly the negative of the hand-written Sobel result. OpenCV and PyTorch compute correlation.

How many multiplies per pixel does a separable 31 × 31 Gaussian need?

Two 1-D passes of 31 weights each: 62, against 961 for the full 2-D kernel.

An image has 5% of its pixels randomly set to pure black or white. Which filter removed this best?

Median 3 × 3 reached 33.92 dB; the best averaging filter 28.66 and bilateral 21.37. A median ignores outliers; an average spreads them.

Why did a 9 × 9 box blur with zero padding make the left edge of the photo dark?

At the leftmost column, 4 of the kernel’s 9 columns lie outside the image, where zero padding supplies black: 68.7 instead of about 124.
