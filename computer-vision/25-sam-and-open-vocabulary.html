Foundation Models: SAM & Open-Vocabulary Detection

The segmenters and detectors of Chapters 13–18 knew a fixed list of classes and had to be retrained for a new one. Two families of foundation models broke that limit. Segment Anything (SAM, 2023) segments whatever you point at, with no notion of class at all. Open-vocabulary detectors such as OWL-ViT find objects named in free text, using CLIP-style image–text training (Chapter 24) to understand the names.

This chapter measures both on COCO, the dataset neither was tuned on. Given one click, SAM usually had the right mask among its three candidates (73.9 mean IoU for the best of the three) but picked the wrong one itself so often that its default answer averaged 48.1; a box prompt gave 77.1. OWL-ViT, given only COCO’s 80 class names, reached 32.6 AP, and found French horns and parachutes, classes COCO does not have, in 92.5% of photos.

Promptable segmentation

SAM reframes segmentation as answering a prompt. The prompt is a click on the object (a positive point), a click on what is not the object (a negative point), a box, or a rough mask; the answer is a mask. There are no classes: SAM does not know or care whether the object is a “dog”. It was trained on SA-1B, 11 million images with 1.1 billion masks, most of them produced by SAM itself in a data engine where annotators corrected its proposals and each round of corrections trained a better model.

A single click is ambiguous. A click on a person’s shirt could mean the shirt, the person, or the person and the bicycle they are riding. Rather than averaging these into a mush, SAM outputs three masks for a single-point prompt, roughly a part, an object and a whole, each with a predicted IoU score, and the caller picks one (by default the highest-scoring). With more prompts the ambiguity shrinks and SAM returns one mask.

A heavy encoder and a light decoder

SAM is split for interactive use. The image encoder, a ViT pre-trained as an MAE (Chapter 23) and run at 1,024 × 1,024, turns the image into a 64 × 64 grid of embeddings; it is most of the computation and runs once per image. The prompt encoder turns clicks and boxes into a few tokens, and the mask decoder, two small transformer blocks attending between prompt tokens and image embeddings, produces masks from them. Because the decoder is tiny, every new click is answered from the cached embedding in milliseconds, which is what makes a click-to-segment tool feel instant.

from transformers import SamModel, SamProcessor
sam = SamModel.from_pretrained("facebook/sam-vit-base").cuda().eval(); sp = SamProcessor.from_pretrained("facebook/sam-vit-base")
emb = sam.get_image_embeddings(sp(images=im, return_tensors="pt")["pixel_values"].cuda())   # once per image: the expensive part
for click in clicks:                                                                        # every click reuses emb
    p = sp(images=im, input_points=[[[click]]], return_tensors="pt").to("cuda")
    out = sam(image_embeddings=emb, input_points=p["input_points"], multimask_output=True)
    masks, scores = out.pred_masks, out.iou_scores                                      # 3 candidate masks and SAM's own IoU guess for each

The split works as designed: after the 78.5 ms encoder pass, each prompt cost 3.8 ms, fast enough to follow a mouse. The accuracy numbers carry the real lesson. With one click, the right mask was usually among the three (73.9 mean IoU if you could pick the best), but SAM’s own predicted-IoU score chose badly: its default answer averaged 48.1, and it did worst on larger objects (44.5–45.7 mean IoU above 5,000 pixels against 55.0 for small ones), where “part or whole?” is most ambiguous. A box removes that ambiguity and gave the best result, 77.1. Three points did worse than the best single-point mask because the random extra points sometimes fell on thin or occluded parts and were trusted literally. Note that COCO’s polygon annotations are themselves coarse; at IoU above 0.9 some of the “errors” are SAM being more precise than the label.

Open-vocabulary detection

A closed-set detector ends in a classifier over its training classes. OWL-ViT (2022) replaces that with text. It starts from a CLIP-style ViT whose image and text encoders are already aligned, removes the final pooling so that every patch token yields a box and an embedding, and fine-tunes on detection data. At inference, the class names you care about are embedded with the text encoder, and each predicted box is scored by the similarity between its embedding and each query. The class list is now an input. Grounding DINO and OWLv2 follow the same principle with stronger backbones and better training.

On COCO, using its 80 class names as queries and no COCO-specific training, OWL-ViT B/32 scored:

Without ever being trained on COCO’s boxes, OWL-ViT reached 32.6 AP from the class names alone, about 9 points short of a 2017-era detector that was trained on them and 19 short of a modern one; the image sets overlap but are not identical (300 against 500 images), so read the gap as approximate. Its weak spot was small objects, 16.1 AP: with 32-pixel patches, a small object is a fraction of one token. The real point of open vocabulary is the other test. For five Imagenette classes COCO has no category for, a query such as “a photo of a French horn” put a box scoring above 0.1 on 92.5% of French-horn photos and parachute photos, and on every photo of a fish; chain saws (52.5%) and gas pumps (65.0%) were harder, plausibly because they are rarer in captions. This test only checks that the detector fired confidently for the right query, not that the box was in the right place, so it is an optimistic measure.

Where they fail

The measurements already show the two biggest: SAM’s own choice among its three masks was wrong often enough to cost 26 points of mean IoU, and OWL-ViT’s small-object AP was 16.1. SAM has no idea what it segments, so it cannot tell you which mask is the “product” and which the “shadow”, and it over-segments textured surfaces into parts. Open-vocabulary detectors inherit CLIP’s weaknesses (Chapter 24): rare or technical names, counts, attributes such as “the red one on the left”, and text written on objects. Both were trained on web-like photos, so microscopy, X-rays and satellite tiles need checking or fine-tuning before use.

Use case: auto-labelling a new dataset

A team building a detector for a new product line needs boxes and masks for 20,000 images. A common pipeline today: an open-vocabulary detector proposes boxes from the product names, SAM turns each box into a mask, and human annotators only accept, reject or fix, which is several times faster than drawing from scratch. Box prompts are used rather than clicks because they were far more reliable here. The final, fast detector is then trained on the corrected labels, because the foundation models are too slow and too unpredictable to deploy directly on a line running at 60 frames per second. The measurements in this chapter are the acceptance test: on your data, how often is SAM’s mask right first time, and what does the detector miss?

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Re-running SAM’s image encoder for every click: cache the embedding.
  • Trusting the single-point mask: SAM’s top-scored mask averaged 48.1 IoU against 73.9 for the best of its three; show users all three, or prompt with a box.
  • Expecting closed-set accuracy from open-vocabulary detection on a benchmark with its own trained detectors: OWL-ViT reached 32.6 AP on COCO against 51.3 for a detector trained on it.
  • Using dataset codes as queries (“n03394916”, “class_7”) instead of words people write.
  • Deploying foundation models on a tight latency budget when a small model trained on their labels would do.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is SAM, and why is it split into an encoder and a decoder?

How a strong answer sounds
SAM is a promptable segmenter: give it a point, a box or a rough mask and it returns a mask for whatever the prompt indicates, with no class labels. It was trained on 1.1 billion masks built with a model-in-the-loop data engine. The image encoder is a large ViT that runs once per image and produces an embedding grid; the prompt encoder and mask decoder are tiny and run for every prompt, so an interactive tool can respond to each click in milliseconds from the cached embedding. For a single click it returns three masks for the part, object and whole interpretations, with predicted IoUs. Measured on COCO objects with SAM ViT-B, the encoder took 78.5 ms and each prompt 3.8 ms. A single click’s top-scored mask averaged only 48.1 IoU, although the best of its three averaged 73.9, while a box prompt gave 77.1, so for automation I prompt with boxes.

Deeper: How does an open-vocabulary detector find a class it never saw in its detection training?

How a strong answer sounds
Its classifier is replaced by similarity to text embeddings. The backbone comes from CLIP-style image–text pre-training on hundreds of millions of captioned images, so region embeddings and text embeddings share a space that covers far more concepts than any detection dataset. Detection fine-tuning teaches it to localise and to keep region embeddings aligned with text. At inference you embed the names you want and score every predicted box against them. It works for concepts the captions covered and degrades for rare, technical or compositional queries. On COCO’s 80 names OWL-ViT B/32 scored 32.6 AP with no COCO training, against 51.3 for a trained Faster R-CNN v2, and it fired on French horns and parachutes, which COCO lacks, in over 90% of photos; for production I would fine-tune on my own classes or use it to propose labels for a closed-set detector.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • SAM segments whatever a point, box or mask prompt indicates, with no classes; one click returns three candidate masks.
  • Its heavy ViT encoder runs once per image and the light decoder per prompt, so clicks are cheap.
  • Measured: one click’s default mask averaged 48.1 IoU, the best of its three 73.9, a box 77.1; the decoder took 3.8 ms against the encoder’s 78.5.
  • Open-vocabulary detectors score boxes against text embeddings, so the class list is an input.
  • OWL-ViT reached 32.6 AP on COCO from names alone (a trained detector: 51.3) and found classes COCO lacks; it is weakest on small objects.

Why does SAM return three masks for a single click?

Which part of SAM runs once per image?

Its embedding is cached; the decoder answers each prompt from it.

What does SAM know about the class of the object it segments?

SAM is class-agnostic; to name the object, pair it with a classifier or an open-vocabulary detector.

How does OWL-ViT decide that a box contains a “French horn”?

The classifier is a set of text embeddings supplied at inference.
