Residual Networks

Deeper networks should be at least as good as shallow ones: the extra layers could simply learn to pass their input through unchanged. In practice, before 2015, they were worse, and not only on the test set but on the training set too. Something about depth itself made networks harder to optimise.

This chapter reproduces that on CIFAR-10 with the exact setup of the ResNet paper’s CIFAR experiments: plain and residual networks of 20, 56 and 110 layers, trained identically. The plain 110-layer network did not learn at all; the residual one with the same layers scored 91.4%. The gradients at the very first step already show why.

The degradation problem

Take a CNN that works and make it deeper by stacking more of the same 3 × 3 convolution layers. With BatchNorm the deeper network starts training without trouble, yet it ends with higher training error than the shallower one. That rules out overfitting, which would show low training error and high test error. The deeper network has more capacity and could in principle copy the shallow one (set the extra layers to identity), but gradient descent does not find that solution. He et al. called this the degradation problem.

The CIFAR networks from their paper: a 3 × 3 stem with 16 channels, then three stages of n blocks at 16, 32 and 64 channels and 32, 16 and 8 pixels, each block two 3 × 3 convolutions, then global average pooling and a linear layer: 6n + 2 layers. Plain and residual versions have exactly the same layers and parameters; the only difference is one addition per block. All six were trained identically here: 30 epochs, SGD with Nesterov momentum, one-cycle learning rate peaking at 0.1, batch 256, crops and flips, bf16.

The residual networks went the other way: every extra block helped a little, 89.8% → 90.8% → 91.4%, with training loss falling each time.

The residual block

A residual block computes y = x + F(x), where F is the block’s two convolutions. Instead of learning the whole mapping, the block learns a correction to its input. If the best thing a block can do is nothing, it only has to push F towards zero, which is easy; a plain block would have to learn the identity through two convolutions and ReLUs, which is not.

class BasicBlock(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, c_in, c_out, stride=1, residual=True):
        super().__init__()
        self.c1 = nn.Conv2d(c_in, c_out, 3, stride, 1, bias=False); self.n1 = nn.BatchNorm2d(c_out)
        self.c2 = nn.Conv2d(c_out, c_out, 3, 1, 1, bias=False); self.n2 = nn.BatchNorm2d(c_out)
        self.residual = residual
        # when the shape changes (stride 2, more channels), the shortcut needs a 1x1 projection
        self.short = nn.Identity() if stride == 1 and c_in == c_out else \
                     nn.Sequential(nn.Conv2d(c_in, c_out, 1, stride, bias=False), nn.BatchNorm2d(c_out))
    def forward(self, x):                                       # x: (N, c_in, H, W)
        y = F.relu(self.n1(self.c1(x)))                             # (N, c_out, H/s, W/s)
        y = self.n2(self.c2(y))
        return F.relu(y + self.short(x)) if self.residual else F.relu(y)   # the one-line difference

Why it works: gradients

Backpropagation multiplies the gradient by each layer’s local derivative on the way back. Through a plain stack that is a product of 110 factors, and a product of many numbers drifts towards zero or infinity. Through a residual block the derivative is I + ∂F/∂x: the identity term gives every gradient a direct path back to early layers, so the product never has to pass through every convolution. The surprise is the direction. With BatchNorm the plain networks did not suffer vanishing gradients here; their early-layer gradients exploded, millions of times larger than at the output for 110 layers, so the first SGD steps throw the early weights around and training never recovers (it is known that stacking BatchNorm and ReLU without skips can make gradients grow exponentially with depth at initialisation). In the residual networks the ratio stayed between 0.3 and 1.8 at every depth: every layer receives a gradient of a sensible size, and depth stops being an obstacle. Bottlenecks and the ResNet family

For the deeper ImageNet models (ResNet-50, -101, -152) a pair of 3 × 3 convolutions on 256 channels would be expensive, so each block is a bottleneck: a 1 × 1 convolution that reduces 256 channels to 64, a 3 × 3 convolution at 64, and a 1 × 1 that expands back to 256, with the shortcut around all three. Per position that is 256·64 + 9·64·64 + 64·256 = 69,632 multiply-adds, against 2 × 9 × 256 × 256 = 1,179,648 for two full 3 × 3 convolutions at 256 channels: 17 times cheaper, with the 3 × 3 working in a narrower space.

🧾
Use case: the residual connection everywhere

ResNet won the 2015 ImageNet classification challenge with a 152-layer network and the COCO detection and segmentation challenges with ResNet backbones. The idea outlived the architecture: every transformer layer is two residual blocks (x + Attention(x), then x + MLP(x)), so a large language model is a residual network many dozens of blocks deep; the U-Nets inside diffusion models are built from residual blocks; and the detection and segmentation backbones in the rest of this course are ResNets or descendants. When a new architecture trains at great depth, look for the skip connection.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Reading higher training error in a deeper net as overfitting. Overfitting lowers training error; degradation raises it.
  • Adding x to F(x) when their shapes differ. Downsampling blocks need a projection (1 × 1, stride 2) or a padded shortcut.
  • Putting the ReLU on the shortcut path, or a BatchNorm after the addition, which breaks the clean identity path.
  • Stacking more plain layers to “add capacity”: plain-110 scored 12.7%.
  • Comparing ResNet variants trained with different recipes and attributing the gap to depth.
🎤
Interview drill

Explain it simply: Why do residual connections let networks be much deeper?

How a strong answer sounds
A residual block outputs x + F(x), so it only has to learn a correction to its input, and the identity is the easy default: push F towards zero. During backpropagation the block’s derivative is the identity plus F’s derivative, so the gradient has a direct path back through every block instead of being multiplied through every convolution, and it neither explodes nor vanishes with depth. Measured on CIFAR-10 with identical layers and training, a plain 110-layer network reached 12.7% test accuracy while the residual one reached 91.4%; at initialisation the plain-110’s first-layer gradient was 4.6 million times its last-layer gradient, against 1.8 times for the residual version.

Deeper: What is a bottleneck block, and why does ResNet-50 use it while ResNet-18 does not?

How a strong answer sounds
A bottleneck block reduces the channels with a 1 × 1 convolution (256 → 64), does the 3 × 3 spatial convolution at the reduced width, and expands back with another 1 × 1 (64 → 256), with the shortcut around all three. At 256 channels that costs about 70,000 multiply-adds per position against about 1.2 million for two full 3 × 3 convolutions, 17 times less, so the network can afford more blocks and wider features for the same compute. ResNet-18 and -34 use narrower stages (64–512 channels) where two 3 × 3 convolutions are already affordable, so the basic block is simpler and faster; from ResNet-50 up, the bottleneck is what makes 50, 101 or 152 layers affordable at 4, 8 or 11 GFLOPs.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Deeper plain networks can train worse, on the training set itself: that is degradation, not overfitting.
  • A residual block outputs x + F(x); with matching shapes the shortcut is free.
  • Plain 20 → 56 → 110 layers scored 88.2% → 79.6% → 12.7%; residual 89.8% → 90.8% → 91.4%, same layers and training.
  • At initialisation the plain-110’s early gradients exploded to 4.6 million times the output’s; the residual ratio stayed below 2.
  • Bottlenecks (1×1 → 3×3 → 1×1) make deep, wide networks affordable: 17× cheaper than two 3 × 3s at 256 channels.
