What Computer Vision Is

Computer vision is getting useful answers out of pixels: what is in this photo, where each thing is, which pixels belong to it, and, more recently, what a photo of something that does not exist would look like. Each of those is a different task with a different kind of output, and models for each now come pretrained in a single import.

This chapter runs three of them, a classifier, a detector and a segmenter from torchvision, on the same seven photos, and looks closely at what comes back: the shapes of the outputs, how long they take on this GPU, and the confident nonsense they produce the moment a photo falls outside what they were trained on. The rest of the course opens each of these models up and builds them.

The tasks

Most of vision is a handful of tasks that differ in how much they say about each image. A classifier gives one label per image. A detector gives a list of boxes, each with a label and a confidence. A semantic segmenter gives a label for every pixel; an instance segmenter also separates touching objects of the same class. A generator runs the other way, from a description or from noise to pixels.

The label sets matter as much as the architectures. The classifier knows 1,000 ImageNet categories, including 120 dog breeds and no “person”. The detector knows COCO’s 80 everyday categories: person, car, cup, spoon, but not French horn or rocket. The segmenter knows only the 20 Pascal VOC classes: cat and person, but not truck. Every answer a model gives is one of its own labels, however poorly that label fits.

Three models, one photo

from torchvision.models import resnet50, ResNet50_Weights
from torchvision.models.detection import fasterrcnn_resnet50_fpn_v2, FasterRCNN_ResNet50_FPN_V2_Weights
from torchvision.models.segmentation import deeplabv3_resnet50, DeepLabV3_ResNet50_Weights

img = torch.from_numpy(data.coffee()).permute(2, 0, 1)               # (3, 400, 600) uint8 RGB

Wc = ResNet50_Weights.IMAGENET1K_V2
clf = resnet50(weights=Wc).cuda().eval()
probs = clf(Wc.transforms()(img)[None].cuda()).softmax(-1)           # (1, 1000)

Wd = FasterRCNN_ResNet50_FPN_V2_Weights.COCO_V1
det = fasterrcnn_resnet50_fpn_v2(weights=Wd).cuda().eval()
out = det([img.float().cuda() / 255])[0]                            # dict: boxes (n, 4) x1,y1,x2,y2 in pixels; labels (n,); scores (n,)

Ws = DeepLabV3_ResNet50_Weights.COCO_WITH_VOC_LABELS_V1
seg = deeplabv3_resnet50(weights=Ws).cuda().eval()
mask = seg(Ws.transforms()(img)[None].cuda())["out"][0].argmax(0)     # (520, 780): a VOC class index per pixel

Notice that each model has its own preprocessing: the classifier resizes to 232 and crops 224; the detector takes the whole image at any size, as floats in 0–1, and normalises internally; the segmenter resizes the shorter side to 520. On scikit-image’s coffee photo the classifier said espresso (55.0%), the detector found a spoon (0.999), a cup (0.995), a dining table (0.985) and a second spoon, and the segmenter labelled 97.6% of the pixels diningtable. What comes back, and what it costs

The three tasks have very different costs. For one image on this GPU, the classifier took about 4 ms, the segmenter about 12–18 ms (it works at 520 px and produces an output for every pixel) and the detector about 26–33 ms, because Faster R-CNN proposes about a thousand candidate regions, classifies each one, refines its box and then removes duplicates. The detector also returned many more boxes than are drawn: on the French-horn photo, 30 boxes scored between 0.05 and 0.5, none above. Where to put the threshold is a product decision (P7), and Chapter 16 measures what it does to accuracy.

Where models fail

The most instructive outputs are the wrong ones. Each failure below comes from the same cause: the photo asks a question the model’s label set cannot answer, and the model answers anyway.

The Hubble image is the warning. Thousands of galaxies, and the classifier says “bubble” with 45.7% confidence, higher than its 39.8% for a real French horn. Softmax probabilities always sum to one across the known classes, so they measure which known class is least unlikely, not whether the image belongs to any of them. Detecting that an input is out of distribution is a separate problem (Chapter 9), and so is calibrating confidence so that 80% means right 80% of the time.

Use case: searching a photo library

Photo apps that let you search your own library for “dog”, “beach” or “receipt” run several of these tasks over every photo, usually on the phone itself when it is idle: a classifier for scene and object tags, a detector for faces and objects, OCR for text, and an embedding model so that free-text queries can match images (CLIP, Chapter 24). Each has the limits shown in this chapter: tags exist only for categories in the label set, confidences need thresholds tuned so that search results are not cluttered with false matches, and the cost per photo, multiplied by tens of thousands of photos, decides what can run on a battery.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Using a model whose label set does not contain your classes, and reading its nearest guess as an answer.
  • Reading softmax confidence as “how sure the model is that this is a known thing”. “Bubble” for a galaxy field scored 45.7%.
  • Giving each model the wrong preprocessing: detectors and segmenters in torchvision take different inputs from classifiers.
  • Choosing a detector for a task a classifier would do, at about 7 times the cost per image here.
  • Leaving the detector’s score threshold at a default without checking precision and recall on your own data.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is the difference between classification, detection and segmentation, and how would you choose between them?

How a strong answer sounds
Classification gives one label per image; detection gives a box, label and score for each object; semantic segmentation gives a label for every pixel, and instance segmentation separates individual objects within a class. They answer progressively more detailed questions at progressively higher cost: on this GPU a ResNet-50 classifier took about 4 ms per image, a DeepLabV3 segmenter about 12–18 ms and a Faster R-CNN detector about 30 ms. I choose the cheapest task that answers the product’s question: “is there a defect in this photo?” is classification; “where are the defects, and how many?” is detection; “how much of the leaf is diseased?” or anything that needs exact shapes is segmentation. Labels follow the same logic: boxes are much cheaper to annotate than masks, so the label budget often decides too.

Deeper: A pretrained ImageNet classifier labels a telescope image “bubble” with 46% confidence. What does that tell you, and what would you do about it in a product?

How a strong answer sounds
It tells you that softmax confidence is relative to the model’s own label set: the probabilities must sum to one over 1,000 ImageNet classes, so an image unlike any of them still gets a winner, and its score can be higher than for an in-distribution image (the French horn here got 40%). The model has no “none of these” option. In a product I would first make sure the label set covers what users send, measured on real traffic. Then I would add out-of-distribution handling: a confidence threshold tuned on held-out data including out-of-scope images, or better, a score designed for it (energy or maximum-logit scores, distance in embedding space to training data), and an explicit “unknown” path. I would also calibrate the probabilities, for example with temperature scaling (Chapter 9), so that thresholds mean what they say, and track the rate of low-confidence inputs as a monitoring signal for drift.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Classification answers “what”, detection “what and where”, segmentation “which pixels”; generation runs the other way.
  • A model can only answer with its own labels: ImageNet has 1,000 classes but no person, COCO has 80, VOC has 20.
  • Each model has its own preprocessing and postprocessing; the network is the middle of a pipeline.
  • Cost grows with the detail of the answer: about 4 ms to classify, 12–18 ms to segment, 26–33 ms to detect one image here.
  • Softmax confidence is not a measure of “known vs unknown”: a galaxy field became “bubble” at 45.7%.

Check your understanding

Which task outputs a class label for every pixel?

A semantic segmenter outputs an (H, W) map of class indices. Detection outputs boxes; classification one label per image.

Why did the ImageNet classifier call the astronaut photo “crash helmet”?

A classifier must pick one of its own labels. The detector, trained on COCO, found the person with 0.994.

Which of the three models was slowest per image on this GPU?

Faster R-CNN took about 26–33 ms: it scores many candidate regions, refines each box and removes duplicates. The classifier took about 4 ms.

The classifier gave “bubble” 45.7% for the Hubble deep field. What does that show?

Probabilities sum to 1 over known classes, so an unknown input still gets a winner, here with more confidence than a real French horn (39.8%).

The segmenter labelled the garbage-truck photo entirely as background. Why?

With no truck class, “background” was its best option. The COCO-trained detector, which has a truck class, found it with 0.999.
