CNN Architectures: VGG to ConvNeXt

Between 2014 and 2022 the best ImageNet CNN went from VGG-16, 138 million parameters and 71.6% top-1, to ConvNeXt-T, 29 million parameters and 82.5%. Each step introduced an idea that is still in use: 3 × 3 stacks, multi-branch modules, 1 × 1 bottlenecks, residual connections, depthwise convolutions, compound scaling, and transformer-era training recipes applied back to convolutions.

This chapter puts fourteen of them, all from torchvision, on the same bench: parameters and FLOPs counted, latency timed on this GPU at batch 1 and 64 and on the CPU, accuracy as published on ImageNet and as measured here on 1,963 Imagenette images. The rankings change depending on which column you sort by, which is the main lesson.

  • Name the key idea each major architecture introduced
  • Read parameters, FLOPs, latency and throughput as different costs
  • Explain why training recipes matter as much as architectures
  • Pick a backbone for a latency budget and a hardware target
  • Recognise what ConvNeXt borrowed from transformers

The accuracies marked * are torchvision’s retrained “V2” weights, which used a modern recipe (long schedules, TrivialAugment, Mixup, CutMix, label smoothing, EMA of weights); the others are the original recipes. That distinction matters: ResNet-50 with the original 2015 recipe scored 76.1%, with the V2 recipe 80.9%, 4.8 points from training alone, more than separates most of the architectures in the table. When a paper compares architectures, check that the recipes match.

VGG-16, with 31 GFLOPs and 138 million parameters, was one of the two fastest models at batch 1 on the GPU (1.09 ms): it has only 16 layers, each a big, regular 3 × 3 convolution that tensor cores run at full speed. At batch 64 it fell to second slowest, and on the CPU it was the slowest by far (42.5 ms), because then arithmetic dominates. DenseNet-121 had few parameters and middling FLOPs but the highest batch-1 latency, because its concatenations make many memory-bound copies. MobileNet v3 had the highest GPU throughput and near-best CPU latency, and the lowest accuracy but one. No single number ranks these models.

Reading the trade-offs

For any pair of columns, the models worth considering are on the Pareto front: no other model is both cheaper and more accurate. Against GPU latency at batch 1 and published ImageNet accuracy, the front has just three models: ResNet-18, VGG-16 and ConvNeXt-T, which gives 82.5% in 2.6 ms and beats every slower model. Against CPU latency the front is long and different: MobileNet v2 → v3 → EfficientNet-B0 → ResNet-50 → ResNeXt-50 → RegNetY → EfficientNet-B3 → ConvNeXt-T. Against GPU throughput at batch 64: MobileNet v3, EfficientNet-B0, ResNet-50, ResNeXt-50 and ConvNeXt-T. Against parameters (size on disk and in memory): MobileNet v2, EfficientNet-B0 and -B3, ConvNeXt-T. ConvNeXt-T is on all four fronts because it is the most accurate model in the set; the rest change with the cost that matters. A backbone is chosen for a deployment, not in general.

Imagenette’s ten easy classes compress the differences and reorder some (Inception v3 scored highest here; GoogLeNet lowest), which is a small reminder of Chapter 9: accuracy on someone else’s benchmark is a prior, and your own data decides. ConvNeXt’s lesson, finally, is about where gains came from: much of the transformers’ apparent advantage in 2020–2021 came from their training recipe and a few design choices, and a convolutional network given the same recipe and choices matched them. Chapter 20 measures the other side of that comparison.

🧾
Use case: “ResNet strikes back”

In 2021, Wightman, Touvron and Jégou retrained a plain ResNet-50 with a modern recipe (long schedules, strong augmentation, binary cross-entropy, LAMB, weight averaging) and reached about 80.4% ImageNet top-1, against 76.1% for the original recipe and in the range of newer architectures it was usually compared against. torchvision’s V2 weights used in this chapter (80.9%) came from a similar effort. The practical consequences: baselines in papers are often under-trained, so new architectures look better than they are; and for a real project, the cheapest accuracy gain is often a better training recipe for the backbone you already have.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Comparing architectures trained with different recipes: V1 vs V2 weights of ResNet-50 differ by 4.8 points.
  • Ranking by FLOPs or parameters when the constraint is latency: VGG-16 was among the fastest at batch 1 on the GPU.
  • Mixing up GFLOPs and GMACs between papers (a factor of 2).
  • Benchmarking at one batch size and deploying at another.
  • Feeding every model the same preprocessing: Inception v3 wants 299 px, EfficientNet-B3 300 px.
  • Choosing by ImageNet accuracy alone without fine-tuning the shortlist on your own data.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Name four ideas that moved CNN architectures forward between VGG and ConvNeXt.

How a strong answer sounds
VGG (2014): stacks of 3 × 3 convolutions, two 3 × 3s seeing what one 5 × 5 sees with fewer parameters. Inception (2014): 1 × 1 convolutions to cut channels before expensive filters, and parallel branches at several scales. ResNet (2015): residual connections, which made 50–150-layer networks trainable, plus 1 × 1 bottleneck blocks. MobileNet/EfficientNet (2017–2019): depthwise-separable convolutions and inverted residuals for efficiency, and compound scaling of depth, width and resolution together. ConvNeXt (2022): a ResNet modernised with transformer-era choices, large 7 × 7 depthwise kernels, LayerNorm, GELU, fewer normalisation and activation layers, and the AdamW training recipe, which reached 82.5% top-1 at ResNet-50-like cost. And I would add the non-architectural idea: modern training recipes, which alone took ResNet-50 from 76.1% to 80.9%.

Deeper: You need a backbone for a GPU service with a 5 ms budget at batch 1 and for a CPU-only edge box. What would you pick, and how?

How a strong answer sounds
Measure, then pick from the Pareto front for each target. On this RTX 5070 Ti at batch 1, ResNet-18 ran in 1.1 ms, ConvNeXt-T in 2.6 ms (82.5% ImageNet), ResNet-50 V2 in 2.7 ms (80.9%) and ResNeXt-50 in 3.4 ms (81.2%), all within 5 ms, so I would shortlist ConvNeXt-T and ResNet-50 and fine-tune both on our data. On the CPU the ranking changes: MobileNet v2 and v3 ran in 5–6 ms on 8 threads and EfficientNet-B0 in 6.9 ms with the best accuracy of the three, while ConvNeXt-T took 19.7 ms and VGG-16 42.5 ms; so EfficientNet-B0 or MobileNet v3, possibly quantised (Chapter 38). In both cases I would time with the real preprocessing and batch size on the real hardware, and check accuracy after fine-tuning rather than trusting ImageNet ranks.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Each architecture added a lasting idea: 3 × 3 stacks, 1 × 1 bottlenecks, residuals, depthwise convs, compound scaling, modernised ConvNets.
  • Training recipes matter as much: ResNet-50 went from 76.1% to 80.9% by retraining alone.
  • Parameters, FLOPs, batch-1 latency, throughput and CPU latency rank models differently: VGG-16 was fast on a GPU at batch 1 and slowest on the CPU.
  • ConvNeXt-T gave 82.5% in 2.6 ms here; MobileNet v3 led GPU throughput (14,695 img/s) and EfficientNet-B0 balanced CPU speed and accuracy.
  • Choose from the Pareto front measured on your target, then fine-tune the shortlist on your own data.

Check your understanding

How much did torchvision’s modern (V2) training recipe add to ResNet-50’s ImageNet top-1?

76.1% (V1) to 80.9% (V2), with the same architecture: more than separates most architectures in the table.

VGG-16 has 31 GFLOPs yet was among the fastest models on the GPU at batch 1. Why?

At batch 1 the GPU is underused, so layer count and regularity matter more than FLOPs. On the CPU it was the slowest (42.5 ms).

What is the Pareto front in a cost-versus-accuracy plot?

Anything off the front is dominated: some other model beats it on both axes, so it is never the right choice for that pair.

Which idea did ConvNeXt borrow from vision transformers?

It stays fully convolutional, but adopts transformer-era design choices and training, and reached 82.5% top-1.

Which family dominated CPU latency at batch 1 here?

5.4–6.9 ms on 8 threads, against 15.2 for ResNet-50 and 42.5 for VGG-16: on CPUs, FLOPs translate into time.
