What a CNN Sees: Feature Maps & Grad-CAM

A trained network is 25 million numbers that produce the right label most of the time. Visualisation is how you check that it produces it for the right reason: that “dog” comes from the dog and not the lawn, and “bird” from the bird and not a watermark in the corner.

This chapter opens a pretrained ResNet-50: its first-layer filters, the feature maps at each depth, and two ways to ask which pixels mattered, the gradient saliency map and Grad-CAM. Then it trains a CIFAR-10 model on data with a shortcut planted on purpose. The model learned the shortcut instead of birds, found 0% of real birds, and Grad-CAM pointed straight at the cause.

  • Read first-layer filters and feature maps at increasing depth
  • Compute a gradient saliency map and a Grad-CAM heat map
  • Explain what each method shows and what it cannot
  • Recognise shortcut learning, and test for it
  • Use visualisation as a debugging tool rather than a decoration

First-layer filters

ResNet-50’s first layer has 64 filters of 7 × 7 × 3. Because they act directly on RGB pixels, they can be drawn as tiny colour images (each scaled to the range of all the weights). Nobody designed them, yet most look like P5’s hand-made kernels: oriented edge detectors at many angles and frequencies (light on one side, dark on the other, like Sobel), colour-opponent blobs (red against green, blue against yellow) and a few smooth low-frequency patterns. The same set appears in almost every CNN trained on natural images, and in the visual cortex. Deeper filters cannot be drawn this way because they act on 64 or more abstract channels; for them we look at what they respond to instead.

Feature maps by depth

A feature map is one channel’s output over the image. Recording the outputs of ResNet-50’s four stages with forward hooks on the cat photo: layer1 (256 channels at 56 × 56) responds to edges and textures everywhere; layer2 (512 at 28 × 28) to texture regions and parts; layer3 (1,024 at 14 × 14) to larger parts such as eyes and ears; layer4 (2,048 at 7 × 7) to whole-object regions, with most channels silent except where the object is. Resolution falls and meaning rises, as Chapter 3’s shapes and Chapter 2’s receptive fields predict.

feats = {}
for name in ["layer1", "layer2", "layer3", "layer4"]:
    getattr(model, name).register_forward_hook(lambda mod, inp, out, n=name: feats.__setitem__(n, out))
logits = model(x)                        # x (1, 3, 224, 224); feats["layer4"] is (1, 2048, 7, 7)

Which pixels mattered: saliency and Grad-CAM

A saliency map is the gradient of the winning class score with respect to the input pixels: how much would the score change if this pixel changed? It is fine-grained but noisy, and it highlights pixels the score is sensitive to, which is not quite the same as pixels it relied on. Grad-CAM works at the last convolutional stage instead. It averages the gradient of the class score over each of layer4’s 2,048 channels to get one weight per channel, sums the channels’ feature maps with those weights, and keeps the positive part. The result is a coarse 7 × 7 map of where the evidence for that class came from, upsampled over the image.

A = feats["layer4"]; A.retain_grad()                       # (1, 2048, 7, 7)
logits[0, cls].backward()                                 # gradient of one class score
weights = A.grad.mean((2, 3), keepdim=True)                # (1, 2048, 1, 1): importance of each channel
cam = F.relu((weights * A).sum(1))[0]                     # (7, 7): weighted sum, positive evidence only
cam = cam / cam.max()                                       # 0..1, then upsample to 224 x 224 for display
saliency = x.grad[0].abs().max(0).values                     # (224, 224): needs x.requires_grad_(True)

Shortcut learning, planted and found

Models learn whatever cue predicts the label most easily, and they have no notion of which cues we meant. To show it cleanly: every one of the 5,000 training birds in CIFAR-10 got a 4 × 4 magenta square in its bottom-right corner, and nothing else changed. Then a ResNet-18 was trained for 15 epochs, and tested on normal, unstamped test images, and on test images with the square added.

The shortcut model learned “magenta corner means bird”, and nothing about birds at all: it found none of the 1,000 unstamped test birds, and labelled 81.9% of stamped frogs, trucks and ships as birds. Its overall accuracy, 80.67%, looks like an ordinary weaker model, which is the danger: an average does not reveal a shortcut. Grad-CAM does. On a stamped frog the model said “bird” with 100% confidence, and its heat map lit up the corner, not the frog.

Use case: the scanner tag in chest X-rays

Studies of deep-learning models for chest X-rays found that some had learned cues unrelated to disease: the hospital or scanner a film came from, recognisable from text markers and image characteristics, correlated with disease prevalence, so models used it. Saliency and Grad-CAM maps showed attention on corner tokens and borders instead of the lungs, and the models’ accuracy fell when tested at a hospital that was not in the training data. The cure was the one sketched above: find the cue, remove or balance it, and always evaluate on data from sites the model has never seen.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Treating a heat map as an explanation. It shows where evidence came from, not why; confirm with a controlled test.
  • Computing Grad-CAM at the wrong layer. Too early gives edges, not object evidence; the last convolutional stage is the usual choice.
  • Only visualising correct predictions. Errors and surprising successes are where shortcuts show.
  • Trusting an average to reveal shortcuts. The shortcut model scored a plausible 80.7%.
  • Evaluating only on data from the same sources as training, where the shortcut still works.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How does Grad-CAM work?

How a strong answer sounds
Take the feature maps of the last convolutional layer, which still have spatial layout, for example ResNet-50’s 2,048 channels at 7 × 7. Backpropagate the score of the class you are interested in to those maps, and average each channel’s gradient over the 7 × 7 positions: that is how much the class score depends on that channel. Multiply each feature map by its weight, sum over channels, and keep only positive values. The result is a 7 × 7 map of where in the image the evidence for that class was, which you upsample and overlay. It is coarse because the layer is coarse, it is class-specific (a different class gives a different map), and it needs no change to the model.

Deeper: How would you check whether a model has learned a shortcut?

How a strong answer sounds
Look for the cue, then test it. Grad-CAM and saliency on a sample of correct and incorrect predictions show whether evidence comes from the object or from borders, backgrounds, text or watermarks. Slicing accuracy by source (site, camera, photographer, time) shows whether performance depends on where an image came from. Then a controlled test: remove the suspected cue from test images, or add it to images of other classes, and measure. In this chapter, a model trained with a magenta square on every bird found 0% of unstamped birds and called 81.9% of stamped non-birds “bird”, while a clean model was unaffected by the square. Finally, the real test of any shortcut is evaluation on data where the cue does not hold: new sites, new sources.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • First-layer filters learn edges and colour blobs much like hand-made kernels.
  • Feature maps lose resolution and gain meaning with depth: edges at 56 × 56, objects at 7 × 7.
  • Saliency is the input gradient (fine, noisy); Grad-CAM weights the last stage’s maps by their gradients (coarse, class-specific).
  • Models learn the easiest cue: a corner square on training birds led to 0% of real birds found.
  • Averages hide shortcuts (80.7% looked normal); heat maps suggest them; controlled tests confirm them.

Check your understanding

Why can first-layer filters be drawn as images but deeper filters cannot?

A 7 × 7 × 3 filter is a tiny colour image. A 3 × 3 × 512 filter has no colour interpretation; we visualise what it responds to instead.

How does Grad-CAM weight each feature map of the last convolutional stage?

The spatially averaged gradient is the channel’s importance for that class; the weighted sum, kept positive, is the heat map.

Why is Grad-CAM on ResNet-50 coarse?

Each of the 49 cells covers a 32-pixel step of the 224 × 224 input (Chapter 2’s jump of 32).

After training with a magenta square on every bird, how many unstamped test birds did the model recognise?

0.0%: it had learned the square, not birds. Stamped non-birds were called “bird” 81.9% of the time.

The shortcut model scored 80.7% overall. Why is that dangerous?

Averages hide shortcuts. Per-class results, heat maps and tests that add or remove the cue reveal them.
