Building a CNN

A convolutional network is three operations repeated: a convolution that detects local patterns, a non-linearity that lets it combine them, and a downsampling step that trades spatial detail for more channels and a wider view. Stack a few of those blocks, add a small classifier on the end, and you have the architecture that ran computer vision for a decade.

This chapter builds one with 308,394 parameters and trains it on CIFAR-10 in 12 seconds, alongside multi-layer perceptrons on the same pixels, one with the same number of parameters and one 13.7 times larger. The CNN wins by 28 points. Then it shifts the test images a few pixels sideways to see where that advantage comes from.

Conv, ReLU, pool

Each block in this chapter is two 3 × 3 convolutions, each followed by batch normalisation (which keeps each channel’s activations at a steady scale so training is stable; Chapter 6 opens it up) and a ReLU (max(0, x): without a non-linearity, stacked convolutions would collapse into one linear filter), then a 2 × 2 max pool, which keeps the largest value in each 2 × 2 window and halves the height and width. Each block doubles the number of channels, so the network trades “where” for “what” as it goes deeper.

def block(c_in, c_out):
    return [nn.Conv2d(c_in, c_out, 3, padding=1), nn.BatchNorm2d(c_out), nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Conv2d(c_out, c_out, 3, padding=1), nn.BatchNorm2d(c_out), nn.ReLU(),
            nn.MaxPool2d(2)]                                  # halves H and W

cnn = nn.Sequential(
    *block(3, 32),                                         # (N, 3, 32, 32)  -> (N, 32, 16, 16)
    *block(32, 64),                                        #                 -> (N, 64, 8, 8)
    *block(64, 128),                                       #                 -> (N, 128, 4, 4)
    nn.Flatten(),                                           #                 -> (N, 2048)
    nn.Linear(128 * 4 * 4, 10))                              #                 -> (N, 10) logits
sum(p.numel() for p in cnn.parameters())                # 308,394

Most of the parameters sit in the last block (its two convolutions and batch norms: 221,952) and the head (20,490); the first block has only 10,272. Most of the compute is the reverse: the first block runs at full 32 × 32 resolution. That inversion, few weights doing a lot of arithmetic early and many weights doing less late, holds for nearly every CNN.

With the same parameter budget the CNN scored 88.0% against 54.7%, and giving the MLP 13.7 times more parameters only raised it to 59.7%. The MLP is not short of capacity: without augmentation it reached 99.4% training accuracy, memorising the pixels, while generalising to 58.6%. What it lacks is the right assumptions. A dense layer treats pixel (3, 7) and pixel (3, 8) as unrelated inputs and must learn every pattern separately at every position. A convolution assumes that nearby pixels go together (locality) and that a pattern means the same thing wherever it appears (weight sharing), and both assumptions are true of photos.

The pooling choices barely mattered: max pool, average pool and strided convolutions all landed between 88.0% and 88.9% (single runs; differences under a point are within seed noise for these settings). The head mattered little too. Global average pooling (average each of the 128 final channels over the 4 × 4 grid, then one linear layer) did as well as flattening with 19,200 fewer parameters, and it has a bigger advantage: it accepts any input size, which is why ResNet and almost every modern CNN end with it.

Equivariance, invariance and the shift test

A convolution is translation equivariant: shift the input and the output shifts by the same amount. Checked directly on the trained CNN’s first layer, shifting the input 3 pixels and then comparing with the shifted output gave a maximum difference of 0.0 away from the borders. Equivariance is not the same as invariance, the property that the answer does not change. Pooling and the classifier head provide some invariance, but only some: max pooling over 2 × 2 tolerates a shift of a pixel within each window, and three of them tolerate a few pixels in total.

A one-pixel shift cost the CNN 1.2 points and the MLP 3.3; at eight pixels (a quarter of the image) the CNN with global pooling kept 78% of its accuracy and the MLP 47%. The global-pooling CNN degraded least because its head does not care where in the 4 × 4 grid a feature fired. Neither CNN is fully invariant: large shifts move objects partly out of frame, the zero-filled strip is something it never saw, and the flatten head ties each weight to a position. This is exactly why the training runs above used random crops: they teach the invariance the architecture only partly provides (Chapter 5).

Use case: LeNet reading cheques

The pattern in this chapter, convolution, non-linearity and subsampling repeated, then a small classifier, is Yann LeCun’s LeNet from the late 1980s and 1990s. LeNet-5, with about 60,000 parameters, was deployed by AT&T and NCR in cheque-reading systems that by the late 1990s were reading a significant share of the cheques written in the United States. The design ideas were the same ones measured here: shared weights so a digit is recognised wherever it is written, local receptive fields, and pooling for tolerance to small shifts and distortions. What changed between LeNet and AlexNet two decades later was mostly scale: data, depth, ReLUs and GPUs (Case Study 41).

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Forgetting the non-linearity between convolutions, so the stack is one linear filter.
  • Getting the flatten size wrong (128 × 4 × 4 = 2,048 here) and only finding out at the first forward pass; global average pooling avoids the question.
  • Assuming a CNN is shift-invariant. An 8-pixel shift still cost it 18–23 points without augmentation.
  • Comparing architectures with different parameter counts and calling it a fair test. Match budgets, or show the curve.
  • Reading a sub-1-point difference from one run as a real effect, such as max vs average pooling here.
  • Putting all the channels early, where resolution is high and every channel is expensive.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Why does a CNN with 300,000 parameters beat an MLP with 4 million on CIFAR-10?

How a strong answer sounds
Because its structure matches images. A convolution looks only at a small neighbourhood (locality) and uses the same weights at every position (weight sharing), so an edge or texture detector learned in one place works everywhere, and the network spends its parameters on what patterns exist rather than on where. An MLP treats every pixel as an independent input: it must learn each pattern separately at every position, and it has no notion that neighbouring pixels are related. Measured here, a 308k-parameter CNN reached 88.0% while a 4.2M-parameter MLP reached 59.7%, and the MLP could memorise the training set (99.4% train accuracy without augmentation), so the gap is generalisation, not capacity. The same structure also makes the CNN degrade more gracefully when the image shifts.

Deeper: What is the difference between translation equivariance and invariance, and which parts of a CNN provide each?

How a strong answer sounds
Equivariance means shifting the input shifts the output by the same amount; invariance means the output does not change at all. Convolutions with stride 1 are equivariant (the first layer here matched to 0.0 under a 3-pixel shift, away from the borders). Pooling, striding and the head convert that into partial invariance: a 2 × 2 max pool ignores where in its window the maximum was, and global average pooling ignores where in the final map a feature fired. It is only partial: striding breaks exact equivariance (a 1-pixel shift of the input is half a pixel at stride 2, which aliases), zero padding gives the network position information at the borders, and a flatten head ties weights to positions. Measured, an 8-pixel shift cost the flatten-head CNN 23 points and the global-pooling one 19. Data augmentation with random crops is what teaches the rest of the invariance.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • A CNN repeats conv → (BN) → ReLU and downsamples between blocks: spatial size falls while channels rise.
  • Parameters gather in the late, wide layers; compute in the early, high-resolution ones.
  • At equal parameters the CNN scored 88.0% against 54.7% for an MLP; 13.7× more parameters lifted the MLP only to 59.7%.
  • Max pool, average pool and strided convs gave 88.0–88.9%; global average pooling matched flatten with fewer parameters and any input size.
  • Convolutions are equivariant; pooling and the head add only partial invariance. Random crops supply the rest.

Check your understanding

A (N, 32, 32, 32) tensor goes through a 2 × 2 max pool. What comes out?

Pooling halves height and width and leaves the channel count alone; the convolutions change the channels.

Without augmentation the 4.2M-parameter MLP reached 99.4% training accuracy and 58.6% test accuracy. What does that show?

Capacity is not the problem; the inductive bias is. The 308k-parameter CNN generalised to 85.8% on the same unaugmented data.

What is the main practical advantage of a global average pooling head over flatten + linear?

Averaging each channel over the whole map gives a fixed-length vector whatever the image size. Here it matched flatten (87.6% vs 88.0%) with 19,200 fewer parameters.
