Debugging Vision Pipelines & Datasets

Most failures of deployed vision systems are not exotic. A colour channel swapped by a decoding library, normalisation statistics copied from the wrong model, augmentation that was meant for training only, a model left in training mode, labels that are wrong, test images that leaked into training. None of them crash. They quietly cost accuracy, or worse, they inflate it in testing and fail in production.

This chapter plants each of those bugs on purpose in a small, known-good CIFAR-10 pipeline and measures what each one does, so that you can recognise the symptom when you meet it. It also shows the cheapest way to find mislabelled images: ask the model which ones it finds hardest. The damage varied enormously. No normalisation cut accuracy from 88.7% to 33%; swapped colour channels and a lower resolution each cost 13–17 points; using ImageNet’s normalisation statistics instead of CIFAR’s cost only 0.7. Leaked test images inflated their own accuracy by 4 points. And ranking training images by loss found the planted wrong labels with 100% precision in the top 500.

  • Recognise the symptoms of preprocessing mismatches
  • Measure the effect of label noise, and find noisy labels
  • Detect train–test leakage and near-duplicates
  • Spot class imbalance hiding behind overall accuracy
  • Work through a debugging checklist systematically

The known-good pipeline

The reference is deliberately simple: a small CNN (Chapter 3’s, 64 base channels) trained for 15 epochs on CIFAR-10 with random crops and flips, normalised with CIFAR’s own per-channel mean and standard deviation. It reached 88.67% on the 10,000 test images. Every bug below changes one thing.

Bugs at test time

The first group leaves training untouched and changes only how the trained model is used, the situation of a model handed from a research notebook to a serving team.

Two bugs were catastrophic, two serious and the rest mild, and the mild ones are the dangerous kind. Skipping normalisation shifted every input by about two standard deviations and cut accuracy to a third; nobody ships that for long. Swapping red and blue cost 13 points on CIFAR-10, a large loss that still leaves a plausible-looking 76%. A lower-resolution source cost 17 points. The surprises are at the bottom of the table. ImageNet’s statistics are close enough to CIFAR’s that using the wrong ones cost 0.7 points; augmentation left on cost 0.8; and train mode cost only 0.1 at batch 1,000, because a large batch’s own statistics are close to the running averages, but 1.8 at batch 16, and it would be far worse at batch 1. Losses of half a point to two points are exactly the size that looks like noise and goes unnoticed, which is why the checklist starts with plotting the tensors rather than waiting for accuracy to complain.

    Label noise hurt less than you might expect: with 20% of labels randomised, accuracy fell only 3 points, because in 15 epochs the network learns the consistent majority before it has time to memorise the noise (longer training makes noisy-label damage grow). Channel order is not wrong in itself: a model trained and tested on BGR was as good as the RGB baseline; what costs 11.5 points is the mismatch. Leakage inflated the reported score by 0.9 points over the honest clean-set figure, and the leaked images themselves scored 94.1%, 4.4 points above clean ones, because the model had trained on them; with longer training or a bigger model, memorisation, and the inflation, grow. Imbalance hid well: removing nine tenths of the cats cost 3.9 points overall, but the model recognised only 26% of test cats and predicted “cat” 287 times for 1,000 real cats. Overall accuracy would never have revealed that.

    Finding mislabelled images

    Networks learn clean, consistent labels before they memorise noisy ones, so after training, examples with a high loss on their own training label are disproportionately mislabelled. Ranking the training set by per-example loss and reviewing the top of the list is the cheapest data-cleaning tool there is; confident learning (cleanlab) refines it with out-of-fold predictions and per-class thresholds.

    Training images reviewed (by highest loss on their own label)Share actually mislabelled
    A random sample (the base rate)17.9%
    Top 500100.0%
    Top 2,00099.8%
    Top 5,000 (10% of the set)98.4%

    With 20% of labels randomised (17.9% actually wrong, since a random label is sometimes the right one), every one of the 500 highest-loss training images was mislabelled, and 98.4% of the top 5,000. Reviewing that top 10% of the dataset would have found 54.9% of all the wrong labels; the remainder are mislabelled images that the network has partly memorised, so their loss is no longer high, which is why cross-validated scoring (each image scored by a model that did not train on it) finds more. As a data-cleaning tool for the cost of one training run, it is hard to beat.

    A debugging checklist

    When accuracy is lower than expected, or higher than seems possible, work through the pipeline in order: the data, the inputs as the model actually sees them, the model’s mode and outputs, and the evaluation. The panel’s last view is a checklist you can tick as you go; the items are the bugs measured above plus the ones that appear across this course.

    🧾
    Use case: the model that was 99% accurate

    A team reports 99% accuracy detecting a disease from photos, far above clinicians. Debugging it follows this chapter. Are patients split between training and test, or do photos of the same patient appear in both (leakage, measured below inflating accuracy on the leaked images)? Do positive images come from one hospital’s camera and negatives from another (a shortcut the model can learn from colour statistics)? Are images resized, colour-ordered and normalised the same way in the app as in training (the test-time bugs)? Is the rare class’s recall reported, not just accuracy (imbalance)? Each of these has been found behind published medical-imaging results that did not replicate.

    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Never looking at the tensors the model receives: plot a batch after preprocessing, at both training and serving time.
    • Trusting a result that is too good: leaked test images scored 94.1% against 89.7% for clean ones; the full test score overstated real performance.
    • Splitting by image when the unit is a person, a video or a scene: near-duplicates cross the split.
    • Reporting overall accuracy on imbalanced data: with 10% of the cats, overall accuracy fell only 3.9 points while cat recall fell to 26%.
    • Fixing a symptom in the model (more epochs, bigger network) when the bug is in the data.
    🎤
    Interview drill

    Explain it simply: Your model’s accuracy dropped sharply after deployment. Where do you look first?

    How a strong answer sounds
    At the inputs, before the model. I would log a sample of production tensors exactly as they enter the model and compare them with training tensors: colour order (OpenCV decodes BGR), value range and normalisation statistics, resize method and resolution, crop, and whether training-only augmentation is still on. Then check the model is in eval mode with the right weights loaded, and that the label mapping is the same. Only after those would I look at genuine distribution shift in the images. In a planted-bug experiment on CIFAR-10, swapping RGB to BGR at test time cost 13 points and dropping normalisation cost 55, while using the wrong normalisation statistics cost under 1: the small ones are the ones that slip through, so I compare tensors directly rather than wait for accuracy to drop.

    Deeper: How would you find and handle label noise in a large dataset?

    How a strong answer sounds
    Train a model, ideally with cross-validation so every example is scored by a model that did not train on it, and rank examples by their loss or by the gap between the given label’s probability and the top prediction; review the top of the list, which is rich in errors, and fix or remove those labels; repeat. Confident learning formalises the thresholds per class. During training, label smoothing, early stopping and robust losses reduce the damage of the noise that remains. Also check that the test set is clean, because label noise there caps measured accuracy. In my experiment, ranking a 20%-noisy training set by loss after one training run, the top 500 were 100% mislabelled and the top 10% contained 55% of all errors.

    🧠 Key takeaways

    • Most pipeline bugs do not crash; they cost or inflate accuracy silently.
    • Measured on CIFAR-10: no normalisation −55 points, lower resolution −17, BGR −13; wrong normalisation statistics −0.7, augmentation left on −0.8.
    • Leaked test images scored 94.1% vs 89.7% clean; removing 90% of the cats cost 3.9 points overall but cat recall fell to 26%.
    • Sorting training images by loss: the top 500 were 100% mislabelled.
    • Debug in order: data, inputs as the model sees them, model mode, evaluation.

    Check your understanding

    You decode images with OpenCV and feed them to a model trained on RGB. What happens?

    OpenCV returns BGR; nothing checks it, so the cost only shows in accuracy.

    Why does leaving a BatchNorm model in train mode at inference matter more with small batches?

    At batch 1 the statistics are a single image’s; predictions then depend on what else is in the batch.

    What does sorting training examples by their loss help you find?

    Networks fit clean labels first; wrong labels stay hard.
