Diffusion Models from Scratch

Diffusion models generate an image by starting from pure noise and removing a little of it, a thousand times. Training is the reverse and is surprisingly simple: take a real image, add a random amount of noise, and train a network to predict the noise that was added. That single regression loss, with no adversary and no blurry pixel target, is behind DALL·E 2, Imagen, Stable Diffusion and most image and video generators since 2022.

This chapter writes a DDPM (denoising diffusion probabilistic model) from scratch, about 120 lines with its U-Net, and trains it on CIFAR-10 on this GPU. Forty-five minutes of training on one GPU produced recognisable horses, trucks, ships and birds from pure noise. The 1,000-step sampler took 204 ms per image; DDIM, using the same network, took 20 ms with 100 steps and 2 ms with 10, with softer but still coherent images.

The forward process: destroying an image slowly

Fix a noise schedule of T = 1,000 small variances β1 … βT, rising linearly from 0.0001 to 0.02. Each step shrinks the image slightly and adds a little Gaussian noise: xt = √(1 − βt) xt−1 + √βt ε. After enough steps nothing of the image is left: xT is indistinguishable from N(0, 1). Because Gaussians compose, you do not need to run the chain to get xt. With αt = 1 − βt and ᾱt the product of α1 … αt:

xt = √ᾱt · x0 + √(1 − ᾱt) · ε, ε ~ N(0, I)

ᾱt is the share of the original signal’s variance that survives to step t. It falls from almost 1 to almost 0 across the schedule; the panel plots it, and shows six test images at seven points along the way.

The loss: predict the noise

Generation needs the reverse step, from xt to xt−1. For small βt that reverse step is also close to Gaussian, and its mean can be computed if you know the noise ε that was mixed in. So the network εθ(xt, t) is trained to predict ε from the noisy image and the step number. The DDPM paper (2020) showed that a much-simplified version of the variational bound works best in practice: plain mean squared error between the true and predicted noise, at a timestep drawn uniformly at random.

betas = torch.linspace(1e-4, 0.02, 1000); alphas = 1 - betas; abar = torch.cumprod(alphas, 0)
for step in range(40_000):
    x0 = batch()                                                  # real images scaled to [-1, 1]
    t = torch.randint(0, 1000, (len(x0),))                         # a random noise level per image
    eps = torch.randn_like(x0)
    xt = abar[t].sqrt()[:, None, None, None] * x0 + (1 - abar[t]).sqrt()[:, None, None, None] * eps
    loss = F.mse_loss(model(xt, t), eps)                               # that is the whole objective
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step(); ema_update()

Two details matter in practice. The network is evaluated through an exponential moving average of its weights (decay 0.9995 here), which gives noticeably cleaner samples than the raw weights. And the loss is an average over very different tasks: at small t the noise is a faint speckle on an almost clean image, at large t the image is a faint ghost in noise. The measured loss by noise level below shows how different they are.

The denoiser: a U-Net that knows the time

The network has to output an image-sized prediction from an image-sized input, which is the U-Net shape of Chapter 17. Two additions make it a diffusion model. The timestep t is turned into a sinusoidal embedding (as for transformer positions), passed through a small MLP and added into every residual block, so the same weights behave differently at each noise level. And self-attention layers at the 16 × 16 and 8 × 8 resolutions let distant parts of the image agree with each other. GroupNorm replaces BatchNorm because each image in a batch is at a different noise level. The one trained here has 7.3 million parameters.

Sampling: walking back from noise

The DDPM sampler starts from xT ~ N(0, I) and applies the learned reverse step 1,000 times: subtract the predicted noise, rescale, and add a little fresh noise (except at the last step). It is faithful to the training process and slow: 1,000 network evaluations per image.

DDIM (2020) uses the same trained network with a different, deterministic update that can jump many timesteps at once. At each step it uses the predicted noise to estimate the clean image x̂0, then re-noises that estimate to the next, much lower, noise level. No retraining is needed; only the number of network calls changes.

def ddim(model, n, steps):
    ts = torch.linspace(999, 0, steps).long(); x = torch.randn(n, 3, 32, 32)
    for k, t in enumerate(ts):
        eps = model(x, t.expand(n))
        x0 = ((x - (1 - abar[t]).sqrt() * eps) / abar[t].sqrt()).clamp(-1, 1)       # current guess of the clean image
        a_next = abar[ts[k + 1]] if k + 1 < steps else torch.tensor(1.0)
        x = a_next.sqrt() * x0 + (1 - a_next).sqrt() * eps                          # jump to the next noise level
    return x

Measured

The model trained for 40,000 steps of 128 images, 45 minutes. The training loss is a poor progress bar: it fell only from about 0.039 to about 0.03 and jumps around from batch to batch, because each batch mixes random noise levels, while the samples changed completely. At 10,000 steps the DDIM samples had the colours and rough layouts of scenes; at 40,000 they contained recognisable objects. (The 2,000-step snapshot is still noise partly because the EMA weights, with decay 0.9995, still carry about a third of the random initialisation at that point.)

Predicting the noise is hardest when there is almost none: at t = 0 the added noise is a tiny speckle on a clean image, and telling it apart from the image’s own fine texture is nearly impossible. At t = 999 the noisy image is almost entirely noise, so predicting ε is trivial. The hard part of generation, inventing the content, happens at high noise levels where the loss looks small, because small errors in ε there are multiplied up when the clean image is estimated.

Noise schedules

The linear schedule from the DDPM paper destroys information quickly: in the panel’s plot, ᾱt is already below 0.5 by about a quarter of the way through, and the last few hundred steps are spent on images that are almost pure noise. The cosine schedule (Improved DDPM, 2021) makes ᾱt fall along a cosine curve, so information is removed more evenly and fewer steps are wasted at either end; it helps most at low resolution, such as CIFAR’s 32 × 32. In the panel’s curves, the linear schedule’s ᾱt crosses 0.5 at t ≈ 260 and is down to 0.08 by t = 500, so more than half of the 1,000 steps are spent on images with less than 8% of their signal left; the cosine schedule crosses 0.5 only at t = 500. Later work goes further and treats the noise level as continuous (EDM, flow matching), which is how most current models are trained; the ingredients, a noise-conditioned network trained by regression to undo noise, are the ones built here.

Use case: generation costs are step counts

A production image generator’s cost per image is roughly (number of denoising steps) × (cost of one network evaluation), so most of the engineering since 2020 has gone into cutting the first factor. Fast samplers (DDIM, DPM-Solver) brought 1,000 steps down to 20–50 with little quality loss; distillation methods (progressive distillation, consistency models, adversarial distillation) bring it to 1–4 steps for real-time use. This chapter’s sampler measurement is the same trade-off in miniature: every halving of the steps halves the GPU time, and the question is always how much quality is lost, which Chapter 30 measures with FID on these very samples.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Sampling from the raw weights instead of the EMA: noisier, less coherent samples.
  • Images in [0, 1] instead of [−1, 1]: the forward process assumes data roughly centred with unit scale like the noise.
  • BatchNorm in the denoiser: every image is at a different noise level; use GroupNorm.
  • Forgetting the time embedding or adding it only at the input: the network cannot tell how much noise to remove.
  • Judging training by the loss value: here it moved only from about 0.039 to 0.03 while samples went from noise to objects, and it is largest at the low noise levels that matter least for content. Look at samples, and measure them (Chapter 30).

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How is a diffusion model trained, and how does it generate?

How a strong answer sounds
Training: take a real image, pick a random timestep t, mix in Gaussian noise with the closed form xt = √ᾱt x0 + √(1 − ᾱt) ε, and train a U-Net that is told t to predict ε with mean squared error. That is the whole loss: no adversary, and the target is the noise, not a blurry image. Generation: start from pure noise and repeatedly use the network’s noise prediction to step to a slightly cleaner image, 1,000 steps with the DDPM sampler or 20–100 with DDIM, which jumps between noise levels deterministically using the same network. I trained one from scratch on CIFAR-10, a 7.3 M-parameter U-Net for 40,000 steps in 45 minutes, and it produced recognisable objects; the 1,000-step sampler took 204 ms per image and 10-step DDIM 2 ms with the same weights.

Deeper: Why do diffusion models produce sharper samples than VAEs, and what do they pay for it?

How a strong answer sounds
A VAE decodes a whole image in one step under pixel error, so uncertainty becomes averaging and blur. A diffusion model splits generation into many small denoising steps; each step only has to resolve a little uncertainty, and the randomness injected along the way commits to one plausible image rather than averaging all of them. Training is a stable regression, unlike a GAN’s game, and it covers the data distribution well. The price is inference cost: many network evaluations per image. In my CIFAR-10 model, 1,000 DDPM steps cost 204 ms per image against 2 ms for 10 DDIM steps from the same network, a 100-fold difference for somewhat softer images. Hence fast samplers, distillation, and latent diffusion (Chapter 29), which runs the process on a small compressed representation.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • The forward process mixes an image with noise in closed form: xt = √ᾱt x0 + √(1 − ᾱt) ε.
  • Training is MSE between the added noise and a time-conditioned U-Net’s prediction of it.
  • Trained here: 7.3 M parameters, 40,000 steps, 45 minutes, recognisable CIFAR-10 objects from noise.
  • Sampling cost is network calls: 204 ms per image with 1,000 DDPM steps, 2 ms with 10 DDIM steps.
  • The cosine schedule removes information more evenly than the linear one.

Check your understanding

What does the network in a DDPM predict during training?

ε-prediction with MSE; the timestep is given to the network, not predicted.

Why can training jump straight to any timestep instead of running the noising chain?

The closed form means one random t and one noise draw per image per step.

How is the timestep given to the denoising U-Net?

Every block needs to know how much noise to expect.

What does DDIM change compared with DDPM sampling?

Estimate x̂0 from the predicted noise, then jump to a much lower noise level.

Why is GroupNorm used instead of BatchNorm in the denoiser?

Per-sample normalisation keeps each image’s statistics separate.
