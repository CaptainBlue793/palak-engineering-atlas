Deploying Vision Models

A model that scores well in a notebook still has to answer within a latency budget, fit the memory of the device it runs on, and cost little enough per request to be worth running. The tools are well known: lower precision, quantisation, compilation, exporting to an optimised runtime, pruning, batching. What is less well known is how much each one actually buys on real hardware, and how often the model is not where the time goes.

This chapter measures them on two ImageNet classifiers, ResNet-50 and MobileNetV3-Large, with accuracy checked on Imagenette after every change, on the RTX 5070 Ti and on the CPU of the same machine. Some results were expected: bf16 halved ResNet-50’s batch time on the GPU, int8 quantisation made it 3.1 times faster on the CPU at no cost in accuracy, and pruning 50% of its weights saved no time at all. Some were not: at batch 1, bf16 was slower than fp32, int8 cost MobileNetV3 5.4 points of accuracy, and ONNX Runtime ran MobileNetV3 4.6 times faster than PyTorch on the same CPU.

  • Set a latency budget and measure latency properly
  • Use reduced precision and post-training int8 quantisation
  • Know when pruning saves time and when it does not
  • Export to ONNX and verify the exported model
  • Find where end-to-end latency goes, including preprocessing

Latency budgets

Start from the requirement, not the model. A camera at 30 frames per second leaves 33 ms per frame for everything; an interactive web request might allow 100–200 ms end to end; a nightly batch job cares about images per second per pound, not latency. Measure the right thing: batch-1 latency for interactive use, throughput at the best batch size for offline work, and always the tail (95th or 99th percentile), because users notice the slow requests. On a GPU, time with CUDA events or synchronise before reading the clock (P8); warm up first, because the first calls pay for kernel selection and memory allocation.

Precision: fp32, bf16, fp16, int8

Inference rarely needs 32-bit floats. bf16 and fp16 halve memory traffic and run on tensor cores; bf16 has fp32’s range and is the safe default on recent GPUs, fp16 more precision but less range. int8 quantisation goes further: weights and activations are stored as 8-bit integers with a scale per tensor or per channel, cutting size four times and using integer arithmetic. Post-training static quantisation needs a small calibration set to record activation ranges; quantisation-aware training simulates rounding during training when PTQ loses too much.

from torch.ao.quantization import get_default_qconfig_mapping
from torch.ao.quantization.quantize_fx import prepare_fx, convert_fx
qm = prepare_fx(model.eval(), get_default_qconfig_mapping("x86"), example_inputs)   # insert observers
for x in calibration_images: qm(x)                                            # 100 images: record activation ranges
q = convert_fx(qm)                                                              # int8 weights and activations

At batch 64, where the GPU is busy, bf16 halved ResNet-50’s time (34.2 to 16.3 ms) and compiling took it to 11.4 ms, three times fp32, with no change in accuracy beyond a few images. At batch 1 the picture inverted: bf16 took 6.97 ms against fp32’s 2.49, because a single small image does not keep the GPU busy, and the extra work of casting tensors at every layer, issued from Python, costs more than the faster arithmetic saves. (A separate end-to-end measurement below timed the same bf16 model at 3.5 ms; batch-1 times of a few milliseconds are sensitive to launch overhead and clocks, which is one more reason to measure on the real path.) torch.compile fixes both cases by fusing operations and cutting the Python overhead, at the price of 38–60 seconds of compilation. On the CPU, int8 made ResNet-50 3.1 times faster and four times smaller with no loss, but cost MobileNetV3 5.4 points while saving only a quarter of its time: depthwise convolutions and hard-swish activations quantise poorly per tensor, the classic weakness the interview drill below discusses. Exporting to ONNX Runtime gave both models a large CPU speed-up with outputs matching PyTorch to 4–9 × 10−6.

Pruning

Pruning removes weights that matter little, typically the smallest in magnitude. Unstructured pruning zeroes individual weights anywhere; structured pruning removes whole channels or filters, which actually shrinks the matrices. The distinction decides whether anything gets faster.

GPU time, batch 64, bf1616.36 ms16.33 ms16.35 ms16.33 ms16.36 ms

A third of the weights could be zeroed with no loss at all, a measure of how over-parameterised ResNet-50 is; at half, accuracy fell 3 points, at 70% by 20, and at 90% the network was destroyed. Fine-tuning after pruning recovers much of the loss at moderate levels. And the time did not change by a hundredth of a millisecond at any level: the zeros are scattered through dense weight tensors, and dense GPU kernels multiply them anyway. Unstructured sparsity saves time only with sparse kernels and hardware support (such as NVIDIA’s 2:4 structured sparsity); structured pruning, which removes whole channels, makes the network genuinely smaller and faster.

ONNX and inference runtimes

Exporting to ONNX freezes the model into a framework-neutral graph that optimised runtimes can run: ONNX Runtime on servers and phones, TensorRT on NVIDIA GPUs (with layer fusion and int8 calibration of its own), OpenVINO on Intel, Core ML on Apple, TFLite and ExecuTorch on mobile. Always verify the export: run the same input through both and compare outputs, because unsupported operators, dynamic shapes and preprocessing differences are common sources of silent errors. Here the exported models matched PyTorch’s outputs to within 4 × 10−6 (ResNet-50) and 9 × 10−6 (MobileNetV3), with identical Imagenette accuracy, and ran 2.3 and 4.6 times faster on the CPU at batch 1, from graph optimisation and fused kernels alone.

On this machine the model was three quarters of the time and the CPU-side preparation a fifth. That share is not fixed: these Imagenette JPEGs are only 320 pixels on the short side. A 12-megapixel phone photo takes tens of milliseconds to decode and resize on a CPU, and on an embedded board with a weak CPU and a fast accelerator the preprocessing routinely becomes the larger part. Moving decode and resize to the GPU (nvJPEG, DALI, torchvision’s GPU decoder) or decoding at reduced resolution are then the biggest wins available.

Use case: a model on a factory camera

An inspection camera on a production line takes a photo every 40 ms and must pass or reject each part before the next arrives, on an embedded board with no data-centre GPU. A typical path is a small architecture (Chapter 12), trained normally, quantised to int8 with a few hundred real line images for calibration, exported to the board’s runtime, and benchmarked end to end on the board itself, including image capture and preprocessing, which on small CPUs can take longer than the network. The acceptance test is the defect recall measured after quantisation, on held-out line images, not the original model’s accuracy.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Timing GPU code without synchronising or without warm-up (P8).
  • Expecting unstructured pruning to speed things up: zeroing 30–90% of ResNet-50’s weights changed its GPU time by less than 0.03 ms.
  • Not re-checking accuracy after every optimisation, especially quantisation and export.
  • Optimising the model when preprocessing dominates: measure first; here the model was 76% of a request, but with large photos or a weak CPU, decoding and resizing can dominate.
  • Calibrating int8 on unrepresentative images (wrong lighting, wrong camera): activation ranges will be wrong in production.
  • Different preprocessing in training and serving (resize method, colour order): the most common silent accuracy loss (Chapter 39).

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How would you make an image classifier fast enough for a 30 fps camera?

How a strong answer sounds
First measure end to end on the target hardware at batch 1, including capture, decoding and preprocessing, with warm-up and proper synchronisation, and look at the tail. If the model dominates: run in bf16 or fp16 on a GPU, or int8 with post-training quantisation calibrated on real frames on a CPU or edge accelerator; compile or export to an optimised runtime and verify outputs match; and if that is not enough, switch to a smaller architecture rather than pruning unstructured weights, which rarely speeds up dense hardware. If preprocessing dominates, move resize and normalisation to the GPU or into the runtime, decode at lower resolution, and avoid copies. Re-check accuracy after every step. On an RTX 5070 Ti, compiling ResNet-50 in bf16 took batch-64 time from 34 to 11 ms, but plain bf16 autocast was slower than fp32 at batch 1; on the CPU, int8 was 3.1× faster with no accuracy loss for ResNet-50, and ONNX Runtime 4.6× faster for MobileNetV3.

Deeper: When does int8 quantisation hurt accuracy, and what do you do about it?

How a strong answer sounds
It hurts when activation or weight distributions have outliers, so a single scale wastes most of the 256 levels on rare values; depthwise convolutions and small mobile models with per-tensor quantisation are classic victims, as are layers like the first convolution and the classifier. Fixes, in order: per-channel weight quantisation, better calibration data and methods (percentile or entropy rather than min/max), leaving sensitive layers in higher precision, and quantisation-aware training, which fine-tunes with simulated rounding. Always measure on representative data, per class if the task is imbalanced. I saw exactly this: post-training int8 kept ResNet-50’s Imagenette accuracy (87.0 to 87.1%) but cost MobileNetV3-Large 5.4 points (80.6 to 75.2%), which is where I would switch to per-channel weights, better calibration, or quantisation-aware training.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Start from a latency budget; measure batch-1 latency, throughput and tails, synchronised and warmed up.
  • On the GPU: bf16 halved ResNet-50’s batch-64 time and torch.compile tripled throughput, but bf16 without compile was slower at batch 1.
  • On the CPU: int8 PTQ made ResNet-50 3.1× faster and 4× smaller at no cost, but cost MobileNetV3 5.4 points; ONNX Runtime gave 2.3–4.6×.
  • Unstructured pruning: up to 30% free in accuracy, zero speed-up at any level.
  • Verify every export and re-measure accuracy after every optimisation.

Check your understanding

What does post-training static int8 quantisation need besides the trained model?

Weights can be quantised directly; activations need observed ranges to choose their scales.

Why does unstructured pruning usually not speed up inference on GPUs and CPUs?

Speed needs structured sparsity (whole channels) or hardware and kernels that exploit sparse patterns.

For an interactive service, which latency number matters most?

Users experience individual requests, and remember the slow ones.

What should you always do after exporting a model to ONNX?

Unsupported operators and shape handling can change results silently.

Which is the safer reduced-precision default on recent GPUs?

bf16 rarely overflows; fp16 can, without loss scaling or care.
