Camera Geometry, Stereo & Depth

Every image in this course was made by a camera squashing a 3D world onto a 2D sensor, and so far we have ignored how. Measuring, mapping or placing anything in 3D, a robot arm reaching for a part, an AR sticker on a table, a car judging the distance to a cyclist, needs the geometry back. This chapter covers the pinhole camera model, how to measure a camera’s parameters, how two cameras give depth, and how networks now estimate depth from one image.

Everything is checked numerically: projection by hand against OpenCV, calibration against a camera whose true parameters are known, and stereo and monocular depth against laser-scanned ground truth for Middlebury’s Motorcycle scene. Hand-written projection matched OpenCV to 6 × 10−14 pixels. Calibration reached the noise floor of reprojection error with only 3 views, while its focal length was 1.8% off and one distortion coefficient 30 times too large: a small error is not a correct answer. On the Motorcycle scene, stereo was accurate but patchy, and a monocular network was complete and plausible, with twice the share of large errors once aligned.

The pinhole camera

A point at (X, Y, Z) in the camera’s coordinate frame (Z pointing out of the lens) lands on the image at u = fx X / Z + cx, v = fy Y / Z + cy. The focal lengths fx, fy (in pixels) and the principal point (cx, cy) form the intrinsic matrix K. Dividing by Z is what makes distant things small, and it is also why one image cannot tell a small near object from a big far one: every point along a ray through the pinhole lands on the same pixel. Points in the world are first moved into the camera frame by a rotation R and translation t, the extrinsics, so in homogeneous coordinates the whole camera is one 3 × 4 matrix, P = K [R | t].

K = np.array([[800, 0, 320], [0, 800, 240], [0, 0, 1]])     # fx = fy = 800 px, principal point at the image centre
Pc = X_world @ R.T + t                                          # world -> camera coordinates
uvw = Pc @ K.T; uv = uvw[:, :2] / uvw[:, 2:]                       # perspective divide by depth Z
ref = cv2.projectPoints(X_world, rvec, tvec, K, None)[0]          # OpenCV, for comparison

The hand-written projection and cv2.projectPoints agreed to 5.7 × 10−14 pixels on the eight corners of a cube: the same equations, up to floating-point rounding. Real lenses add distortion: radial distortion bends straight lines into curves (barrel or pincushion), modelled as a polynomial in the distance from the image centre, xd = x(1 + k1r² + k2r⁴ + …), plus small tangential terms. The panel’s first view projects a cube through this model and lets you change f, the distance and k1.

Calibration

Calibration estimates K and the distortion coefficients from photos of a known pattern, usually a chessboard. Every detected corner gives two equations relating its known 3D position on the board to its pixel position; with several views at different angles, a least-squares solver (Zhang’s method, used by cv2.calibrateCamera) recovers the intrinsics, the distortion and each view’s pose. The quality check is the reprojection error: project the board corners with the estimated parameters and measure the distance to where they were detected.

To know the true answer, the boards here are synthetic: a 9 × 6 corner chessboard with 25 mm squares, placed at random poses in front of a simulated camera with f = 800 pixels, principal point (320, 240) and barrel distortion k1 = −0.12, k2 = 0.05, with 0.3 pixels of noise added to every detected corner.

The reprojection error was about 0.41 pixels for every number of views, which is exactly what 0.3 pixels of noise on each corner coordinate should give (0.3 × √2): from 3 views on, the solver fitted the noise as well as possible. The parameters behind that perfect-looking fit were not right. With 3 views the focal length was 1.8% too long, the principal point 12 pixels off, and k2 was 1.5 instead of 0.05. More views pulled fx to within 0.5% and the principal point to within 4 pixels, but k2 stayed poorly determined even at 20 views (0.29), because these boards rarely reached the image corners, where higher-order distortion shows. Leaving distortion out of the model cost only 0.08 pixels of RMS error but biased the focal length by 1.9%. The practical lessons: a low reprojection error is necessary, not sufficient; cover the whole image, especially the corners, at varied tilts; and check calibration on held-out views or a known measurement.

Two cameras: epipolar geometry and stereo

With two cameras, a point seen at pixel p in the left image must lie, in the right image, somewhere on a single line, its epipolar line: the projection of the left camera’s ray through p. Matching becomes a 1D search instead of 2D. The relationship is captured by the 3 × 3 fundamental matrix F, with prT F pl = 0 for every matching pair (the essential matrix E is the same for calibrated, normalised coordinates). Stereo rigs go one step further and rectify both images so that epipolar lines are horizontal rows: a point in row y on the left is in row y on the right, shifted left by its disparity d.

Depth follows from similar triangles: Z = f · B / d, with B the baseline between the cameras. Disparity is inversely proportional to depth, so a one-pixel error at large distances is a huge depth error. That is why stereo is accurate near and coarse far away, and why wider baselines see further.

Block matching slides a small window along the row and picks the disparity with the smallest sum of absolute differences; it is fast but fails on textureless surfaces and at depth edges. Semi-global matching (SGM) adds a smoothness cost: neighbouring pixels should have similar disparities, with a small penalty for changes of one and a larger one for jumps, optimised along several directions across the image. It is the classical workhorse in cars and drones; learned stereo networks such as RAFT-Stereo now lead the benchmarks.

Depth from a single image

A single image has no parallax, so metric depth is impossible in principle: a doll’s house photographed close looks like a house photographed far. Yet people judge depth from one eye easily, using cues a network can learn: familiar object sizes, perspective, texture gradients, occlusion, shading, haze. Monocular depth networks such as MiDaS and Depth Anything are trained on millions of images with depth from many sources (stereo films, lidar, synthetic data, and pseudo-labels from bigger models) and predict relative depth: correct ordering and shape up to an unknown scale and shift. To compare with ground truth, or to use the output metrically, you must align it, here by least squares to the true disparity.

Depth Anything V2 Small (24.8 M parameters, 17.4 ms on the GPU) from the left image alone, aligned to the true disparity with one scale and one shift, gave a mean absolute error of 1.55 pixels of disparity with 17.4% of pixels off by more than 2, against 6.8–8.4% for stereo, but it covered every pixel and produced clean object boundaries; its mean relative error was 5.2%. The fitted scale and shift, 10.3 and 6.6, are numbers a single image cannot provide: without the ground truth to fit them, the output would be correct in shape and wrong in every absolute value. That is the trade-off in one experiment: geometry is metric where it can match, learning is complete and plausible everywhere and needs something metric to anchor it.

🧾
Use case: robot picking and phone portrait mode

A bin-picking robot needs millimetre-accurate depth at arm’s length, so it uses a calibrated stereo or structured-light camera, and its calibration is re-checked routinely, because a reprojection error creeping from 0.2 to 1 pixel turns into missed grasps. A phone’s portrait mode needs only to separate the person from the background, so it combines a small baseline between two of its lenses, or the dual-pixel sensor, with a monocular depth network that fills in where stereo fails, such as on hair. The trade-off measured here is the general one: geometry gives metric accuracy where it has texture; learned monocular depth gives complete, plausible maps with no scale.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Calibrating with too few or too similar views: with 3 views the reprojection error was already at its floor (0.41 px), yet the focal length was 1.8% off and k₂ was 30 times too large.
  • Leaving out the distortion model for a wide lens: fitting no distortion barely raised the reprojection error (0.41 → 0.49 px) but biased the focal length by 1.9%.
  • Using monocular depth as metric depth without alignment: it is relative.
  • Running stereo on unrectified images: matches are no longer along rows.
  • Mixing up disparity and depth: Z = fB/d, so averaging disparities is not averaging depths.
  • Changing the zoom or resolution after calibrating: K changes with them.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How does stereo give depth?

How a strong answer sounds
Two calibrated cameras a known baseline apart see a point at slightly different horizontal positions once the images are rectified so that matching points share a row. The shift is the disparity, and by similar triangles depth is focal length times baseline divided by disparity. So the job is to find, for each pixel, its match along the same row: block matching compares small windows, semi-global matching adds smoothness constraints, and learned networks do best. Because depth is inversely proportional to disparity, errors grow quickly with distance, and textureless or occluded areas have no reliable match. On Middlebury’s Motorcycle, OpenCV block matching had 1.2 pixels of mean error but left 24% of pixels without an estimate; semi-global matching covered 85% at 1.45 pixels and took 14 times longer.

Deeper: A team wants to use a monocular depth network instead of a stereo camera on a delivery robot. What do you tell them?

How a strong answer sounds
A single image cannot determine absolute scale, so these networks predict relative depth that must be scaled with something metric, for example a known camera height above a flat floor, wheel odometry, sparse lidar, or a few stereo points. Their maps are dense and handle textureless walls where stereo fails, but their errors are hard to bound and depend on whether the scene looks like their training data. For obstacle avoidance I would keep a metric sensor for safety and use the network to fill gaps, and evaluate on the robot’s own environment with ground truth, including glass, mirrors and night. I aligned Depth Anything V2 to ground truth on a stereo benchmark: it covered every pixel with a plausible map, but needed a fitted scale and shift, and had 17% of pixels off by more than 2 disparity pixels, against 7–8% for classical stereo.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Pinhole projection: u = fxX/Z + cx; the camera is P = K[R | t], plus lens distortion.
  • Calibration fits K and distortion from chessboards; measured, a perfect 0.41 px reprojection error came with a 1.8% focal-length error at 3 views.
  • Epipolar geometry reduces matching to a line; after rectification, depth = focal length × baseline / disparity.
  • Measured stereo: block matching 1.21 px error on 76% of pixels, semi-global 1.45 px on 85%; compare errors only at equal coverage.
  • Monocular depth is relative: after a fitted scale and shift it covered 100% of pixels with 17% large errors.

Check your understanding

What does the intrinsic matrix K contain?

Rotation and translation are extrinsics; distortion is modelled separately.

What is reprojection error?

It is what calibration minimises and the first number to check.

Depth from rectified stereo is…

Z = fB/d: larger disparity means nearer; errors in d matter most far away.

What does semi-global matching add to block matching?

Small penalty for changes of one, larger for jumps: smooth surfaces, sharp edges where the image supports them.

Why must a monocular depth network’s output be aligned before comparing it with metric depth?

A small near object and a large far one project identically.
