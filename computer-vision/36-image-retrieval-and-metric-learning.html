Image Retrieval & Metric Learning

Search by image, “find similar products”, face verification, duplicate detection and landmark recognition are all the same problem: turn each image into a vector, an embedding, such that similar images have nearby vectors, then find nearest neighbours fast. Unlike a classifier, a retrieval system must handle classes it never saw in training: new products, new faces, new buildings. That changes how the embedding is trained and how it is evaluated.

This chapter measures three off-the-shelf embeddings on pet-breed retrieval, trains small metric-learning heads with three losses and tests them on breeds held out from training, and builds an approximate nearest-neighbour index over 60,000 embeddings to see what speed costs in accuracy. Frozen DINO and ResNet-50 features found a same-breed pet as the nearest neighbour 91% of the time; CLIP, 79%. Every metric-learning head trained on 25 breeds made retrieval on the 12 unseen breeds worse than the frozen features it started from, a useful warning. And an IVF index found 97.7% of the exact top 10 while scanning 6.6% of the gallery.

  • Frame search as nearest-neighbour lookup in an embedding space
  • Evaluate retrieval with recall@k
  • Train embeddings with contrastive and triplet losses and hard negatives
  • Test generalisation to classes unseen in training
  • Trade accuracy for speed with approximate nearest-neighbour indexes

Embeddings for search

A retrieval system embeds every image in the gallery once, stores the vectors in an index, and at query time embeds the query and returns the k most similar gallery vectors, usually by cosine similarity on L2-normalised vectors. All the intelligence is in the embedding; the search is geometry. The standard metric is recall@k: for each query, is at least one of the k returned images a true match (here, the same breed)? Recall@1 is the strict version: is the very nearest image right?

Self-supervised DINO matched, and slightly beat, the supervised ImageNet ResNet-50 with less than half the dimensions, consistent with Chapter 23’s kNN results. ImageNet’s own labels include many dog and cat breeds, which is why the supervised model is strong here. CLIP was 12 points behind at R@1 but caught up by R@10: its embedding groups images by what a caption would say (“a fluffy white cat”), which puts similar-looking breeds close but not always in the right order. The right embedding depends on what “similar” means for your users.

Metric learning

A classifier’s features are trained to separate the training classes, not to place similar images close. Metric learning trains the embedding directly for distances. The contrastive loss pulls pairs of the same class together and pushes different-class pairs apart until they are at least a margin m away: ‖ea − eb‖² for positives, max(0, m − ‖ea − eb‖)² for negatives. The triplet loss works on (anchor, positive, negative) triples and asks only for a relative ordering, the positive closer than the negative by a margin: max(0, d(a, p) − d(a, n) + m). Which triples you use matters enormously, because most are already satisfied and give zero gradient. Batch-hard mining uses, for each anchor in a batch, its farthest positive and its nearest negative. A third option is to train a normalised softmax classifier and use its embedding (NormFace, ArcFace and CosFace refine this with angular margins, and dominate face recognition).

e = F.normalize(head(features), dim=1)                    # (B, 128) unit vectors
d = torch.cdist(e, e); same = y[:, None] == y[None]; eye = torch.eye(len(y), dtype=torch.bool)
hardest_pos = (d * (same & ~eye)).max(1).values                  # farthest image of the same breed
hardest_neg = (d + 4 * same.float()).min(1).values                # nearest image of another breed
loss = F.relu(hardest_pos - hardest_neg + 0.2).mean()             # batch-hard triplet loss

The test that matters for retrieval is unseen classes. Here the heads (2,048 → 512 → 128) are trained on frozen ResNet-50 features of 25 of the 37 Oxford Pets breeds, and retrieval is scored on the 12 breeds they never saw, as well as on test images of the 25 seen ones.

Contrastive head (margin 0.8)91.50%97.31%91.74%

This is the result to remember from the chapter, and it is not the one the method promises. On the 25 breeds the heads were trained on, softmax and triplet heads improved test retrieval slightly (94.1% to 95.1% and 94.4%). On the 12 breeds they never saw, every head was worse than using the frozen features directly: 96.8% and 96.2% against 98.2%, and the contrastive head lost 6.7 points. The heads learned to separate the training breeds and, in compressing 2,048 dimensions to 128 to do so, threw away information that distinguished the others. ResNet-50’s ImageNet features were already excellent at breeds, so there was little to gain and much to lose. (R@1 is higher on 12 breeds than on 37 simply because there are fewer wrong neighbours to choose from.) Metric learning pays when the starting embedding is poor for your notion of similarity, for example faces, products or a new domain, and it must always be judged on held-out classes; a gain on the training classes is not evidence.

Approximate nearest neighbours

Exact search compares the query with every gallery vector. For 60,000 vectors on a GPU that is a single matrix multiply; for a billion product images it is not. Approximate nearest-neighbour (ANN) indexes trade a little recall for a lot of speed. An inverted file (IVF) index clusters the gallery with k-means into, say, 256 lists, and at query time scans only the nprobe lists whose centroids are closest to the query. Product quantisation compresses vectors to a few bytes so more fit in memory, and graph indexes such as HNSW walk a neighbourhood graph towards the query. Libraries such as FAISS, ScaNN and the vector databases built on them combine these.

The accuracy columns are the point: scanning 16 of 256 lists, 6.6% of the vectors, recovered 97.7% of the exact nearest neighbours, and 64 lists recovered all of them. The time column (*) is a warning about measurement, not about IVF: this index is a Python loop over queries, so its fixed overhead per query dwarfs the arithmetic, and it was slower than exact search on the same CPU except at nprobe 1. Optimised libraries such as FAISS run the same algorithm in batched native code, where scanning 6.6% of the data takes roughly 6.6% of the time. At 60,000 vectors, exact search on a GPU in 5.6 ms is the right answer; ANN earns its place at hundreds of millions of vectors, or when the gallery does not fit in GPU memory. The exact top 10 were the same CIFAR-10 class 89% of the time, a measure of the DINO embedding itself.

Use case: visual product search

A shopper photographs a lamp and the app shows similar lamps for sale. Behind it: every catalogue image embedded by a model fine-tuned with a metric-learning loss on product pairs (same item from different photos as positives, visually similar but different items as hard negatives), stored in an ANN index sharded across machines, queried in tens of milliseconds. The catalogue changes daily, so the embedding must work on products added after training, the unseen-class test of this chapter, and the index must be rebuilt or updated without downtime. Teams monitor recall@k on a labelled query set and the index’s recall against exact search, separately, because either can degrade.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Evaluating on the training classes only: retrieval must work on new classes; hold whole classes out.
  • Forgetting to L2-normalise before cosine search, so vector length dominates.
  • Random triplets: most give zero loss; mine hard (or semi-hard) negatives in the batch.
  • Letting the query retrieve itself from the gallery, which inflates recall@1 to 100%.
  • Reporting ANN speed without its recall against exact search: an index that scans 0.4% of the data (nprobe 1) found only 51% of the true top 10 here.
🎤
Interview drill

Explain it simply: How would you build “search by photo” for a catalogue?

How a strong answer sounds
Embed every catalogue image into a normalised vector with a model whose distances reflect visual similarity, store the vectors in an approximate nearest-neighbour index, and at query time embed the photo and return the nearest items by cosine similarity. I would start from a strong pretrained embedding (DINO or CLIP), measure recall@k on a labelled set of query photos, then fine-tune with a metric-learning loss on matched pairs with hard negatives if needed, always testing on products not used in training. The index is tuned separately for its recall against exact search at the latency budget. On Oxford Pets, frozen DINO and ImageNet ResNet-50 features both put a same-breed image first about 91% of the time without any training on the task.

Deeper: Triplet loss or a softmax classifier for training embeddings?

How a strong answer sounds
Triplet and contrastive losses optimise distances directly and need no fixed class list, but their quality depends heavily on mining informative triplets, and training can be slow and unstable. A normalised softmax classifier, especially with an angular margin as in ArcFace, uses every class at every step, is stable and scales to millions of identities, which is why face recognition uses it; its weakness is needing class labels and a classifier head that grows with them. In practice both work when tuned, and the decision should rest on held-out-class recall. I tested both on Pets with frozen ResNet-50 features, training on 25 breeds and testing on 12 unseen ones: the softmax and triplet heads were close (96.8% and 96.2% R@1) and both slightly worse than the frozen features (98.2%), so the evaluation on unseen classes decided whether to train a head at all.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Retrieval = embed, index, nearest neighbours; evaluate with recall@k on held-out queries.
  • Measured on Pets: frozen DINO R@1 91.6%, ResNet-50 91.2%, CLIP 79.3%.
  • Contrastive and triplet losses train distances directly; batch-hard mining makes triplets informative.
  • Heads trained on 25 breeds were all worse on 12 unseen breeds than the frozen features (98.2% R@1): always test on held-out classes.
  • IVF scanning 6.6% of the gallery recovered 97.7% of the exact top 10; at 60k vectors, exact GPU search took 5.6 ms for 1,000 queries.

Check your understanding

What does recall@5 measure in retrieval?

It rises with k; recall@1 is the strictest version.

What does the triplet loss ask for?

max(0, d(a, p) − d(a, n) + m): only the ordering matters.

Why use batch-hard mining?

Mining focuses the loss on the cases the embedding gets wrong.

Why are metric-learning heads tested on breeds unseen in training?

A head can overfit the training classes and get worse on new ones.

What does nprobe control in an IVF index?

At nprobe = number of lists, IVF is exact search.
