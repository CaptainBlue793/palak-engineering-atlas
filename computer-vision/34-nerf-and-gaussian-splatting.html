NeRF & Gaussian Splatting

Give a computer a few dozen photos of an object from different angles, and ask for a photo from an angle nobody took. That is novel view synthesis, and two ideas transformed it. NeRF (2020) stores a whole scene inside a small neural network that maps a 3D point to a colour and a density, and renders images by marching along camera rays through it. 3D Gaussian splatting (2023) stores the scene as millions of explicit, coloured, semi-transparent blobs and renders by projecting them onto the image, fast enough for real time.

This chapter writes volume rendering from scratch, trains a tiny NeRF on 40 rendered views of a scene whose truth is known exactly, and tests the idea that made NeRF work: positional encoding. Gaussian splatting’s 3D rasteriser is a large CUDA project, so its core idea is tested here on a 2-D image instead, against a coordinate network. With positional encoding the NeRF reached 35.4 dB on held-out views in 23 seconds of training; without it, 21.0 dB and a smeared floor. Halving the samples per ray from 64 to 32 cost 3.6 dB, and 8 samples cost 17. In 2-D, 2,000 Gaussians matched an encoded MLP’s quality with an eighth of its parameters, but a naive renderer made them slower, which is exactly why splatting needs its tiled rasteriser.

Novel view synthesis

The input is a set of photos with known camera poses (in practice estimated by structure-from-motion software such as COLMAP from the photos themselves). The output is a representation from which a photo can be rendered at any new pose. Classical approaches built meshes or point clouds and struggled with thin structures, reflections and semi-transparent surfaces. NeRF and Gaussian splatting both optimise a scene representation directly so that renderings match the training photos, and both rely on a renderer that is differentiable, so the photo error can be pushed back into the scene.

Volume rendering

Treat the scene as a fog with a density σ (how much light it blocks per unit length) and a colour c at every point. For a camera ray r(t) = o + t·d, the pixel colour is the colour accumulated along the ray, each point weighted by its density and by the transmittance T(t), the share of light that survives from the camera to that point. Sampling N points along the ray turns the integral into a sum:

C = Σi Ti (1 − e−σiδi) ci, Ti = Πj<i e−σjδj

Each term is “opacity of this slab × how much light reaches it × its colour”. It is exactly alpha compositing from front to back, and every operation is differentiable.

def render(field, o, d, n=64, near=1.0, far=4.5):
    t = torch.linspace(near, far, n).expand(*o.shape[:-1], n) + jitter()          # n depths per ray
    sigma, rgb = field(o[..., None, :] + d[..., None, :] * t[..., None])           # query the scene at every sample
    delta = torch.diff(t, append=1e10)
    alpha = 1 - torch.exp(-sigma * delta)                                     # opacity of each slab
    T = torch.cumprod(torch.cat([ones, 1 - alpha], -1), -1)[..., :-1]          # light surviving to each slab
    w = alpha * T
    return (w[..., None] * rgb).sum(-2) + (1 - w.sum(-1, keepdim=True))       # + white background

NeRF: a scene inside an MLP

NeRF makes the field a neural network: an MLP F(x, y, z) → (σ, rgb) (the full model also takes the viewing direction, for reflections). Training picks random pixels from the training photos, renders their rays through the network with the function above, and minimises the squared error against the true pixel colours. Nothing else: no depth, no masks, no 3D supervision. The network learns geometry because only a consistent 3D scene can explain all views at once.

One detail turned out to be essential. An MLP fed raw coordinates is biased towards smooth functions (the “spectral bias” of neural networks) and produces blurry scenes. NeRF first passes each coordinate through a positional encoding, γ(p) = (sin(20πp), cos(20πp), …, sin(2L−1πp), cos(2L−1πp)), which gives the network high-frequency inputs to build sharp detail from.

The scene used here is defined analytically, three coloured spheres above a chequered floor, so that training views and held-out test views can be rendered exactly with 256 samples per ray. A camera circles the scene: 40 training views at 64 × 64 pixels, and 8 test views from angles halfway between training views.

The encoding was worth 14 dB, a factor of 28 in squared error, for almost no extra parameters or time. The raw network learned the spheres’ colours and rough shapes quickly and then stalled around 20–22 dB: it could not produce the chequered floor, which it rendered as a grey smear (panel, first view), because a checkerboard is a high-frequency pattern and an MLP on raw coordinates is biased towards smooth functions. With sinusoids of six octaves as inputs, the same network reproduced the floor and the sphere edges and was still improving at 4,000 steps. Both models were trained only on 40 photos; the test views are angles they never saw, so the 35 dB is genuine novel-view synthesis, on a deliberately simple scene.

The cost: one network call per sample

NeRF’s weakness is rendering speed. Every pixel needs a ray, every ray needs tens to hundreds of MLP evaluations, so a single 800 × 800 image with 192 samples per ray is 120 million network calls. Fewer samples are cheaper but miss thin surfaces or make them flicker.

Even a 64 × 64 image needs a quarter of a million network calls at 64 samples per ray. Quality collapses with too few samples, 17.9 dB at 8, because the samples step over thin surfaces and the opacity sum is badly estimated; doubling to 128 added only half a decibel for nearly three times the time. Scale that to 800 × 800 and you have NeRF’s rendering problem: seconds per frame on a large GPU.

The follow-up work attacked exactly this: Instant-NGP replaced most of the MLP with a multi-resolution hash grid of learned features, cutting training from hours to seconds; others baked NeRFs into textures or sparse voxel grids for real-time viewing.

Gaussian splatting: explicit primitives, rasterised

3D Gaussian splatting abandons the network. The scene is a few hundred thousand to a few million 3D Gaussians, each with a position, a 3D covariance (scale and rotation), an opacity and a view-dependent colour (spherical harmonics). Rendering projects every Gaussian to a 2D ellipse on the image, sorts them by depth per screen tile, and alpha-composites them front to back, the same compositing rule as volume rendering but over a sorted list of blobs instead of samples along a ray, and on the GPU’s rasterisation path. Training optimises all their parameters by gradient descent on photo error, starting from the structure-from-motion point cloud, and periodically clones, splits and prunes Gaussians where the error demands. The published result renders at 100+ frames per second at 1080p with quality matching or beating NeRF, which is why it took over the field within a year.

The 3D rasteriser is a substantial CUDA project, so the comparison here is its 2-D analogue: fit one image with N coloured 2D Gaussians, each with 9 parameters, against a coordinate MLP that maps (x, y) to a colour, the 2-D version of NeRF, with and without positional encoding.

Two results, one expected and one instructive. Expected: explicit primitives are an efficient representation. 2,000 Gaussians with 18,000 numbers came within about 1 dB of an encoded MLP with 143,000, and 500 Gaussians, 4,500 numbers, already beat the raw MLP by 11 dB; each Gaussian sits where detail is needed. Instructive: in this implementation the Gaussians were 240 times slower to render than the MLP, and far slower to train. That is because the code here evaluates every Gaussian at every pixel, 2,000 × 10,880 weights per image, a dense computation no faster than any other. The speed of real Gaussian splatting comes entirely from its rasteriser: each Gaussian is projected, its footprint bounded to a few screen tiles, the list sorted per tile, and each pixel blends only the handful of Gaussians that overlap it, in a hand-written CUDA kernel. Representation and renderer go together; the representation alone does not make anything fast.

Use case: property tours, film sets and digital twins

Estate agents, museums and visual-effects studios now capture spaces by walking through them with a phone or a drone and reconstructing a Gaussian splat or a NeRF, viewable in a browser. The engineering questions are those of this chapter: how many photos and from where (the scene is only as good as its views, and areas seen by few cameras fill with floating blobs), how long training takes, and how fast it renders on the target device. Factories use the same captures as digital twins for planning and inspection, where the lack of a clean mesh is the main drawback: splats look right but are awkward to measure or edit, so pipelines often extract a mesh afterwards.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Feeding raw coordinates to a coordinate network: the NeRF without encoding plateaued at 21 dB and could not draw the chequered floor; with it, 35 dB.
  • Inaccurate camera poses: a few pixels of pose error blur the whole reconstruction; check the structure-from-motion step first.
  • Evaluating on training views: they are what was fitted; hold out views between them.
  • Too few or clustered photos: unseen regions fill with “floaters”.
  • Forgetting the background: a NeRF renders whatever it must to explain the pixels, including the sky as a wall of fog.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How does a NeRF represent and render a scene?

How a strong answer sounds
A NeRF is an MLP that maps a 3D position, and usually a viewing direction, to a density and a colour; the whole scene lives in its weights. To render a pixel you cast a ray from the camera, sample points along it, query the network at each, and alpha-composite the colours front to back weighted by density and by how much light survives to each point, the volume-rendering sum. Training renders random pixels from posed photos and minimises the squared error with the true colours; only a consistent 3D scene explains every view, so geometry emerges without 3D supervision. Coordinates go through a sinusoidal positional encoding so the MLP can represent fine detail. I trained a tiny NeRF on 40 views of a synthetic scene: with positional encoding it reached 35.4 dB on unseen views in 23 seconds, without it 21.0 dB.

Deeper: Why is Gaussian splatting so much faster to render than NeRF?

How a strong answer sounds
NeRF must evaluate an MLP at many samples along every ray, most of them in empty space, so cost scales with pixels × samples × network size. Gaussian splatting stores the scene as explicit primitives, so rendering is projecting each Gaussian to an ellipse, sorting by depth per tile and compositing, with no network at all, a workload GPUs are built for; empty space costs nothing because there are no Gaussians there. The trade-offs are memory (millions of primitives, hundreds of megabytes) and a less regular representation that is harder to edit or turn into a surface. I also learned the hard way that the representation alone is not the speed-up: fitting an image with 2,000 2-D Gaussians evaluated densely at every pixel was 240 times slower to render than a coordinate MLP, although it used an eighth of the parameters. The tiled, culled rasteriser is what makes splatting real-time.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Novel view synthesis fits a 3D representation to posed photos through a differentiable renderer.
  • Volume rendering composites density-weighted colours along each ray, front to back.
  • Measured: positional encoding took a tiny NeRF from 21.0 to 35.4 dB on held-out views; 8 samples per ray gave 17.9 dB, 64 gave 35.3.
  • In 2-D, 2,000 Gaussians (18 k numbers) nearly matched an encoded MLP (143 k) but rendered slower without a tiled rasteriser.
  • Gaussian splatting replaces the network with explicit blobs that are rasterised, which is why it runs in real time.

Check your understanding

What does a NeRF’s MLP output for a 3D point?

Images are made by compositing those along camera rays.

In volume rendering, what is the transmittance Ti?

The product of (1 − opacity) of everything in front; an occluded point gets almost no weight.

Why does NeRF use positional encoding?

Without it, scenes come out blurry, as measured here.

What dominates NeRF’s rendering cost?

Pixels × samples × network: hundreds of millions of calls per frame.

How does 3D Gaussian splatting render an image?

Same compositing rule as volume rendering, over explicit primitives instead of ray samples.
