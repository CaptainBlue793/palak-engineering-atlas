Optical Flow & Object Tracking

Chapter 31 classified whole clips. Many applications need something finer: where every pixel moved between two frames (optical flow), or where each individual object is in every frame, with a stable identity (multi-object tracking). Flow powers video stabilisation, frame interpolation and motion features; tracking powers traffic counting, sports analytics and every self-driving stack.

Both are measured here against ground truth that is exactly known. For flow, a photo is moved by a known rotation and shift, so the true motion of every pixel is known. For tracking, a simulated scene has eight objects bouncing around a pillar that hides them, with noisy detections and false alarms. RAFT recovered the true motion to within 0.05 pixels on average, against 1.8 pixels for the classical Farnebäck method. For tracking, adding a Kalman filter to Hungarian matching halved the missed objects and identity switches, and raised IDF1 from 71.9 to 83.6.

Optical flow

Optical flow assigns to every pixel of frame 1 a vector (u, v): how far, in pixels, it moved to reach its position in frame 2. The classical starting point is brightness constancy: a point keeps its brightness as it moves, so I(x + u, y + v, t + 1) = I(x, y, t). Linearising gives one equation per pixel, Ixu + Iyv + It = 0, with two unknowns, so extra assumptions are needed: Lucas–Kanade assumes the flow is constant in a small window, Horn–Schunck that it is smooth everywhere, and Farnebäck fits local polynomials at several scales of an image pyramid to handle larger motions. All of them struggle with large displacements, textureless regions, and occlusions, where a pixel simply has no match.

Learned methods treat flow as dense matching. RAFT (2020) extracts features from both frames, builds a 4D correlation volume holding the similarity between every pixel of frame 1 and every pixel of frame 2 (pooled at several scales), and then runs a recurrent unit that starts from zero flow and refines it a dozen times, each time looking up the correlations around where the current estimate points. Accuracy is measured with end-point error (EPE): the average Euclidean distance, in pixels, between predicted and true flow vectors.

This is a friendly test, a rigid motion of a single textured photo with no occlusions or motion blur; on real benchmarks such as Sintel errors are one to two pixels, but the ordering holds.

Tracking by detection

A tracker’s job is to link detections from frame to frame into tracks with persistent IDs. The dominant recipe, tracking by detection, runs a detector on every frame and solves an association problem each time:

The simplest tracker skips prediction and matches each track greedily to the detection it overlaps most. Better matching uses the Hungarian algorithm on the IoU matrix, as DETR did in Chapter 21, to find the assignment with the largest total overlap. Better prediction uses a Kalman filter: each track carries a state (here centre x, y and velocity) and its uncertainty; each frame it predicts the state forward with constant velocity, then corrects it with the matched detection, weighting the two by their uncertainties. Crucially, a track with no detection can keep coasting on its prediction for a while, so an object hidden behind something can be re-matched when it reappears. That combination, Kalman prediction plus Hungarian matching on IoU, is SORT (2016); DeepSORT adds an appearance embedding to the matching cost, and ByteTrack also matches low-confidence detections.

class KF:                                                    # state: centre x, y and velocity vx, vy
    def predict(s): s.x = s.F @ s.x; s.P = s.F @ s.P @ s.F.T + s.Q                # move forward, grow uncertainty
    def update(s, z):                                                           # z: detected centre
        y = z - s.H @ s.x; S = s.H @ s.P @ s.H.T + s.R; K = s.P @ s.H.T @ np.linalg.inv(S)   # Kalman gain
        s.x = s.x + K @ y; s.P = (np.eye(4) - K @ s.H) @ s.P
for dets in frames:
    for tr in tracks: tr.kf.predict()

Optimal matching instead of greedy matching removed about a quarter of the identity switches (11.8 to 8.6) and nothing else changed, because with eight well-separated objects greedy and optimal assignments rarely differ. Prediction changed everything. Without it, an object that disappears behind the pillar loses its track at once, is missed until it reappears, and comes back with a new ID: 294 missed detections and 15.8 track IDs for 8 objects. The Kalman tracker kept coasting each track on its velocity for up to 20 frames, so most objects were still tracked behind the pillar and re-matched with their old ID on the far side: misses fell to 135, switches to 4.8, IDs used to 12, and IDF1 rose by 12 points. The price is 25 false positives per scene, frames where a coasting track is reported but the object is somewhere else; that is the trade-off the max_age setting controls.

The metrics measure different failures. MOTA (multiple object tracking accuracy) is 1 − (misses + false positives + identity switches) / ground-truth objects, summed over frames; it is dominated by detection quality. IDF1 asks a different question: if each true object is assigned the one track ID that follows it longest, what fraction of detections carry the right ID? It is the metric that punishes trackers which keep finding objects but keep renaming them. Identity switches count how often a true object’s matched track ID changes. HOTA, the newer standard, balances detection and association explicitly.

Use case: counting people and vehicles

A city counting cyclists on a bridge, or a shop counting how many people pass a shelf, needs IDs more than boxes: one person crossing in front of a pillar must not be counted twice. Deployments typically run a fast detector (Chapter 15) and a SORT-family tracker with a counting line; the number that matters is the identity-switch rate in occlusions, exactly the pillar of this chapter’s simulation, and it is tuned with how long tracks may coast (max_age) and how strict the match threshold is. Too short and occluded people get new IDs; too long and a coasting track latches onto someone else.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Deleting tracks the moment a detection is missed: without prediction and coasting, occluded objects were missed 294 times per scene and came back with new IDs; a Kalman filter that coasts up to 20 frames halved both.
  • Reporting MOTA alone: it barely changes with identity switches; report IDF1 or HOTA too.
  • Matching by IoU when objects move fast relative to their size: predicted boxes stop overlapping; use prediction, or centre distance and appearance.
  • Feeding RAFT unnormalised images: use the weights’ transforms, which scale to [−1, 1].
  • Computing flow between frames of different sizes or after cropping one: the flow then includes the crop offset.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How does SORT track objects?

How a strong answer sounds
It runs a detector on every frame and keeps a Kalman filter per track with the box centre and velocity. Each frame it predicts every track forward, builds an IoU matrix between predicted boxes and new detections, and solves the assignment with the Hungarian algorithm, accepting matches above an IoU threshold. Matched tracks are corrected with their detection, unmatched detections start new tracks, and tracks with no match keep coasting on their prediction for a number of frames before being deleted, which is what carries an ID through a short occlusion. In a simulated scene with a pillar hiding objects, SORT reached 83.6 IDF1 against 71.9 for the same Hungarian matching without prediction, and used 12 IDs for 8 objects instead of 16.

Deeper: Why is RAFT more accurate than classical optical flow?

How a strong answer sounds
Classical methods rely on brightness constancy plus a smoothness or local-constancy assumption, linearised around the current estimate, so they handle small motions in textured regions well and break down with large displacements, textureless areas and occlusion boundaries. RAFT learns features for matching, compares every pixel with every other through an all-pairs correlation volume at several scales, so large motions are visible, and refines a full-resolution flow field with a recurrent update that looks up correlations around its current estimate, a learned optimiser trained end to end on synthetic data with exact flow. On a test pair with known motion, RAFT large reached 0.05 pixels of end-point error against 1.8 for Farnebäck; even one of its refinement steps beat the classical method.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Optical flow is per-pixel motion, scored by end-point error; RAFT matches all pixel pairs and refines iteratively.
  • Measured on known motion: RAFT large 0.049 px EPE, Farnebäck 1.81 px; one RAFT update already beats Farnebäck.
  • Tracking by detection: predict each track, match by IoU with the Hungarian algorithm, update, coast through misses.
  • Prediction matters more than matching: SORT raised IDF1 from 71.9 to 83.6 and halved misses and ID switches, at the cost of some false positives while coasting.
  • MOTA is dominated by detection; IDF1 and identity switches measure whether identities survive.

Check your understanding

What does end-point error measure?

EPE = mean of ‖predicted − true‖ over pixels; 0 is perfect.

Which assumption underlies classical optical flow?

Linearised, it gives Ix·u + Iy·v + It = 0, one equation for two unknowns per pixel.

What does the Kalman filter add to a tracker?

Predict with constant velocity, correct with the detection, weighted by uncertainty.

Which metric is designed to punish a tracker that keeps finding objects but keeps changing their IDs?

IDF1 credits a detection only when it carries the ID consistently assigned to that object.

Why can RAFT handle large motions that classical methods miss?

Linearised classical methods only see motion near the current estimate.
