Training a CNN on CIFAR-10

Chapter 3 showed that a CNN is the right shape for images. This chapter is about the other half: training it well. The same architecture can finish at 92% or at 10% depending on the learning rate, the batch size, the schedule and how much data it sees, and the loss curves say which is happening long before the run ends.

A 1.19-million-parameter CNN is trained from random weights to 91.68% on CIFAR-10 in 47 seconds on this GPU. Then it is trained again 21 more times, changing one thing each time: seven learning rates, four batch sizes with and without the linear scaling rule, no weight decay, and a tenth of the data, to overfit on purpose.

  • Read training and test curves, and tell underfitting, good fitting and overfitting apart
  • Find a learning rate with a short sweep
  • Scale the learning rate with the batch size, and know where that rule breaks
  • Use a warm-up and decay schedule, and weight decay
  • Recognise overfitting, including the case where test loss rises while test accuracy still improves

The dataset and the recipe

CIFAR-10 has 50,000 training and 10,000 test images, 32 × 32 pixels, in ten balanced classes (airplane, automobile, bird, cat, deer, dog, frog, horse, ship, truck). It is small enough to keep on the GPU as uint8 (150 MB) and hard enough that architecture and training choices show up clearly. The model is Chapter 3’s three-block CNN at 64/128/256 channels, 1,188,170 parameters. The recipe, used for every run unless stated:

model = cnn(width=64).cuda().to(memory_format=torch.channels_last)
opt = torch.optim.SGD(model.parameters(), lr=0.1, momentum=0.9, nesterov=True, weight_decay=5e-4)
sched = torch.optim.lr_scheduler.OneCycleLR(opt, max_lr=0.1, total_steps=epochs * 196,
            pct_start=0.15, anneal_strategy="linear", div_factor=10, final_div_factor=100)
for epoch in range(epochs):
    perm = torch.randperm(50_000, device="cuda")
    for k in range(0, 50_000, 256):                              # 196 steps per epoch
        idx = perm[k:k + 256]
        x = crop_flip(norm(X[idx]))                                # (256, 3, 32, 32): random 4-px crop + flip, on the GPU
        with torch.autocast("cuda", torch.bfloat16):
            loss = F.cross_entropy(model(x), Y[idx])
        opt.zero_grad(set_to_none=True); loss.backward(); opt.step(); sched.step()

The one-cycle schedule warms the learning rate up linearly from 0.01 to 0.1 over the first 15% of steps, then decays it linearly to 0.0001. Warm-up avoids large, badly aimed steps while the random weights and BatchNorm statistics are still settling; the long decay lets the model settle into a minimum at the end. Weight decay 5 × 10⁻⁴ shrinks every weight slightly at every step, a penalty on large weights that reduces overfitting.

Finding the learning rate

Ten epochs at each peak learning rate, everything else fixed:

Peak LR0.0030.010.030.10.31.03.0
Test accuracy82.94%86.26%88.00%86.32%77.04%10.00%10.00%

The curve is the classic shape: too small and the model has not got far in ten epochs; a broad plateau of good values spanning about a factor of ten; then a cliff, where the run diverges and the model predicts one class. For short runs the best rate was 0.03; for the 30-epoch run 0.1 was used and worked well, because a longer schedule spends more steps at low rates. A sweep over factors of three, a few epochs each, is the cheapest experiment in deep learning and the one most often skipped.

Batch size and the linear scaling rule

A larger batch averages more images per step, so each gradient is less noisy, and there are fewer steps per epoch. The linear scaling rule says: when you multiply the batch by k, multiply the learning rate by k too, so that an epoch moves the weights about as far. Ten epochs each:

BatchSteps per epochLR fixed at 0.1LR scaled (0.1 × batch / 256)Time, 10 epochs
321,56387.26%89.31% (lr 0.0125)42.8 s

5129882.70%66.11% (lr 0.2)14.4 s
2,0482551.40%10.00%, diverged (lr 0.8)14.1 s

Two lessons. Below 256, scaling the rate down helped. Above it, the rule broke: at 512 the scaled rate 0.2 did far worse than the unscaled one, and at 2,048 the scaled rate 0.8 diverged. With ten epochs, a batch of 2,048 gets only 250 steps in total, and no rate rescues that: large batches need more epochs, a longer warm-up, or optimisers designed for them (LARS, LAMB). Speed saturated too: from batch 128 upwards the GPU was already full, so 2,048 was barely faster per epoch (14.1 s against 15.7 s) while being much worse per epoch. Batch size is a compute-efficiency setting; choose the smallest batch that keeps the GPU busy, then tune the learning rate for it.

Overfitting on purpose

Take 5,000 training images, a tenth of the set, and remove the regularisers: no augmentation, no weight decay, 60 epochs. Training accuracy reached 100% and test accuracy finished at 61.99%. The model memorised its 5,000 images. With the crops, flips and weight decay put back, the same 5,000 images gave 72.48%: regularisation was worth 10.5 points when data was scarce, against 1.4 points for weight decay on the full set (91.68% vs 90.32%).

The unregularised run also shows a trap. Its test loss bottomed out at 1.34 at epoch 24 and then rose to 2.29, while its test accuracy kept improving, from 53% at that point to 62%. Both are true at once: the model got more of the test images right, and became more confidently wrong on the ones it missed, which cross-entropy punishes heavily (P7). Early stopping on test loss would have stopped at the wrong point for accuracy. Decide which metric you care about before you stop on it, and stop on a validation set, never the test set.

🧾
Use case: training ImageNet in an hour

In 2017 a team at Facebook trained ResNet-50 on ImageNet in one hour on 256 GPUs with a total batch of 8,192, matching the accuracy of the usual 256-image batch. The recipe was this chapter’s two ideas at scale: the linear scaling rule (learning rate 0.1 × 8,192 / 256 = 3.2) and a gradual warm-up over the first five epochs, without which the large rate diverged early, as the batch-2,048 run did here. Beyond about 8,000 the rule broke down for them too, which led to layer-wise optimisers such as LARS that adapt the rate per layer. Batch size buys speed across many GPUs, never accuracy, and the learning rate has to follow it with care.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Using one learning rate for every batch size, or scaling it linearly far beyond where the rule holds.
  • Skipping warm-up with a high learning rate, and diverging in the first steps.
  • Raising the batch size to “go faster” after the GPU is already full: here 2,048 was 10% faster per epoch and 36 points worse.
  • Comparing training accuracy measured with augmentation against test accuracy measured without.
  • Early stopping on the test set, or on test loss when the goal is accuracy.
  • Changing several settings at once and not knowing which one helped.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Your training accuracy is 100% and test accuracy is 62%. What do you do?

How a strong answer sounds
That is overfitting: the model has memorised the training set. The fixes, roughly in order of how much they usually buy: more data, or more effective data through augmentation (random crops and flips here took a 5,000-image run from 62.0% to 72.5% together with weight decay); weight decay; a pretrained backbone, which brings knowledge from millions of other images (Chapter 8); a smaller or more constrained model; and early stopping on a validation set. I would also check that the validation set is really separate (no duplicates of training images) and that the gap is not a preprocessing difference between training and test. Then change one thing at a time and keep the curves.

Deeper: You increase the batch size from 256 to 2,048 to train faster. What else must change, and what can go wrong?

How a strong answer sounds
The learning rate, the warm-up and probably the number of epochs. With eight times the batch there are eight times fewer steps per epoch, so at the same rate the model moves much less; the linear scaling rule says to multiply the rate by eight. That rule holds only up to a point: here, at batch 512 the scaled rate did much worse than the unscaled one in ten epochs, and at 2,048 the scaled rate of 0.8 diverged, while the unscaled one reached only 51%, because 250 steps were simply too few. So large batches need a longer warm-up, more epochs to keep the step count up, and sometimes layer-wise adaptive optimisers (LARS, LAMB). And check that it is faster at all: on one GPU the speed had saturated at batch 128, so 2,048 bought 10% per epoch. Large batches pay off when they let you spread work over more GPUs.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • A 1.2M-parameter CNN reached 91.68% on CIFAR-10 in 47 s with SGD, one-cycle LR, crops, flips and weight decay.
  • Read train and test curves together: close and poor is underfitting; a widening gap with flat test is overfitting.
  • A factor-of-three LR sweep is cheap: 0.03 was best for 10 epochs; 1.0 and above diverged to 10%.
  • Linear LR scaling helped below batch 256 and broke above it; batch 2,048 at the scaled LR diverged.
  • Regularisation mattered most with little data: +10.5 points on 5,000 images, +1.4 for weight decay on 50,000.
  • Test loss can rise while test accuracy improves: the model grows more confident in its remaining errors.

Check your understanding

Training and test accuracy are both 60% and close together after many epochs. What is the most likely diagnosis?

No gap means the model is not memorising; low accuracy on both means it cannot fit well yet: more capacity, more epochs or a better learning rate.

The linear scaling rule says that when the batch size doubles, the learning rate should…

Twice the images per step and half the steps per epoch; doubling the rate keeps the movement per epoch similar. Here it broke above batch 256.

Why is a learning-rate warm-up used at the start of training?

Early gradients are large and poorly aimed. Starting low and ramping up avoids divergence, which matters most for large learning rates and batches.

On 5,000 images with no regularisation, test loss rose from 1.34 to 2.29 while test accuracy rose from 53% to 62%. How?

Loss and accuracy measure different things. Overconfident errors raise cross-entropy even as the count of correct answers grows.

Going from batch 128 to 2,048 on this GPU changed the time per epoch by about…

15.7 s to 14.1 s for 10 epochs: the GPU was already busy at 128. The larger batch bought almost no speed and cost 36 points of accuracy.
