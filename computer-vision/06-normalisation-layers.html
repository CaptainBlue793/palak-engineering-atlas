Batch Norm & Normalisation Layers

Batch normalisation is in almost every CNN, usually after every convolution. It makes networks train faster, at higher learning rates, and is part of why the deep networks of Chapter 7 train at all. It is also the one layer whose output for an image depends on the other images in the batch, and that single fact is behind a family of bugs that raise no errors.

This chapter trains a ResNet-20 with BatchNorm, GroupNorm and no normalisation, then takes the trained BatchNorm model and breaks it on purpose: evaluating in the wrong mode, with small batches, with batches of one class, and on darkened images. One mistake took it from 89% to 10%; another silently and permanently cost it 11 points.

What BatchNorm computes

For each channel, BatchNorm takes the activations of that channel across the whole batch and every spatial position, computes their mean μ and variance σ², and normalises: x̂ = (x − μ) / √(σ² + ε). Then it rescales with two learned numbers per channel, y = γ x̂ + β, so the layer can undo the normalisation if that helps. The effect is that every channel entering the next layer has a stable scale whatever the weights before it are doing, which smooths optimisation and lets learning rates be higher.

def batchnorm_train(x, gamma, beta, running, momentum=0.1, eps=1e-5):
    # x: (N, C, H, W). Statistics per channel, over the batch AND all positions.
    mu = x.mean((0, 2, 3))                                   # (C,)
    var = x.var((0, 2, 3), unbiased=False)                  # (C,)
    running["mean"] = (1 - momentum) * running["mean"] + momentum * mu   # remembered for evaluation
    running["var"] = (1 - momentum) * running["var"] + momentum * var
    xh = (x - mu[:, None, None]) / (var[:, None, None] + eps).sqrt()
    return gamma[:, None, None] * xh + beta[:, None, None]

def batchnorm_eval(x, gamma, beta, running, eps=1e-5):
    # no batch statistics: use the averages collected during training
    xh = (x - running["mean"][:, None, None]) / (running["var"][:, None, None] + eps).sqrt()
    return gamma[:, None, None] * xh + beta[:, None, None]

There are two modes. In training mode it uses the current batch’s statistics, and also updates an exponential moving average of them, the running mean and variance. In evaluation mode it uses only the running averages, so each image’s output depends on that image alone. model.train() and model.eval() switch every BatchNorm layer (and dropout) between the two. Forgetting the switch is not an error; the model just behaves differently.

BatchNorm was best by 4.6 points over GroupNorm and 5.6 over the best unnormalised run, and the unnormalised network was sensitive to the learning rate in a way the others were not. GroupNorm normalises each image on its own, over groups of channels and all positions, so it does not depend on the batch at all; it was 62% slower here because its kernels are less optimised than cuDNN’s fused BatchNorm. LayerNorm is the same idea over all channels at once; it is the standard in transformers (Chapters 19–21), where batch statistics over tokens of different images make little sense.

The train/eval switch, broken on purpose

The same trained model, each test on a fresh copy, 10,000 test images:

train mode, batch 471.32%four images give a poor estimate of a channel’s mean and variance
train mode, batch 19.95%each image normalised by itself: the features that distinguish it are removed
train mode, batches of one class15.23%normalising within one class removes what the class has in common

The last two rows are why a model that works in a notebook fails in a service: the notebook evaluated large shuffled batches, the service sends one image at a time, and nobody called eval(). Worse, train mode also updates the running statistics. After one pass over the class-sorted test batches in train mode, the same model evaluated correctly in eval mode scored 77.73%, 11.3 points lower than before, permanently, until retrained or reset. (The first run of this chapter’s own experiment fell into exactly this trap: its later measurements were made on a model whose statistics an earlier test had overwritten, and had to be redone on fresh copies.) A shuffled train-mode pass did much less damage (88.87%) because shuffled batches look like the training batches.

Small batches when training

Training with tiny batches has the same problem from the other side: the statistics BatchNorm normalises with are noisy, and the running averages it stores are averages of noisy estimates. Five epochs at each batch size, learning rate scaled with batch:

Training batch41664256
BatchNorm81.94%82.34%82.73%81.79%
GroupNorm74.51%76.29%74.94%66.64%

BatchNorm barely degraded down to batch 4 on this small network: 81.9% against 82.7% at batch 64. That is better than its reputation, partly because CIFAR images are small and fairly uniform, and partly because at training time the noise in batch statistics acts as a mild regulariser. (A batch-2 run was started and stopped unfinished to free the GPU, so it is not reported.) GroupNorm was the weaker layer throughout on this small network, and at batch 256 its scaled learning rate was too high for it in five epochs. GroupNorm’s case is not that it beats BatchNorm when both work, but that it keeps working where BatchNorm cannot: in detection and segmentation (Chapters 14–18), where high-resolution inputs leave room for only one or two images per GPU, and in any model that must give identical results however it is batched.

Shifted data: re-estimating the statistics

Running statistics encode what the training data looked like. If deployed images differ, say darker, from a different camera, they no longer match. Testing on CIFAR-10 test images darkened to 50% brightness: with the stored statistics the model scored 85.34% (on the second half of the test set, 89.12% when not darkened). Resetting every BatchNorm layer’s running statistics and re-estimating them from 5,000 darkened images in train mode, with no labels and no weight updates, recovered 88.18%. This is a cheap form of test-time adaptation (sometimes called AdaBN): useful when a whole deployment shifts at once, dangerous if done on small or unrepresentative batches, as the class-sorted result above shows.

Folding BatchNorm for deployment

In eval mode BatchNorm is a fixed per-channel scale and shift, and a convolution followed by a scale and shift is just another convolution. So for deployment it can be folded into the convolution before it: W′ = W · γ/√(σ² + ε) per output channel, b′ = β − γ μ/√(σ² + ε). On the trained ResNet-20 the folded stem matched the original to 0.0023 (bf16 inputs) and the fully folded network had the same accuracy within 0.05 points, while running 7% faster at batch 256 (1.25 ms against 1.34 ms) because it skips a pass over every feature map. Deployment toolchains (ONNX Runtime, TensorRT, mobile runtimes) do this automatically; Chapter 38 measures the rest of the deployment stack.

🧾
Use case: synchronised BatchNorm in detection training

Object detectors train on large images, often at 800 to 1,333 pixels, so each GPU holds only two to eight of them, and BatchNorm statistics computed per GPU are noisy, the situation measured above. Detection libraries handle this in three ways: freeze the backbone’s BatchNorm layers at their ImageNet statistics (torchvision’s detection models use a frozen BatchNorm in the backbone for this reason), use synchronised BatchNorm, which averages statistics across all GPUs in each step at the cost of extra communication, or replace BatchNorm with GroupNorm. All three exist because a layer that depends on the batch behaves badly when the batch is small.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Forgetting model.eval() before inference: at batch 1 the model scored 10%.
  • Running test or production data through a model in train mode, which silently overwrites its running statistics (−11.3 points here, permanently).
  • Training BatchNorm models with batches of 2–4 per GPU without freezing, synchronising or replacing the layers.
  • Feeding batches that are all one class (or one camera, one patient) through BatchNorm in train mode.
  • Putting a bias in a convolution followed by BatchNorm: the mean subtraction removes it, so it is wasted parameters.
  • Fine-tuning with tiny batches and BN still updating, drifting the pretrained statistics.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: A classifier scores 89% in offline evaluation and about 10% in production, where it serves one image at a time. What would you check first?

How a strong answer sounds
Whether the serving code calls model.eval(). In train mode BatchNorm normalises with the statistics of the current batch; with one image per batch each channel is normalised by that image’s own mean and variance, which removes exactly the information the next layers need. Measured on a ResNet-20, that was 89.0% in eval mode and 9.95% in train mode at batch 1. I would also check that the production model’s running statistics were not overwritten by an earlier pass in train mode (a sorted or one-class pass cost 11 points permanently here), and then the usual preprocessing suspects. The fix is model.eval() plus torch.inference_mode(), and a test that runs the same images at batch 1 and batch 64 and checks the outputs match.

Deeper: When would you choose GroupNorm or LayerNorm over BatchNorm?

How a strong answer sounds
When batch statistics are unreliable or unwanted. BatchNorm needs reasonably large, representative batches: detection and segmentation on high-resolution images often fit only one or two images per GPU, video and 3D models likewise, and some settings need outputs independent of batch composition (per-request inference that must be deterministic, contrastive learning where batch statistics can leak information between samples). GroupNorm normalises each sample over groups of channels and has no train/eval difference; LayerNorm normalises over all features of each token and is the default in transformers. When batches are large and i.i.d., BatchNorm is usually the best and fastest choice for CNNs: here it beat GroupNorm by 4.6 points at batch 128 and trained 1.6 times faster.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • BatchNorm normalises each channel with batch statistics in training and with running averages in evaluation.
  • It was the best layer here: 89.4% vs 84.8% for GroupNorm and 83.8% with none.
  • Train mode at inference depends on the batch: 88.4% at 1,000, 71.3% at 4, 10.0% at batch 1, 15.2% for one-class batches.
  • Train mode also overwrites the running statistics: one bad pass cost 11.3 points for good. Deep-copy before experiments.
  • Re-estimating statistics on unlabelled shifted data recovered darkened-image accuracy from 85.3% to 88.2%.
  • In eval mode BN is an affine map and folds into the preceding convolution: same accuracy, 7% faster.

Check your understanding

Over which dimensions does BatchNorm compute its mean for a (N, C, H, W) tensor?

One mean and variance per channel, over the batch and all spatial positions. GroupNorm and LayerNorm instead normalise each image on its own.

A BatchNorm model is evaluated in train mode with batch size 1. What happened to accuracy?

Each image is normalised by its own statistics, removing what distinguishes it. PyTorch only raises an error when a BN layer sees a single value per channel, such as after global pooling.

Why did a single train-mode pass over class-sorted test batches lower later eval-mode accuracy?

No gradients flowed, but the running averages moved. Eval accuracy dropped from 89.0% to 77.7% and stayed there.

What does folding BatchNorm into the preceding convolution change?

In eval mode BN is a fixed affine map per channel, so W and b absorb it. Same accuracy within 0.05 points, 7% faster here.

Images in deployment are darker than in training. Which cheap step recovered most of the lost accuracy?

85.3% → 88.2%, with no labels and no weight updates. Done on large, shuffled batches, then back to eval mode.
