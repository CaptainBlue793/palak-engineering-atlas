Data Augmentation

A photo of a dog, flipped left to right, is still a photo of a dog. So is the same photo cropped a little, a little darker, or with a patch missing. Data augmentation applies changes like these at random during training, so the model sees a slightly different version of every image every epoch and learns which differences do not matter.

Every augmentation here was trained on the same CNN for 30 epochs, one at a time, on CIFAR-10. Crops and flips together took it from 87.6% to 91.1%. Some popular methods made it worse at this budget, one augmentation that is simply wrong for these labels cost 12 points, and with a tenth of the data the right augmentation was worth 11 points.

  • Use Mixup, CutMix and cutout, and know their training-length trade-off

Why augmentation works

A model generalises by learning what is common to every image of a class and ignoring what is not. With 5,000 images per class it cannot tell from the data alone that “the dog faces left” is irrelevant while “the animal has floppy ears” is not, so it may learn both. Augmentation states the invariance directly: if every training dog is randomly mirrored, facing left can no longer predict anything, and the model stops relying on it. In effect it multiplies the dataset by every transformed version the label survives, which is why the gains are largest when data is scarce.

Chapter 3 measured the architectural side of this: a CNN is shift-equivariant but only partly shift-invariant. Random crops supply the rest. Flips supply mirror invariance, colour jitter supplies invariance to lighting and white balance, and cutout teaches the model not to depend on any single region.

Crop and flip, on the GPU

The standard CIFAR recipe pads each 32 × 32 image by 4 pixels and crops a random 32 × 32 window (so objects shift by up to 4 pixels), then flips half of the images. With the dataset on the GPU, both run on a whole batch at once with indexing, so augmentation costs almost nothing:

def crop_flip(x, pad=4):                                         # x: (N, 3, 32, 32) float, normalised
    N, C, H, W = x.shape
    xp = F.pad(x, (pad, pad, pad, pad))                              # (N, 3, 40, 40), zeros around
    i = torch.randint(0, 2 * pad + 1, (N,), device=x.device)           # top offset per image
    j = torch.randint(0, 2 * pad + 1, (N,), device=x.device)           # left offset per image
    rows = i[:, None] + torch.arange(H, device=x.device)                # (N, 32)
    cols = j[:, None] + torch.arange(W, device=x.device)                # (N, 32)
    x = xp[torch.arange(N)[:, None, None, None], torch.arange(C)[None, :, None, None],
           rows[:, None, :, None], cols[:, None, None, :]]               # (N, 3, 32, 32): one crop per image
    flip = torch.rand(N, 1, 1, 1, device=x.device) < 0.5
    return torch.where(flip, x.flip(3), x)                            # mirror half of them

For datasets loaded from disk, the same operations come from torchvision.transforms.v2 (RandomCrop(32, padding=4), RandomHorizontalFlip()) in the DataLoader workers (P6). The v2 transforms also move bounding boxes and masks along with the image, which detection and segmentation need (P4).

Mixup and CutMix: soft labels

Mixup blends two training images, x = λ·x₁ + (1 − λ)·x₂, and trains on the same blend of their labels (with cross-entropy against λ·y₁ + (1 − λ)·y₂). CutMix pastes a rectangle of one image onto another and mixes the labels by the rectangle’s area. Both are strong regularisers and calibrate predictions better, and both are standard in recipes that train ImageNet models for hundreds of epochs. Here, at 30 epochs, both made the model worse (90.2% and 89.4%). The training accuracy says why: 94.7% and 93.2%, far from fitting. A model given a harder task needs more steps to learn it. Strong regularisers are a trade: they raise the accuracy you can reach and lower the accuracy you reach quickly. Measure at your training budget, not someone else’s.

Augmentations that break the label

An augmentation is only valid if the label survives it. Mirroring left to right is fine for CIFAR’s objects. Turning them upside down is not quite: an upside-down boat in a photo is a capsized boat, an upside-down horse is rare, and the sky being at the top is a useful cue the model then cannot use. Vertical flips cost 2.2 points; rotations up to 180° cost 12.4, and the cat class fell from 81.5% to 55.1%. What is valid depends on the data, not on the method. For satellite or microscope images, which have no “up”, vertical flips and 90° rotations are standard and free. For digits they are fatal (a rotated 6 is a 9), and for text, mirroring is.

Augmentation also defines what a model is robust to, and nothing else. On a test set turned upside down, the crop + flip model scored 42.54% while the model trained with vertical flips scored 88.57%, nearly its normal accuracy. If upside-down images can occur in use, train with them; if they cannot, do not pay 2 points for robustness you will never need.

Test-time augmentation and small data

Test-time augmentation (TTA) averages the model’s predicted probabilities over transformed copies of each test image. Averaging over the image and its mirror took the crop + flip model from 91.05% to 92.13% and the unaugmented model from 87.64% to 88.64%, at twice the inference cost. It helped the CutMix model by only 0.3, because that model was already less sure of itself.

And augmentation is worth most when data is scarce. Trained on 5,000 images for 80 epochs: no augmentation 63.44%, crop + flip 74.73% (+11.3), crop + flip + CutMix 69.07%. With 50,000 images the same crop + flip was worth 3.4 points. Chapter 8 shows the other cure for small data, starting from pretrained weights.

Use case: augmentation policies for medical images

A chest X-ray classifier cannot use a horizontal flip as freely as a photo classifier: the heart is on the left, and a mirrored X-ray is a different (rare) anatomy, so some flipped training images would be anatomically wrong. Medical-imaging teams therefore choose augmentations per modality: small rotations and scale changes, brightness and contrast changes that mimic differences between scanners, elastic deformations for tissue, and no flips where laterality matters. The same reasoning as this chapter’s vertical-flip result, applied to a domain where getting it wrong is harder to notice.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Using augmentations that change the label: vertical flips on photos (−2.2 here), rotations on digits, mirroring on text or laterality-sensitive scans.
  • Augmenting the test set by accident, or the validation set, so the reported score is noisy and lower.
  • Copying a long-schedule recipe (Mixup, CutMix) into a short run and losing accuracy.
  • Forgetting to transform boxes, masks and keypoints with the image.
  • Comparing train accuracy measured with augmentation against test accuracy without it.
  • Expecting robustness to changes that were never in training: crop + flip models scored 42.5% upside down.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Why does data augmentation reduce overfitting?

How a strong answer sounds
It turns each training image into many slightly different images with the same label, so features that do not survive the transformation (which way the object faces, its exact position, the exact lighting) stop predicting the label and the model stops relying on them. It also makes the training set impossible to memorise exactly, which is what overfitting is. On CIFAR-10 with a 1.2M-parameter CNN, no augmentation reached 100% training and 87.6% test accuracy; crop + flip lowered training accuracy to 99.2% and raised test to 91.1%; adding cutout lowered training to 97% and raised test to 91.8%. The effect is largest with little data: on 5,000 images crop + flip added 11 points. The condition is that the label must survive: vertical flips and big rotations hurt here.

Deeper: A colleague adds CutMix and the accuracy drops. Is CutMix bad?

How a strong answer sounds
Not necessarily: it is probably under-trained. CutMix and Mixup make each training example harder (two images, a soft label), so the model needs more epochs to fit; recipes that report gains typically train for hundreds of epochs. Here, at 30 epochs, CutMix lowered test accuracy from 91.1% to 89.4% while training accuracy was only 93%, a sign the model was still underfitting. I would check the training curve, then train longer (and possibly apply CutMix with a probability below 1, or turn it off for the last epochs), and compare at equal, adequate budgets. I would also check calibration and robustness, where mixing methods often help even when top-1 accuracy is flat, and make sure it was implemented with the mixed labels, since mixing images without mixing labels teaches the wrong targets.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Augmentation teaches invariances: random crops and flips took the CNN from 87.6% to 91.1% on CIFAR-10.
  • Useful augmentations lower training accuracy and raise test accuracy; the gap fell from 12.4 to 5.2 points with cutout.
  • Mixup and CutMix need long training: at 30 epochs they cost 0.9 and 1.7 points.
  • The label must survive: vertical flips cost 2.2 points and ±180° rotations 12.4, but made the model robust to upside-down images (88.6% vs 42.5%).
  • Flip TTA added about a point at twice the inference cost.
  • Augmentation matters most with little data: +11.3 points on 5,000 images.
