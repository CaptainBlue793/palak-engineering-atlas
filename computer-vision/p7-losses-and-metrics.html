Losses & Metrics for Classifiers

A classifier ends in a vector of scores, one per class. Training needs one number to push down, the loss; people need numbers that say how good the model is, the metrics. They are not the same thing, and the gap between them is where many evaluation mistakes start.

This chapter goes from logits to the metrics every later chapter reports, using a small CNN trained for this chapter on CIFAR-10 (77.7% accuracy, so it makes plenty of interesting mistakes). It measures where the loss actually comes from, which classes the model confuses, what precision and recall say that accuracy hides, and how a 90%-accurate model can find no cats at all.

  • Report accuracy and top-k accuracy, and read a confusion matrix
  • Compute precision, recall and F1 per class, and their macro average
  • Pick a decision threshold, and read precision–recall and ROC curves
  • Spot the accuracy paradox on imbalanced data

Logits, softmax and its overflow

The last layer of a classifier outputs logits: one unbounded real number per class. Softmax turns them into probabilities, pi = ezi / Σj ezj, which are positive and sum to 1. Written literally it overflows: e1000 is infinity in float32, and infinity divided by infinity is NaN. Every library subtracts the largest logit first, which changes nothing mathematically and keeps every exponent at most 0.

z = torch.tensor([1000., 0., -5.])
z.exp() / z.exp().sum()                     # tensor([nan, 0., 0.]): e^1000 overflowed
z.softmax(-1)                                # tensor([1., 0., 0.]): computed as exp(z - max(z))
F.cross_entropy(z[None], torch.tensor([1]))   # 1000.0: exact, via logsumexp
-torch.log(z.softmax(-1)[1])               # inf: p underflowed to 0 before the log

The last two lines are the reason to always pass logits to F.cross_entropy: it computes log-softmax in one stable step (log py = zy − logsumexp(z)) and gets exactly 1000 where the two-step version returns infinity.

Cross-entropy: the loss

Cross-entropy for one image is −log py, the negative log of the probability given to the right class. It is 0 when the model is certain and right, ln 10 = 2.30 for a uniform guess over ten classes, and grows without limit for confident mistakes. The training loss is its average over a batch. On the 10,000 CIFAR-10 test images the trained CNN’s mean was 0.662, but that average is dominated by a few images: the median image cost 0.126, the 90th percentile 2.16, the worst 13.8, and the 5% most costly images made up 34.3% of the total loss. Cross-entropy punishes confident errors very hard, which is what makes it a good training signal and also why a handful of mislabelled images can pull a model around (Chapter 39).

Accuracy, top-k and the confusion matrix

Accuracy is the share of images whose highest-scoring class is the true one: 77.67% here. Top-k accuracy counts an image as right if the true class is anywhere in the k highest scores; top-3 was 95.13%. ImageNet results are traditionally reported as top-1 and top-5, because with 1,000 classes, many of them near-duplicates (dozens of dog breeds), the second guess is often a fair answer.

Precision, recall and F1

For one class, call a prediction of that class a positive. Precision is the share of positives that are right: of the images the model called “cat”, how many were cats? Recall is the share of real members that were found: of the real cats, how many did it call “cat”? F1 is their harmonic mean, 2PR / (P + R), which is low if either is low.

Cat and dog are mirror images: the model leans toward “dog” for anything furry, so dog recall is high and its precision low, and cat the reverse. Accuracy says neither. The macro F1, the plain average of the ten per-class F1 scores, was 0.775; on a balanced test set like this it sits close to accuracy, and on an imbalanced one it does not, because it weights rare classes equally.

Thresholds and the accuracy paradox

Picking the highest score is one decision rule. Many real systems ask a yes/no question instead (“is there a cat?”, “is this a defect?”) and must pick a threshold on the score. A low threshold finds more cats (higher recall) and raises more false alarms (lower precision); a high one the reverse. The precision–recall curve traces that trade-off over all thresholds, and its area, average precision, was 0.713 for cats. The ROC curve plots true-positive rate against false-positive rate; its area (AUC) was 0.943, which sounds much better, because the 9,000 non-cats make false-positive rates look small. On rare classes, read the precision–recall curve.

Finally the accuracy paradox. Cats are 10% of this test set, so a “detector” that answers “not a cat” for every image is 90.0% accurate, while finding zero cats. In a defect-detection line with 0.1% defects, the do-nothing model is 99.9% accurate. Accuracy is only meaningful next to the class balance; for rare positives report precision and recall (or average precision), and state the base rate.

🧾
Use case: choosing the threshold for a content filter

An image moderation service scores every upload for, say, violent content. A false negative lets a harmful image through; a false positive blocks a legitimate post and costs a human review. Such services commonly run two thresholds: above a high one, act automatically (high precision); between the two, queue for human review; below the low one, allow. The thresholds are chosen from the precision–recall curve on recent, labelled traffic, against the review team’s capacity, and re-checked when the traffic or the model changes. No single accuracy figure can make that decision.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Computing softmax by hand and then log, so confident predictions give inf loss. Pass logits to F.cross_entropy.
  • Reporting accuracy on imbalanced data without the base rate: 90% can mean “found nothing”.
  • Quoting ROC AUC for a rare class where the precision–recall curve tells the real story.
  • Averaging per-class metrics without saying how (macro, weighted, micro give different numbers).
  • Treating 0.5 as the natural threshold instead of choosing one from the costs of each error.
  • Optimising the metric you report on the test set, including the threshold. Tune it on validation data.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: A defect detector reports 99% accuracy. Is it good?

How a strong answer sounds
It depends on the base rate, and 99% might be useless. If 1% of parts are defective, a model that always says “fine” is 99% accurate and finds no defects. I would ask for the defect rate in the test set, then precision and recall for the defect class at the chosen threshold, or the precision–recall curve and its average precision. Then I would ask what each error costs: a missed defect shipped to a customer versus a good part sent for manual inspection, and set the threshold from that. In this chapter a “not a cat” rule was 90% accurate on a 10%-cats test set while finding zero cats, the same paradox at a smaller scale.

Deeper: Why train with cross-entropy rather than directly maximising accuracy?

How a strong answer sounds
Accuracy is piecewise constant in the weights: nudging a weight almost never changes which class wins, so its gradient is zero almost everywhere and gradient descent cannot use it. Cross-entropy is smooth and keeps giving a signal even on images already classified correctly (pushing the right class’s probability up) and especially on confident mistakes. It is also the negative log-likelihood of the labels, so minimising it gives probabilities that tend to be calibrated, which matters for thresholds. The cost is sensitivity to a few very wrong images: here the 5% most costly test images accounted for 34% of the loss, so mislabelled data or outliers can dominate it, which is one motivation for label smoothing and robust losses.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Softmax must subtract the max logit; cross-entropy should be computed from logits (exactly 1000 where the two-step version gave infinity).
  • Cross-entropy is −log p(true class): 2.30 for a ten-way guess, unbounded for confident errors. 5% of test images made 34% of the loss.
  • Accuracy 77.7%, top-3 95.1%: report both when classes are similar.
  • The confusion matrix shows which mistakes: cat recall 0.49, dog precision 0.61, because furry animals go to “dog”.
  • A threshold trades precision for recall; choose it from the cost of each error. Average precision 0.71 vs ROC AUC 0.94 for the same cat detector.
  • A rule that never says “cat” is 90% accurate here. Always give the base rate with accuracy.

Check your understanding

What does exp(z) / exp(z).sum() return for logits [1000, 0, −5] in float32?

e^1000 overflows to infinity, and infinity / infinity is NaN. torch.softmax subtracts the max first and returns [1, 0, 0].

The model called 1,000 images “dog”, and 610 of them were dogs. Which metric is 0.61?

Precision is the share of predicted positives that are correct. Recall would be the share of real dogs that were found (0.83 here).

Cats are 10% of a test set. What accuracy does “always answer not-cat” get on the cat-vs-rest task?

It is right on all 9,000 non-cats and wrong on 1,000 cats: 90%, with a recall of zero. That is the accuracy paradox.

Lowering the threshold of a cat detector usually does what?

More images are called cats: more real cats are found, and more non-cats are wrongly included.

Why is cross-entropy used for training instead of accuracy?

Small weight changes rarely flip a prediction, so accuracy does not change and gives no gradient. Cross-entropy changes smoothly with every probability.
