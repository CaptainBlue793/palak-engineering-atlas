One-Stage Detectors: YOLO, RetinaNet & Anchor-Free

A one-stage detector drops the proposal step: one network predicts a class score and a box at every position of every feature map, and NMS cleans up. It is simpler and usually faster than Faster R-CNN, and for years it was clearly less accurate, for a reason that turned out to be one line in the loss.

That reason is imbalance. Measured on RetinaNet’s own anchors over 50 COCO images, there were 1,067 background anchors for every one on an object, and with plain cross-entropy 85% of the loss came from easy background the model already got right. Focal loss flips that: the objects get 74% of it. This chapter measures that, explains anchor-free and YOLO-style heads, and compares five detectors on the same 500 COCO images.

Dense prediction

RetinaNet uses the same ResNet-50 + FPN backbone as Faster R-CNN, then attaches two small convolutional heads to every pyramid level: one predicts, for each of 9 anchors at each position, a score for each of the 80 classes (with a sigmoid per class, not a softmax); the other predicts 4 box offsets per anchor. Every position is a classifier and a box regressor, so the whole image is processed in a single dense pass with no cropping, no proposals and no second stage.

# RetinaNet head, per pyramid level (A = 9 anchors per position, K = 80 classes)
cls_head = nn.Sequential(*[conv3x3_relu(256, 256) for _ in range(4)], nn.Conv2d(256, A * K, 3, padding=1))
box_head = nn.Sequential(*[conv3x3_relu(256, 256) for _ in range(4)], nn.Conv2d(256, A * 4, 3, padding=1))
# the same two heads (shared weights) run on P3..P7: 168,863 anchors for one 800 px COCO image
cls_head[-1].bias.data.fill_(-math.log((1 - 0.01) / 0.01))   # prior: every anchor starts at p = 0.01

The last line is part of the fix for imbalance: initialising the classification bias so that every anchor starts out predicting a probability of 0.01 for every class, instead of 0.5, stops the huge number of background anchors from producing an enormous loss in the first iterations.

The imbalance, measured

On 50 COCO validation images, every RetinaNet anchor was labelled as in training: foreground if its IoU with a ground-truth box is at least 0.5, background below 0.4, ignored in between. Then the per-anchor classification loss of the pretrained model was split by kind, with plain binary cross-entropy and with focal loss:

Each easy background anchor contributes a tiny loss, but there are over eight million of them, so together they dominate: with cross-entropy, 85% of the training signal says “this sky is not a car”, which the model already knows. Two-stage detectors avoid this because the RPN and its NMS discard most background before the classifier sees it, and they sample a fixed ratio of positives to negatives. One-stage detectors before RetinaNet used hard-negative mining (train only on the worst background anchors). RetinaNet changed the loss instead.

Focal loss

For a prediction with probability pt of the true label, cross-entropy is −log pt. Focal loss multiplies it by (1 − pt)γ: −α (1 − pt)γ log pt. When an example is already well classified (pt near 1) the factor is near 0, and the loss vanishes; for a hard example (pt small) it is near 1 and the loss is almost unchanged. With γ = 2, an anchor at pt = 0.9 contributes 100 times less than with cross-entropy, one at 0.99, 10,000 times less. The panel lets you change γ and see the curve and the measured split of the loss.

def focal_loss(logits, targets, alpha=0.25, gamma=2.0):            # (A, K) logits, (A, K) 0/1 targets
    p = logits.sigmoid()
    ce = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(logits, targets, reduction="none")
    p_t = p * targets + (1 - p) * (1 - targets)                      # probability of the true label
    a_t = alpha * targets + (1 - alpha) * (1 - targets)
    return (a_t * (1 - p_t) ** gamma * ce).sum() / max(1, num_foreground) # = torchvision.ops.sigmoid_focal_loss

Anchor-free and YOLO-style heads

FCOS (2019) removes anchors. Every feature-map position inside a ground-truth box is a positive for that box and predicts four distances, to the box’s left, top, right and bottom edges, plus a “centre-ness” score that down-weights positions near the edge, whose boxes tend to be poor. Objects are assigned to pyramid levels by size. No anchor sizes, ratios or IoU matching to tune, and one prediction per position instead of nine. CenterNet goes further: one heat-map peak per object at its centre, plus a size, and no NMS needed for well-separated objects.

YOLO (2016) started as a single grid: divide the image into 7 × 7 cells, and each cell predicts a few boxes and one class distribution. Later versions added anchors fitted to the dataset by k-means (v2), multi-scale feature pyramids (v3), heavy augmentation such as mosaic (v4, v5), and anchor-free decoupled heads (YOLOX, v8 onwards). The family’s defining goal is real-time speed on modest hardware, traded carefully against accuracy (Case Study 43). YOLO models are not in torchvision, so the comparison below uses torchvision’s one-stage detectors.

At equal backbones (ResNet-50 + FPN at 800 px), the one-stage models were about 20% faster and 6–9 AP behind the v2 two-stage detector here, though RetinaNet matched it on small objects. The SSD models resize to 300–320 pixels, which makes them fast and almost blind to small objects (2.5–7.8 AP small). Their batch-1 times on this GPU are dominated by fixed overheads rather than compute, so the speed gap to RetinaNet is much smaller than the FLOP gap (Chapter 12 again); on a phone the SSDlite model’s 3.4 million parameters are the point.

Use case: real-time detection on a factory line

Inspection cameras on a production line typically need decisions at 30 or more frames per second on an edge GPU, for a handful of classes (part present, part missing, defect type). Teams usually choose a one-stage detector from the YOLO family or similar, train it at the camera’s native resolution, and spend their effort on the data: balanced examples of rare defects, augmentation for lighting changes, and a per-class confidence threshold chosen from the precision–recall curve (Chapter 16). The focal-loss idea matters here too: defects are rare, so most of the image, and most of the anchors, are background.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Training a dense detector with plain cross-entropy and no sampling: 85% of the loss here was easy background.
  • Initialising the classification bias at 0 (p = 0.5 for every anchor), so the first steps produce a huge, unstable loss.
  • Normalising focal loss by the number of anchors instead of the number of foreground anchors.
  • Picking a detector by its overall AP when the task is small objects: SSD scored 7.8 AP on small objects.
  • Comparing speed at different input resolutions as if it were the architecture.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What problem does focal loss solve, and how?

How a strong answer sounds
A one-stage detector classifies every anchor in the image, and almost all of them are background: measured on RetinaNet over 50 COCO images, 1,067 background anchors per foreground one. Each easy background anchor has a small cross-entropy loss, but there are millions, so they dominate the total (85% of it here) and the gradient mostly teaches the model what it already knows. Focal loss multiplies cross-entropy by (1 − p_t)^γ, so well-classified examples contribute almost nothing: with γ = 2 an example at p_t = 0.9 counts 100 times less. On the same anchors the foreground share of the loss went from 5% to 74%. Together with an α weight, a prior on the classification bias (start at p = 0.01) and normalising by the number of foreground anchors, it let RetinaNet match two-stage accuracy without proposals or hard-negative mining.

Deeper: How does an anchor-free detector like FCOS assign training targets without anchors?

How a strong answer sounds
Every feature-map location whose position falls inside a ground-truth box is a candidate positive for that box, and it regresses the four distances from itself to the box’s edges. To avoid ambiguity, objects are assigned to pyramid levels by size (each level handles a range of maximum regression distances), and when a location falls inside several boxes it takes the smallest. Locations near a box’s edge produce poor boxes, so a centre-ness branch predicts how close the location is to the box centre, and its output multiplies the classification score at test time so NMS keeps the central predictions. The benefits are no anchor hyperparameters and one prediction per location; on these 500 COCO images FCOS scored 42.8 AP against RetinaNet’s 44.8 (the RetinaNet weights here use a newer training recipe), at similar speed.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • One-stage detectors predict classes and boxes densely at every position, with no proposal stage.
  • The catch is imbalance: 1,067 background anchors per object; with cross-entropy, easy background made 85% of the loss.
  • Focal loss down-weights easy examples by (1 − p_t)^γ; the foreground share rose from 5% to 74%.
  • FCOS predicts distances to box edges from points, no anchors; YOLO trades accuracy for real-time speed by design.
  • Here two-stage v2 led on AP (51.3); RetinaNet and FCOS were about 20% faster at 43–45; low-resolution SSDs nearly missed small objects.

Check your understanding

With plain cross-entropy, where did most of RetinaNet’s classification loss come from?

85.3%: each easy background anchor has a tiny loss, but there were 8.4 million of them in 50 images.

With γ = 2, how much less does an example with p_t = 0.9 contribute under focal loss than under cross-entropy?

(1 − 0.9)² = 0.01: a hundredth of its cross-entropy.

Why is RetinaNet’s classification bias initialised so that p = 0.01?

At p = 0.5 every background anchor would start with a large loss, destabilising early training.

What does an FCOS location regress instead of anchor offsets?

Plus a centre-ness score that down-weights predictions from near the box edges.

Why did SSDlite score only 2.5 AP on small objects?

Resizing a 640-pixel image to 320 halves every object; a 20-pixel object becomes 10 pixels, below what its features resolve.
