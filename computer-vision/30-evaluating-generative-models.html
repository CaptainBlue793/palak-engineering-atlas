Evaluating Generative Models

A classifier has a right answer for every test image. A generator does not: there is no correct new picture of a horse. What we can ask is whether its samples, taken together, look like they came from the same distribution as real images: each one realistic (fidelity) and the whole set as varied as the real data (diversity). The standard tools compare the two distributions in the feature space of a pretrained network, and each one has blind spots that change what you should conclude.

This chapter writes FID from scratch, uses it to score the GAN and diffusion samples trained in Chapters 27 and 28, and then attacks it: real images with noise, blur, a single class, or just 100 images copied 100 times. The diffusion model scored an FID of 17.8 against the GAN’s 43.4, and cutting its sampler from 1,000 to 10 steps raised FID to 45.8 while precision went up. Adding noise of 10 grey levels to real photos, barely visible, gave a worse FID (51.2) than the GAN; and two halves of the same real data scored 72.4 at 500 images each.

Why likelihood is not enough

Some generators, VAEs and diffusion models among them, can report a likelihood or a bound on it: how probable the test images are under the model. It is a principled number and a poor guide to what people see. A model can achieve good likelihood while producing poor samples, by spending its probability mass on imperceptible pixel noise, and a model with excellent samples can have poor likelihood. GANs have no likelihood at all. And per-image scores like PSNR need a reference image, which generation does not have. So evaluation moved to comparing sets of images in a feature space where distance means something perceptual.

FID: Fréchet Inception Distance

FID (2017) passes real and generated images through Inception-v3, trained on ImageNet, and takes the 2,048-dimensional output of its final pooling layer. It fits a Gaussian to each set of features, a mean vector μ and a covariance matrix Σ, and measures the Fréchet (Wasserstein-2) distance between the two Gaussians:

FID = ‖μr − μg‖² + Tr( Σr + Σg − 2 (Σr Σg)½ )

The first term catches a shift in the average image (wrong colours, wrong content), the second a difference in spread and shape (too little variety, or variety of the wrong kind). Lower is better; 0 means identical feature statistics.

net = inception_v3(weights=Inception_V3_Weights.IMAGENET1K_V1).eval(); net.fc = nn.Identity()      # 2,048-d pool features
def feats(u8):                                                        # uint8 images -> (N, 2048)
    x = F.interpolate(u8.float() / 255, size=(299, 299), mode="bilinear")
    return net((x - MEAN) / STD).double()
def fid(f1, f2):
    m1, m2, s1, s2 = f1.mean(0), f2.mean(0), np.cov(f1, rowvar=False), np.cov(f2, rowvar=False)
    cs = scipy.linalg.sqrtm(s1 @ s2).real                                       # matrix square root
    return ((m1 - m2) ** 2).sum() + np.trace(s1 + s2 - 2 * cs)

Two warnings before using it. FID numbers are only comparable when computed the same way: the official implementation uses a specific TensorFlow Inception graph and resizing, and torchvision’s weights give somewhat different absolute values (the ones here are internally consistent, not comparable with papers). And FID is biased by sample count: estimating a 2,048 × 2,048 covariance from a few thousand images is noisy, and the noise always adds distance.

The bias is easy to measure: compute FID between two disjoint sets of real CIFAR-10 training images, which come from exactly the same distribution, so the true answer is 0.

At 500 images per set, identical distributions scored 72, worse than the GAN below at 10,000. The bias shrinks roughly in proportion to 1/n, which is why papers report FID at 10,000 or 50,000 samples, and why two FIDs computed at different counts cannot be compared at all. The 10,000 training images against the 10,000 test images scored 4.98, the floor for this setup.

Precision and recall for generators

FID folds fidelity and diversity into one number. Precision and recall (2019) separate them using nearest neighbours in the same feature space. Around every real image draw a ball reaching its 3rd nearest real neighbour; the union of balls approximates the region real images occupy. Precision is the share of generated images that land inside the real region: how many samples look real. Recall is the share of real images that land inside the generated region, built the same way from the samples: how much of the real variety the generator covers. A collapsed generator scores high precision and low recall; a noisy one the reverse.

The diffusion model was far closer to the real distribution than the GAN: FID 17.8 against 43.4, after 45 minutes of training against 72 seconds, so this is not a fair race, but it is the same ordering the field found. Precision and recall show where the difference lies. Both generators’ samples looked real about equally often (precision 68%, the same as real images’ 70% under this k = 3 test), but the GAN covered less than half as much of the real variety (recall 23.8% against 51.6%): the diversity failure of Chapter 27, now measured on images. Fewer DDIM steps made FID worse steadily, 17.8 to 45.8, and the split shows why: precision rose to 75.8% while recall fell to 30%. Short samplers produce simpler, more typical images, which are more often inside the real distribution and cover less of it. FID alone would only say “worse”. The 2,000-sample column ranks all models identically but every value is 18–20 points higher, the sample-count bias again.

What FID notices, and what it misses

To see how FID behaves, it helps to feed it sets of real images that are wrong in a known way.

FID is very sensitive to texture statistics that people barely notice: faint grain of 10 grey levels gave an FID of 51, worse than the GAN, and a mild blur gave 84. Inception features respond strongly to high-frequency content, so FID punishes noise and blur heavily, more than human judges would. It also catches missing diversity: one class of perfect real images scored 78, and 100 images copied 100 times scored 139, the worst of all, although every image is a real photo. Precision and recall separate the two kinds of failure cleanly. Blur kept precision at 68% (blurred images still sit near real ones in feature space) but collapsed recall to 9.5%; the copies kept precision at 62% and had recall exactly 0. One number cannot tell “unrealistic” from “not varied”; two can.

Text-to-image: CLIP score and people

For text-to-image models, matching the training distribution is not the goal; matching the prompt is. CLIP score (used in Chapter 29) is 100 × the cosine similarity between CLIP’s embeddings of the image and of its prompt, averaged over a prompt set. It rewards alignment and is blind to everything CLIP is blind to: counting, spatial relations, and text drawn in the image that matches the prompt. Benchmarks such as GenEval and T2I-CompBench test those specific skills with detectors and VQA models, and in the end leading labs rely on human preference studies, side-by-side comparisons on hundreds of prompts, because no automatic metric tracks what people prefer closely enough. The practical stack is: FID or a variant (often on better features such as DINOv2, called FDDINOv2) for distribution match, precision and recall to diagnose it, CLIP score or a compositional benchmark for prompt adherence, and people for the final call.

Use case: deciding whether a cheaper sampler can ship

A team wants to cut its image generator from 50 to 20 sampling steps to halve serving cost. The decision procedure is this chapter in miniature: generate 10,000 or more images with both settings from the same prompts and seeds, compute FID against a held-out real set with the same feature extractor and sample count each time, check precision and recall to see whether the change costs fidelity or diversity, check prompt adherence with CLIP score or a compositional benchmark, and run a blind side-by-side preference test on a few hundred prompts. A change that moves FID by less than its run-to-run noise, measured by repeating with different seeds, is not a change.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Comparing FIDs computed with different sample counts: two sets of the same real images scored 72.4 at 500 each and 4.9 at 10,000.
  • Comparing FIDs from different implementations or resizing methods; the absolute numbers differ.
  • Reading FID as fidelity alone: 100 real photos copied 100 times scored 139, worse than any generator here; FID mixes realism and variety.
  • Computing FID against the training set for a model that may memorise it; use held-out images and check nearest neighbours for copies.
  • Trusting CLIP score for counts, positions or text in images.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What does FID measure?

How a strong answer sounds
It compares two sets of images, real and generated, in the feature space of an ImageNet-trained Inception network. Each set’s 2,048-dimensional features are summarised by a mean and a covariance, and FID is the Fréchet distance between the two Gaussians: the squared distance between the means plus a term for how different the covariances are. Lower means the generated set’s statistics are closer to the real one’s, which reflects both realism and variety. It needs many samples, typically 10,000 or 50,000, because it is biased upwards when the covariance is estimated from few. I measured the bias directly: two disjoint halves of CIFAR-10’s training set gave FID 72 at 500 images each and 4.9 at 10,000. Scoring my own models at 10,000 samples, a DDPM got 17.8 and a DCGAN 43.4.

Deeper: Two models have the same FID. How would you tell them apart?

How a strong answer sounds
FID mixes fidelity and diversity, so I would compute precision and recall in the same feature space: one model may produce very realistic but less varied images, the other varied but less realistic ones. I would check the run-to-run spread of FID with different seeds to see if they are actually distinguishable, look at nearest training neighbours of samples for memorisation, evaluate per class or per prompt category to find where each fails, try a different feature space such as DINOv2 because Inception features are ImageNet-biased, and for text-to-image add prompt-adherence benchmarks and a human preference study. In my measurements, a 10-step DDIM sampler and a DCGAN had similar FIDs (45.8 and 43.4) for opposite reasons: the sampler had the highest precision of all (75.8%) and low recall, the GAN ordinary precision and the lowest recall (23.8%).

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Generators are evaluated as distributions: compare real and generated feature statistics, not individual images.
  • FID is the Fréchet distance between Gaussians fitted to Inception features; lower is better.
  • FID is biased upwards at small sample counts: identical distributions scored 72 at 500 images and 5 at 10,000.
  • Measured: DDPM 17.8, DCGAN 43.4 (recall 52% vs 24%); fewer DDIM steps raised precision and cut recall; faint noise alone scored 51.
  • For text-to-image, add CLIP score or compositional benchmarks, and human preference.

Check your understanding

What does FID compare?

A Gaussian is fitted to each set’s 2,048-d features and the Fréchet distance between them is computed.

Why must FIDs be compared at the same sample count?

Two halves of the same real data give a non-zero FID that shrinks as n grows.

A generator that has collapsed onto a few kinds of image scores…

Its samples look real (inside the real region) but cover little of the real variety.

What does CLIP score measure for a text-to-image model?

It is alignment only, and it inherits CLIP’s blind spots such as counting.

Why are published FID numbers hard to compare with your own?

Compare within one implementation and protocol, never across.
