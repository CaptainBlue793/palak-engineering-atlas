Training ViTs: Data, DeiT & ViT vs CNN

The first Vision Transformer only beat CNNs after pre-training on 300 million images; trained on ImageNet alone, it lost to a ResNet of similar size. A year later DeiT trained a ViT on ImageNet alone to 81.8% by changing the recipe, not the model. Both facts come from the same cause: a ViT assumes almost nothing about images, so it must learn from data what a CNN gets for free.

This chapter measures that directly. A small ViT and a ResNet-18 are trained from scratch on 5,000, 10,000, 25,000 and 50,000 CIFAR-10 images, with (nearly) the same number of optimiser steps for each size. The ResNet won everywhere, but its lead shrank from 23.4 points to 12.0 as data grew. Then the DeiT recipe and the patch size are tested on the full set.

Inductive bias: what a CNN assumes

An inductive bias is an assumption built into a model that lets it generalise from fewer examples. A convolution makes three about images: locality (a 3 × 3 kernel only looks at neighbours), weight sharing (the same detector is applied everywhere), and through these, translation equivariance (a cat moved 10 pixels gives feature maps moved 10 pixels). Pooling adds a little translation invariance and the pyramid builds a hierarchy from edges to parts to objects.

A ViT keeps almost none of this. Only the patch embedding is local; after it, every token can attend to every other from block 1, and the position embeddings start random, so the model does not even know which patches are neighbours until it learns it. That is more flexible, and with enough data it is an advantage, because the model is not stuck with assumptions that are only roughly true. With little data, the model has to spend its examples learning what a convolution was handed.

ViT vs ResNet-18 by dataset size

Both models trained from scratch on random subsets of CIFAR-10’s training set and tested on all 10,000 test images. The ViT is the Chapter 19 code at a small size: 32 × 32 input, 4 × 4 patches (64 tokens plus the class token), width 192, 6 blocks, 3 heads, 2.69 M parameters. ResNet-18, adapted to 32 × 32 (3 × 3 stem, no max-pool), has 11.17 M, about four times as many. Each subset got the optimiser steps of 60 epochs of the full set, about 11,700, so that smaller sets are not short-changed on training; the one exception is the 5,000-image set, whose epochs were capped at 300, about 5,900 steps, so its numbers are slightly pessimistic for both models. Both used random crops and flips; the ViT AdamW (lr 1e-3, weight decay 0.05, 10% warm-up), the ResNet SGD (lr 0.1), each the usual choice for its family.

The CNN wins at every size, which is the honest result at this scale: 50,000 small images are far from the regime where ViTs overtake CNNs. But the shape is the point. Going from 5,000 to 50,000 images added 25.3 points to the ViT and 13.9 to the ResNet: the ViT has more to gain from data because it starts with less built in. The original ViT paper saw the curves cross only at ImageNet-21k (14 M images) to JFT-300M scale. The ViT also trained in about 30% less time for the same steps, so its loss is not for lack of compute.

DeiT: closing the gap with the recipe

DeiT (Data-efficient image Transformers, 2021) trained ViT-B on ImageNet-1k alone to 81.8% top-1 against the original ViT’s 77.9%, with the same architecture. The changes were all training: RandAugment, Mixup and CutMix, random erasing, stochastic depth, label smoothing, AdamW with a batch of 1,024 and weight decay 0.05, over 300 epochs. (DeiT also distilled from a CNN teacher through an extra token, which pushed it higher, but the recipe alone did most of the work.) The heavy augmentation plays the role of the missing inductive bias: if the model is shown a hundred mixed and cut versions of every image, it cannot memorise them and has to learn what generalises.

The core of that recipe on the full CIFAR-10 set (half of batches Mixup with α = 0.8, half CutMix with α = 1, plus label smoothing 0.1):

The recipe was worth 3.7 points at the same 60 epochs and 8.9 points with a longer schedule, which is the DeiT lesson in miniature: mixing makes every epoch harder to memorise, which is what lets a longer schedule keep paying. This experiment did not run the plain recipe for 200 epochs, so part of the 8.9 points belongs to the schedule itself. It did not close the gap. The ViT at 200 epochs with the full recipe was still 3.1 points behind a ResNet-18 trained for 60 epochs with crops and flips, at more than three times the steps. At 50,000 small images the CNN’s built-in assumptions are worth more than any recipe tested here. (A repeat of the plain 60-epoch ViT, run for the patch-size table below, scored 82.35% against 82.03%: differences of a few tenths are noise.)

Patch size: tokens against detail

The patch size sets the token count, and with it both detail and cost. At 32 × 32 input:

Patch sizeTokensEach token coversTest accuracy (60 epochs)Train time
8 × 8176% of the image77.31%91 s
4 × 4651.6%82.35%158 s
2 × 22570.4%79.98%896 s

The parameter count barely changes (2.69–2.72 M, only the patch projection and position table differ), but compute does. Going from 8 to 4 pixels quadrupled the tokens, gained 5 points and cost 1.7 times the time. Going to 2 pixels quadrupled them again and cost 5.7 times as long, and accuracy fell by 2.4 points: with 257 tokens of 12 numbers each, every token carries very little, attention has to assemble objects from many more pieces, and 60 epochs were not enough to learn it. Smaller patches are not free detail; they move work from the patch embedding into attention, at quadratic cost. At ImageNet resolution the sweet spot is usually 14–16 pixels for the same reason.

What to do in practice

Almost nobody trains a ViT from scratch on a small dataset. The standard path is to start from weights pre-trained on a large dataset (ImageNet-21k, or self-supervised as in Chapters 22–23, or CLIP in Chapter 24) and fine-tune, which is where ViTs’ appetite for data becomes an asset: they absorb the big pre-training set better than CNNs, and transfer well. If you must train from scratch on tens of thousands of images, a CNN or a hybrid with convolutional early layers is the better default, and if it must be a ViT, use the DeiT recipe and long schedules. The attention maps of a well-trained ViT (Chapter 19) also show what it learned: early blocks attend locally, as if they had rediscovered convolution, and later blocks globally.

Use case: picking a backbone for a medical-imaging start-up

A team with 20,000 labelled X-rays has to choose a backbone. This chapter’s measurement predicts what happens if they train from scratch: a ResNet will beat a ViT at that size. Their real choice is between pre-trained models: a ResNet or ConvNeXt pre-trained on ImageNet, or a ViT pre-trained on a large dataset (often self-supervised on unlabelled scans, Chapter 23). Fine-tuned, the pre-trained ViT usually matches or beats the CNN, and it plugs into multimodal report-generation models. The deciding tests are the ones from Chapter 9: accuracy on a held-out hospital, calibration and per-subgroup recall, measured on their own data rather than assumed from ImageNet leaderboards.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Training a ViT from scratch on a small dataset with a CNN recipe (SGD, light augmentation, 90 epochs) and concluding ViTs do not work.
  • Comparing models at equal epochs on different dataset sizes, which gives small sets far fewer steps; compare at (close to) equal steps, as here.
  • Forgetting warm-up and weight decay for ViTs: AdamW with a warm-up is close to required for stable training.
  • Using small patches for speed-insensitive work without checking cost: tokens grow with the inverse square of the patch size.
  • Reading one run’s half-point difference as a result: one seed per setting is noisy.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Why do ViTs need more data than CNNs?

How a strong answer sounds
A CNN builds in assumptions about images: locality, the same detector everywhere, and a hierarchy from small to large features, so it does not have to learn them. A ViT only has the patch embedding as a local operation; attention is global from the first block and even the neighbour relations between patches are learned through position embeddings. That flexibility pays off with very large datasets, but with small ones the model spends its data learning what a convolution gets for free. I measured it on CIFAR-10 with matched step budgets: a ResNet-18 beat a small ViT by 23.4 points at 5,000 images and by 12.0 at 50,000, so the ViT gained much more from extra data. In practice you fix this with pre-training or a heavy augmentation recipe like DeiT’s.

Deeper: What did DeiT change to train a ViT on ImageNet-1k alone, and why does it work?

How a strong answer sounds
Not the architecture: the recipe. Strong data augmentation (RandAugment, random erasing), Mixup and CutMix, label smoothing, stochastic depth, AdamW with weight decay 0.05 and warm-up, and a long 300-epoch schedule, plus optional distillation from a CNN teacher through a distillation token. The augmentations and regularisers act as a substitute for the inductive bias the model lacks: by never showing the same image twice in the same form, they stop the model from memorising and push it towards features that generalise, and the long schedule gives it time to learn them. On CIFAR-10 I saw the same effect at small scale: Mixup, CutMix and label smoothing lifted a small ViT from 82.0% to 85.7% at 60 epochs and to 90.9% at 200, though it stayed below a ResNet-18, so I would still start from pre-trained weights when I could.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • CNNs build in locality, weight sharing and a hierarchy; ViTs must learn them from data.
  • With matched step budgets, ResNet-18 beat a small ViT by 23.4 points at 5k CIFAR images and by 12.0 at 50k.
  • The DeiT-style recipe (Mixup, CutMix, label smoothing, longer training) lifted the ViT from 82.0% to 90.9%, still 3.1 points short of ResNet-18.
  • Patch 4 beat both 8 and 2 on 32-pixel images; patch 2 cost 5.7× the time and lost 2.4 points.
  • In practice: pre-trained ViT and fine-tune, or a CNN when training from scratch on small data.

Check your understanding

Which inductive bias does a CNN have that a ViT largely lacks?

Both use residuals and normalisation. Only the CNN restricts each layer to a small neighbourhood; ViT attention is global from block 1.

What happened to ResNet-18’s lead over the ViT as the training set grew from 5k to 50k images?

The ViT gained 25.3 points from 10× more data, the ResNet 13.9: the ViT has more to learn from data.

Why were the smaller subsets trained for more epochs?

At equal epochs, 5,000 images would get a tenth of the updates of 50,000, mixing up “less data” with “less training”. (Here the 5,000 set was capped at 300 epochs, half the steps of the others.)

What was DeiT’s main change relative to the original ViT?

Same ViT-B architecture, ImageNet-1k only: 81.8% against the original’s 77.9%.

Halving the patch size at a fixed input resolution multiplies the number of tokens by about…

Patches per side double, so tokens quadruple; attention cost then grows about 16×.
