CLIP & Zero-Shot Vision

Every classifier so far had a fixed list of classes built into its last layer. CLIP (2021) has no class list. It was trained on 400 million image–caption pairs from the web to put an image and a sentence describing it close together in one shared embedding space. To classify, you write the class names as sentences, embed them, and pick the sentence nearest the image. A new class costs a new sentence, not a new dataset.

This chapter measures OpenAI’s CLIP ViT-B/32 and ViT-L/14 zero-shot on four datasets, from everyday objects to satellite images. With no training, ViT-L/14 named 99.9% of Imagenette photos and 93.6% of 37 pet breeds correctly; on satellite land use it managed 63% at best, where a linear probe with labels reached 95.7%. Rewording the prompts moved accuracy by up to 18 points, and writing a wrong class name on a photo made ViT-B/32 change its answer to that name one time in five.

Contrastive image–text training

CLIP has two encoders: an image encoder (a ResNet or ViT) and a text encoder (a transformer over the caption’s tokens). Each produces a vector, which is projected and normalised into a shared space of 512 dimensions (768 for ViT-L/14). Training takes a batch of N image–caption pairs, computes all N × N cosine similarities, and applies the InfoNCE loss of Chapter 22 in both directions: each image must pick out its own caption among the N, and each caption its own image. The negatives are simply the other captions in the batch, and with a batch of 32,768 there are a lot of them.

The difference from SimCLR is the second view. Instead of another augmented crop of the same photo, the “other view” is a sentence written by a person about it. That turns the learned space into one where directions mean things people describe in words: “dog”, “satellite photo”, “blurry”, “at night”. The price is that whatever the web’s captions do not mention, or mention with bias, the model does not learn, or learns with that bias.

Zero-shot classification

A zero-shot classifier is built from text alone. For each class, fill a template such as “a photo of a {class}.”, embed it with the text encoder, and normalise: those vectors are the rows of a linear classifier’s weight matrix. An image is classified by the largest cosine similarity. Nothing is trained.

from transformers import CLIPModel, CLIPProcessor
m = CLIPModel.from_pretrained("openai/clip-vit-base-patch32").eval(); p = CLIPProcessor.from_pretrained("openai/clip-vit-base-patch32")
names = ["tench", "English springer", "garbage truck", ...]
with torch.no_grad():
    W = F.normalize(m.get_text_features(**p(text=[f"a photo of a {n}." for n in names], return_tensors="pt", padding=True)), dim=-1)   # (C, 512)
    e = F.normalize(m.get_image_features(**p(images=photos, return_tensors="pt")), dim=-1)                              # (N, 512)
pred = (e @ W.T).argmax(-1)                                     # nearest class sentence

Because the templates are free text, they can carry knowledge about the domain: “a satellite photo of {}.” for land use, “a photo of a {}, a type of pet.” for breeds. Prompt ensembling averages the text embeddings of several templates per class (“a blurry photo of a {}.”, “a close-up photo of a {}.”, …), which smooths out the quirks of any single wording.

On everyday photos zero-shot CLIP is remarkable: 99% on Imagenette with nothing but ten words, and 93.6% on 37 cat and dog breeds for the larger model. Wording matters most where the classes are fine-grained or the images unusual. On Pets, the template “a photo of a {}, a type of pet.” gained 8.4 points over bare breed names for ViT-B/32, because names like “boxer” and “sphynx” are ambiguous without context. On EuroSAT the generic “a photo of a {}.” made ViT-B/32 worse than bare names (35.9% against 39.7%), and only the satellite-specific template helped; for ViT-L/14 it was worth 18 points over bare names. Satellite tiles are rare in web captions, and the model is far from its training distribution: even its best zero-shot score is 63%. The larger model (428 M parameters against 151 M) was better everywhere.

Zero-shot against linear probes

Zero-shot is convenient, but is it as good as training on labels? Two linear probes on the same frozen image embeddings answer that: one trained on all labelled training images, one on just 4 per class.

The two datasets tell opposite stories. On pet breeds, a zero-shot classifier written from 37 names beat a linear probe trained on 4 labelled photos per breed by 10–22 points and came within 1.5 points of a probe trained on all 3,680 labels: the text encoder already knew what a Bengal cat looks like. On satellite images, 4 labels per class beat the best prompt by 20–29 points, and all labels by 33–51: CLIP’s image features separate land-use classes well (the probe reaches 95.7%), but its text side does not know how to describe them. The features were fine; the names were the weak link. That suggests a practical rule: if zero-shot is poor but a few-shot probe is good, the problem is the prompts or the vocabulary, and a handful of labels fixes it.

Blind spots: text, counting and bias

CLIP learned from captions, and captions often describe the text visible in an image. The result is a model that reads, sometimes in preference to looking. On every third Imagenette image (329 photos), ViT-B/32 was right 99.4% of the time. Writing another class’s name on a white label in the corner, for example “French horn” on a photo of a springer spaniel, changed its answer on 21.6% of the photos, and in 21.9% it answered with the written word. A ResNet-50 trained on ImageNet labels was not fooled once: it was still right on 100% of the labelled photos and never predicted the written word. A supervised classifier never had reason to learn reading; CLIP did, because captions describe the words that appear in pictures.

Other known failures follow from the same training data. CLIP is poor at counting (“three dogs” and “five dogs” embed close together), at spatial relations (“a mug to the left of a laptop”), and at fine-grained or specialist categories that captions rarely name precisely. It also absorbs social biases from web text: the CLIP paper’s own audit found it misclassified photos of people into offensive and crime-related categories at different rates by race and age when such classes were offered. Zero-shot classifiers inherit whatever their class wording implies, so wording is part of the model’s behaviour and must be tested like code.

Use case: searching a photo library by description

Photo apps and stock-image sites let users search “dog on a beach at sunset” without anyone having tagged the photos. The mechanism is CLIP-style: every image is embedded once at upload and stored in a vector index (Chapter 36), and each query is embedded with the text encoder and matched by cosine similarity. The same embeddings drive content moderation (“is this image close to any of these prohibited descriptions?”) and deduplication. The engineering lessons of this chapter apply directly: prompt wording changes results, so the query side is often expanded with templates; specialist domains (medical, satellite) need fine-tuned or domain-trained encoders; and a photo of a sign reading “beach” can rank as a beach, so text in images needs handling.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Using bare class names as prompts for fine-grained classes: on Pets, bare breed names lost 8.4 points (ViT-B/32) against a template with context.
  • Assuming zero-shot works in any domain: on satellite images CLIP’s best zero-shot score was 63%, while 4 labels per class gave 83%.
  • Forgetting to normalise embeddings before taking dot products.
  • Ignoring text in images: a written label can override what the model sees.
  • Using CLIP to classify people into sensitive or loaded categories: its biases are documented.
  • Mixing models’ embeddings: an image embedded with ViT-B/32 and text with ViT-L/14 live in different spaces.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How does CLIP classify an image into classes it was never trained on?

How a strong answer sounds
CLIP was trained on hundreds of millions of image–caption pairs to put each image near its own caption and away from other captions in a shared embedding space, with a contrastive loss in both directions. For zero-shot classification I write each class as a sentence, such as “a photo of a garbage truck.”, embed the sentences with the text encoder, and assign the image to the class whose sentence embedding has the highest cosine similarity with the image embedding. The class sentences are effectively the weights of a linear classifier built from text. Measured, ViT-L/14 reached 99.9% zero-shot on Imagenette and 93.6% on 37 pet breeds, within 1.5 points of a linear probe trained on all the labels; on satellite images it managed only 63%, so zero-shot is not a universal substitute for labels.

Deeper: Your CLIP zero-shot classifier is poor on your domain. What do you try, in order?

How a strong answer sounds
First the cheap things: better prompts with domain context and an ensemble of templates, and checking that class names are words people actually use in captions (“highway” rather than a dataset code). Then a few labelled examples: a linear probe on frozen CLIP features, or using the few-shot examples to adjust the text-derived classifier. In my EuroSAT measurement, a domain template took ViT-L/14 from 45% to 63%, and a linear probe on just 4 labels per class reached 83%, so a few labels beat any prompt there. If the domain is far from web photos, such as medical or industrial images, I would fine-tune the image encoder or switch to a model pre-trained on that domain, and test robustness to text in the image and to distribution shift before deploying.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • CLIP trains an image and a text encoder so matching pairs are close, using InfoNCE over a batch of captions.
  • Zero-shot classification: embed “a photo of a {class}.” for each class and pick the nearest.
  • Measured zero-shot: 99%+ on Imagenette, 93.6% on 37 pet breeds (ViT-L/14), but only 63% on satellite land use.
  • Prompt wording moved accuracy by up to 18 points; domain-specific templates helped most where images were unusual.
  • A written label flipped ViT-B/32’s answer on 21.6% of photos; a supervised ResNet-50 was never fooled.

Check your understanding

What plays the role of the negatives in CLIP’s training?

Each image must pick its own caption out of all N in the batch; the other N − 1 are negatives, and the same holds the other way.

In zero-shot CLIP classification, what are the classifier’s weights?

Nothing is trained; the text encoder writes the classifier.

Why does prompt ensembling help?

Each template’s embedding has noise unrelated to the class; the mean keeps what they share.

What is a typographic attack on CLIP?

Captions often mention visible text, so CLIP learned to read; a handwritten label can win over the object.

Which task is CLIP known to be weak at?

Captions rarely state counts or positions precisely, so “three dogs” and “five dogs” embed close together.
