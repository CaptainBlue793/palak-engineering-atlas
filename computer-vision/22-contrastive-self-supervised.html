Self-Supervised Learning: SimCLR & MoCo

Labels are the expensive part of computer vision. Unlabelled images are nearly free: a factory camera records millions a day, a hospital archives every scan. Self-supervised learning makes up a task that needs no labels, trains a network on it, and keeps the network’s features. The most successful family of the early 2020s, contrastive learning, uses one rule: two random crops of the same photo should map to nearby vectors, and crops of different photos to distant ones.

This chapter writes SimCLR from scratch, about 40 lines, and trains a ResNet-18 on CIFAR-10’s 50,000 training images with the labels thrown away. Forty epochs later, a linear classifier on its frozen features reached 78.2%, against 43.5% on a random network’s features. With only 1% of the labels (500 images), the self-supervised features gave 66.0%; a ResNet-18 trained on those 500 labelled images alone reached 42.7%.

Why learn without labels

Chapter 8 showed that features pre-trained on ImageNet’s 1.3 million labelled images transfer to new tasks. Self-supervised learning asks whether the labels were needed for that. If a network can learn equally good features from unlabelled images, three things follow: pre-training can use far more data (billions of web images rather than one curated set), it can use data from your own domain (X-rays, satellite tiles, factory parts) that has no ImageNet-style labels, and the labelled set you do pay for only has to train a small head. Early “pretext” tasks, such as predicting an image’s rotation or solving jigsaw puzzles of its patches, worked a little. Contrastive learning worked a lot.

Views: the augmentations define the task

SimCLR (2020) takes each image in a batch and makes two views of it with random augmentation: a random crop covering 20–100% of the image, resized back to full size and randomly flipped; colour jitter (brightness, contrast, saturation, each ±40%, applied 80% of the time); and greyscale 20% of the time. Both views go through the same encoder (a ResNet-18 here, 512-dimensional output) and a small projection head (512 → 512 → 128 with a ReLU). The training signal is: the two views of an image are a positive pair; every other image in the batch is a negative.

The augmentations decide what the features ignore. A model that must match a full-colour crop of a dog’s head with a greyscale crop of its body learns that colour and position do not change identity, but shape and texture do. Choose them badly and the task becomes trivial: if both views keep the original colours, the network can match them by colour histogram alone. The ablation below measures exactly that.

The InfoNCE loss

For a batch of N images there are 2N views. Normalise each projection z to unit length so that the dot product is the cosine similarity. For each view, its partner is the right answer among the other 2N − 1 views, and the loss is plain cross-entropy over those similarities divided by a temperature τ:

lossi = −log [ exp(sim(zi, zj) / τ) / Σk ≠ i exp(sim(zi, zk) / τ) ]

def info_nce(z1, z2, tau=0.5):                               # z1, z2 (N, 128): projections of the two views
    z = F.normalize(torch.cat([z1, z2]), dim=1)               # (2N, 128), unit length
    sim = z @ z.T / tau                                           # (2N, 2N) cosine similarities / temperature
    sim.fill_diagonal_(float("-inf"))                            # a view is not its own partner
    N = len(z1); target = torch.cat([torch.arange(N, 2 * N), torch.arange(0, N)])
    return F.cross_entropy(sim, target.to(z.device))             # "which of the 2N-1 others is my partner?"

for x in batches:                                                # x: 512 unlabelled images
    loss = info_nce(proj(enc(views(x))), proj(enc(views(x))))
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()

The temperature sets how sharply the loss focuses on the hardest negatives. With a small τ, the softmax is dominated by the few negatives most similar to the anchor, so the gradient concentrates on separating near-duplicates; with a large τ, all negatives count about equally and the loss is gentler. The panel’s first view shows the effect on a row of similarities. And because the denominator sums over negatives, more negatives make the task harder and more informative: SimCLR used batches of 4,096 on TPUs. That is the practical problem MoCo solved.

MoCo: negatives from a queue

MoCo (Momentum Contrast, 2019–2020) decouples the number of negatives from the batch size. It keeps a queue of the projections of the last 65,536 images seen and uses them as negatives, so a batch of 256 still gets 65,536 negatives. Those stored projections must stay consistent as the network trains, so they come from a second momentum encoder whose weights are a slow moving average of the main encoder’s (θk ← 0.999 θk + 0.001 θq) rather than trained by gradients. MoCo v2 borrowed SimCLR’s projection head and stronger augmentation and matched it on an 8-GPU machine instead of a TPU pod. Both ideas, the momentum teacher in particular, reappear in DINO (Chapter 23).

Measured: SimCLR on CIFAR-10

A CIFAR ResNet-18 (3 × 3 stem, no max-pool) trained with the code above for 40 epochs on the 50,000 training images, no labels, batch 512, τ = 0.5, SGD with Nesterov momentum, in 4.8 minutes. Then the encoder was frozen and its 512-d features evaluated two ways. A linear probe trains only a linear classifier on the features with the training labels; a kNN classifier labels each test image by a similarity-weighted vote of its 20 nearest training images in feature space, with no training at all. Both measure how linearly separable and well-clustered the features are, which is what “good features” means for transfer.

Without seeing a label, the encoder’s features went from 43.5% to 78.2% linearly separable, and a nearest-neighbour vote went from 40.3% to 74.5%. (A random network is not at 10% chance because random convolutional features already capture colour and texture statistics.) This is well short of published CIFAR-10 SimCLR numbers, around 90% linear probe, because those train for 500–1,000 epochs rather than 40; contrastive learning keeps improving long after the loss looks flat. It is also well short of the 94% a supervised network reaches with all labels, which is the expected order: the probe is a frozen-feature test, and the labels are exactly what self-supervision did not use.

Label efficiency

The case for self-supervision is strongest when labels are scarce. The same frozen SimCLR features with a linear probe trained on a fraction of the labels, against a ResNet-18 trained from scratch on only those labelled images:

Labelled imagesSimCLR features + linear probeResNet-18 trained from scratch on themDifference
500 (1%)66.00%42.65%+23.4
5,000 (10%)74.03%78.92%−4.9
50,000 (100%)78.10%93.38%−15.3

With 500 labels, the self-supervised features won by 23 points: the expensive part, learning what images look like, had been done on 50,000 unlabelled images, and the labels only had to place a line. Already at 5,000 labels the supervised network won, and with all labels it won by 15 points. Two things explain the crossover. A linear probe is the weakest way to use pre-trained features; fine-tuning the whole encoder on the labels would beat both columns. And 40 epochs of SimCLR is a small budget. The honest summary: self-supervision pays when labels are scarce relative to unlabelled data, and its features should then be fine-tuned, not just probed.

Colour augmentation is the one that matters most: without it, the probe fell 17.7 points while the pretext task became the easiest of all, 92.2% of pairs matched. Two crops of one photo share a colour histogram, so the network matched them by colour and learned little else. That is the clearest demonstration of the common mistake: a lower contrastive loss is not better features. A large temperature (1.0) hurt by 2.4 points; 0.1 and 0.5 were within noise. Batch size went the “wrong” way: batch 128 beat 512 by 1.7 points. At a fixed 15 epochs, the smaller batch takes four times as many optimiser steps, and in short runs that outweighs having fewer negatives; the SimCLR paper also found the advantage of large batches shrinks as training gets longer. Compare batch sizes at equal steps, or at the long schedules where negatives become the bottleneck, before concluding either way.

Use case: defect inspection with few labelled faults

A production line records a million images of circuit boards a week, of which a few hundred have been labelled by an engineer as one of five fault types. Training a classifier on the few hundred labels alone overfits, exactly the 1%-label row above. The common recipe is to pre-train an encoder self-supervised on the unlabelled images from the line itself, so its features capture solder joints and component edges rather than ImageNet’s dogs, and then train a small head on the labelled faults, checking with a kNN probe that features of known faults cluster before investing in fine-tuning. The augmentation list needs care: colour jitter may destroy the discoloration that defines a burn fault, so the ablation habit of this chapter, test one augmentation at a time, carries straight over.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Evaluating the projection-head output instead of the encoder’s features: the head is tuned to the augmentation task and transfers worse.
  • Dropping colour augmentation, which lets views be matched by colour statistics: it cost 17.7 points here.
  • Using augmentations that destroy what the downstream task needs (colour for ripeness, orientation for text).
  • Reading the contrastive loss or pair accuracy as feature quality: an easier task gives a lower loss and worse features. Probe.
  • BatchNorm leaking between views on one GPU: per-batch statistics can let the model cheat; SimCLR used global BN across devices, MoCo shuffled BN.
  • Comparing a linear probe with a fine-tuned model as if they were the same evaluation.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How does SimCLR learn useful features without labels?

How a strong answer sounds
Each image is augmented twice, with random crops, flips, colour jitter and greyscale, and both views go through the same encoder and a small projection head. The InfoNCE loss is a cross-entropy that asks each view to pick out its partner among all other views in the batch by cosine similarity, so views of the same image are pulled together and different images pushed apart. To succeed, the network has to ignore what the augmentations change (position, scale, colour) and keep what identifies the image (shape, texture, parts), and those are good general features. After training, the projection head is dropped and the encoder is used. I trained one from scratch on CIFAR-10 for 40 epochs: a linear probe on its frozen features reached 78.2%, against 43.5% for a random network, and with 1% of the labels it beat supervised training from scratch by 23 points.

Deeper: Why does contrastive learning need many negatives, and how does MoCo avoid huge batches?

How a strong answer sounds
The loss is a classification of the positive against the negatives; with few negatives the task is easy, the gradient is weak, and the features are less discriminative, so SimCLR needed batches of thousands. MoCo keeps a queue of tens of thousands of projections from recent batches and uses them as negatives, so the number of negatives no longer depends on the batch. The stored keys must come from a slowly changing network or they would be inconsistent with the current one, so MoCo encodes them with a momentum encoder, an exponential moving average of the main encoder’s weights. In my own short CIFAR-10 runs, batch 128 actually beat 512 at equal epochs, because it took four times as many steps, so I would control for steps before claiming a negatives effect. Later methods such as BYOL and DINO drop explicit negatives altogether and avoid collapse with a momentum teacher and a predictor or centring.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Contrastive learning trains an encoder to match two augmented views of an image against the rest of the batch, with no labels.
  • InfoNCE is a cross-entropy over cosine similarities divided by a temperature; the augmentations define what is ignored.
  • SimCLR from scratch, 40 epochs on CIFAR-10: 78.2% linear probe and 74.5% kNN, against 43.5% and 40.3% for a random network.
  • With 1% of labels, SimCLR features beat supervised training from scratch by 23 points; from 10% labels the supervised network won.
  • Removing colour jitter made the pretext task easier (92% pair accuracy) and the features 17.7 points worse: lower loss is not better features.

Check your understanding

In SimCLR, what is a positive pair?

No labels are used: the only “same” relation is two views of one photo. Images of the same class in the batch count as negatives.

What does a smaller temperature τ do in InfoNCE?

Dividing similarities by a small τ magnifies their differences; the largest negatives dominate the denominator.

How does MoCo get many negatives without a huge batch?

The queue holds tens of thousands of keys; the momentum encoder keeps them consistent as the main encoder changes.

What does a linear probe measure?

Only the linear layer is trained, so the score reflects the features themselves.

With only 1% of CIFAR-10’s labels (500 images), which did better in the measurement?

The features were learned from all 50,000 unlabelled images; the 500 labels only had to train a linear layer.
