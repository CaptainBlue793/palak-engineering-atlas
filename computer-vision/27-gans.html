Generative Adversarial Networks

A VAE (Chapter 26) learns to generate by minimising pixel error, and pixel error rewards blur. A generative adversarial network (GAN, 2014) throws pixel error away. A generator turns random noise into images, and a second network, the discriminator, is trained to tell those images from real ones. The generator’s only goal is to fool it. Blur is easy to spot, so blur gets punished, and for most of the 2010s GANs made the sharpest images in machine learning.

They are also famously hard to train. This chapter shows the main failure, mode collapse, on a toy problem you can see whole: eight clusters of points on a ring. Then it trains a DCGAN and a class-conditional DCGAN on CIFAR-10 here, in just over a minute each. The results did not follow the textbook script. The standard non-saturating loss covered all eight clusters at first and then lost two of them, while the original minimax loss, usually described as the one to avoid, kept all eight on this easy problem; giving the discriminator one number describing the batch’s spread kept all eight with the standard loss too.

The adversarial game

The generator G maps a noise vector z (128 numbers from N(0, 1)) to an image. The discriminator D maps an image to a probability that it is real. D is trained as an ordinary binary classifier, real images labelled 1 and generated ones 0. G is trained to make D output 1 on its images. The two take turns, one gradient step each:

for real in batches:
    fake = G(torch.randn(128, 128))
    # discriminator step: real -> 1, fake -> 0 (softplus(-x) = -log sigmoid(x))
    d_loss = F.softplus(-D(real)).mean() + F.softplus(D(fake.detach())).mean()
    oD.zero_grad(); d_loss.backward(); oD.step()
    # generator step, non-saturating: make D call the fakes real
    g_loss = F.softplus(-D(fake)).mean()                       # = -log D(G(z))
    oG.zero_grad(); g_loss.backward(); oG.step()

In the original formulation the generator minimised log(1 − D(G(z))), the exact opposite of D’s loss on fakes, a minimax game whose equilibrium has the generator reproducing the data distribution and D outputting ½ everywhere. In practice that loss saturates: early in training D easily rejects the fakes, D(G(z)) ≈ 0, and log(1 − D) is flat there, so the generator gets almost no gradient exactly when it needs one most. The non-saturating loss, maximise log D(G(z)), has the same fixed point but a strong gradient when the fakes are bad. The same paper recommended it, and almost every GAN since uses it.

Mode collapse, seen in two dimensions

The generator is rewarded for any output D accepts, not for covering the whole distribution. If it finds one kind of image D likes, it can produce only that; D then learns to reject it, the generator jumps to another, and the cycle repeats. That is mode collapse: low diversity, sometimes a single output for every z. It is easiest to see where the data is drawn on a page: 8 tight clusters of points on a circle, a small fully connected G and D, and 6,000 steps with three generator losses. A cluster counts as covered if more than 20 of 2,000 generated points land within 0.3 of its centre.

Read the two columns together. With the non-saturating loss the generator got more precise as training went on (97.4% of its points on a cluster) while covering fewer clusters: two of the eight, on opposite sides of the ring, were simply abandoned. That is mode collapse in its partial, common form: no warning in the loss, sharp samples, missing variety. The minimax loss did not collapse here, which is a useful correction to the usual story; its known weakness is vanishing gradients when the discriminator rejects fakes too confidently, which matters on hard data such as images, where early fakes are easy to spot, and much less on a 2-D toy where the discriminator never gets that far ahead. One seed per row, so treat the details as an example, not a law.

The third row uses a minibatch standard deviation feature: the discriminator is given, as an extra input, the spread of the whole batch it is looking at. A collapsed generator produces batches with almost no spread, which D can now detect directly, so collapse is punished. StyleGAN uses the same trick on images. Other stabilisers in common use: spectral normalisation of D’s weights, gradient penalties (WGAN-GP, R1), two time-scale learning rates, and exponential moving averages of G’s weights for sampling.

DCGAN on CIFAR-10

DCGAN (2015) was the recipe that made GANs work on images: a generator built from transposed convolutions with BatchNorm and ReLU ending in tanh, a discriminator built from strided convolutions with LeakyReLU and no pooling, Adam with a low learning rate (2 × 10−4) and β₁ = 0.5. The one trained here has 1.19 M generator parameters and took 72 seconds for 40 epochs.

Epoch 1 already produces colour blobs with a sky-and-ground layout; by epoch 5 there are textures and edges; by epoch 40 the samples are sharp and varied, with recognisable fragments (a red car, birds against sky, animals on grass) but few coherent objects. That is typical of a small DCGAN on CIFAR-10, and the opposite failure to the VAE of Chapter 26: sharp but often meaningless, rather than meaningful but blurry. The losses tell a story of a dominant discriminator. By the last epoch D’s mean output was 0.92 on real images and 0.08 on fakes and its loss had fallen to 0.19, while the generator’s loss stayed around 3.6: D was confidently separating them, and the generator kept receiving useful gradients only because of the non-saturating loss.

GAN losses do not go down the way a classifier’s does. They are the two sides of a contest, so a healthy run has both hovering around a level rather than either one going to zero. If D’s loss collapses to zero, D has won and G receives useless gradients; if it climbs steadily, D has been overpowered, often by a collapsed G exploiting it. The quality of samples has to be judged by looking and by sample-based metrics, which is the subject of Chapter 30 (this run’s 10,000 samples are scored there).

Conditional GANs

A conditional GAN gives both networks the label: the generator produces “an image of class y”, and the discriminator judges whether the image is a real example of that class. Here the generator multiplies its noise by a learned embedding of the class, and the discriminator uses a projection: its score gains a term equal to the dot product between the image features and a learned class vector, so it can reject a realistic image that does not match the label. Trained the same way for 40 epochs (78 seconds), each row of its samples uses one noise vector across all ten classes. The label clearly steers the result: ship samples are set on blue water, automobile samples contain car-like shapes, frogs and deer appear on green backgrounds. Fine structure is still weak, and the animal classes in particular are hard to tell apart at this size and training budget.

🧾
Use case: where GANs are still used

Diffusion models (Chapter 28) have taken over general image generation, but adversarial training is far from gone. The decoders that turn latents into pixels in modern image and video generators are usually trained with an adversarial loss alongside reconstruction losses, because that is what keeps fine texture sharp. Super-resolution and image restoration (Real-ESRGAN, face restoration), real-time style and avatar filters, and single-step “distilled” diffusion models all rely on discriminators. And because a GAN generates in one forward pass, it remains the choice when an image has to be produced in milliseconds on a phone. The failure modes of this chapter, collapse and instability, are what engineers on those systems monitor.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Using the minimax generator loss for image GANs: it saturates when the discriminator easily rejects early fakes. (On the 2-D toy here it was fine; on images it is not the default for good reason.)
  • Reading a falling generator loss as progress: the losses measure the contest, not image quality.
  • Forgetting .detach() on the fakes in the discriminator step, which pushes gradients into G with the wrong sign.
  • Letting the discriminator win: a perfect D gives G no useful gradient. Balance learning rates and capacity, or add a gradient penalty.
  • Checking diversity on a handful of samples: mode collapse can hide in small grids; look at many, and use a metric.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How does a GAN learn to generate images?

How a strong answer sounds
Two networks are trained against each other. The generator turns random noise into an image; the discriminator is a classifier trained to output 1 for real images and 0 for generated ones. They alternate: one step improves the discriminator at telling them apart, then one step updates the generator, through the discriminator’s gradient, to make its images look more real to it. The generator never sees real data directly. Because the judge is a learned network rather than pixel error, blurry outputs are easy to reject, so GANs produce sharp images. In practice the generator uses the non-saturating loss, maximising log D(G(z)), because the original minimax loss gives almost no gradient early on. On a toy ring of eight Gaussians I saw the standard loss lose two of the eight modes after a few thousand steps, which is why I monitor diversity, not just the losses.

Deeper: What is mode collapse, and how do you detect and reduce it?

How a strong answer sounds
The generator is rewarded for any output the discriminator accepts, not for covering the data, so it can concentrate on a few modes and cycle between them as the discriminator catches up. Detect it by looking at many samples from different noise, by sample-based diversity metrics such as recall or FID, and on toy data by counting covered modes. Reduce it by letting the discriminator see batch statistics (minibatch discrimination or minibatch standard deviation), regularising D (spectral normalisation, gradient penalties), using unrolled or two time-scale updates, conditioning on labels, and keeping an EMA of the generator for sampling. Measured on the 8-Gaussian toy, the non-saturating GAN fell from 8 to 6 covered modes while getting more precise; adding a single minibatch-standard-deviation input to the discriminator kept all 8.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • A GAN trains a generator to fool a discriminator that is learning to tell real from fake; no pixel loss, so no reward for blur.
  • Use the non-saturating generator loss; the minimax one has vanishing gradients early.
  • Measured on 8 Gaussians: the non-saturating loss collapsed from 8 to 6 clusters while getting more precise; minibatch std kept all 8.
  • A DCGAN trained here in 72 s gives sharp, varied but mostly incoherent CIFAR-10 samples; its discriminator ended up dominant (0.92 real vs 0.08 fake).
  • Conditional GANs give both networks the label; a projection discriminator checks image and label agree.

Check your understanding

How does the generator learn what real images look like?

The generator never sees a real image; everything flows through D.

Why is the non-saturating generator loss preferred over the minimax one?

Same fixed point, but −log D(G(z)) has a strong gradient precisely when the fakes are bad.

What is mode collapse?

It is a diversity failure: samples can be sharp but nearly identical.

How does a minibatch standard deviation feature fight collapse?

A collapsed generator produces batches with almost no spread; D can now detect that directly.

In a healthy GAN run, what do the two losses look like?

A loss at zero means one side has won, which usually means the generator has stopped learning.
