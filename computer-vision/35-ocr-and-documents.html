OCR & Document Understanding

Optical character recognition turns pixels into text: receipts into expense lines, scanned contracts into searchable files, number plates into database lookups, street signs into navigation. It is usually split into finding text (detection) and reading it (recognition), and reading has a problem no classifier so far has had: the output is a sequence of unknown length, and nobody labels where each character starts and ends.

This chapter solves that with the classic answer, a convolutional-recurrent network trained with the CTC loss, written from scratch and trained on 60,000 word images rendered here in six fonts. It is tested on those fonts, on three fonts it never saw, and on blurred, rotated, noisy versions, against Microsoft’s pretrained TrOCR. On the six training fonts the CRNN made 1 character error in 5,000; on three fonts it had never seen, 6.2 errors in 100; on blurred, rotated, noisy versions it read almost nothing. TrOCR, trained on far more varied text, was worse on the familiar fonts and much better on everything else, once a quirk of its output was accounted for: it writes almost only capital letters.

The OCR pipeline

Detection uses segmentation-style networks (EAST, DBNet, CRAFT) that predict, for every pixel, whether it belongs to text, then group pixels into word or line polygons; general detectors struggle because text boxes are long, thin and often rotated. Recognition reads one cropped word or line at a time, and is the subject of the experiment here.

Reading a word: CRNN and CTC

A CRNN (2015) treats a word image as a left-to-right sequence. Convolutions turn a 32-pixel-high image into a feature map 2 rows high and W/4 columns wide; each column becomes one feature vector, a slice of the word about 4 pixels wide. A bidirectional LSTM reads the columns in both directions so each one sees its context, and a linear layer predicts, for every column, a distribution over the 67 characters plus one extra symbol, the blank.

There are far more columns than characters, and no label says which column belongs to which letter. Connectionist temporal classification (CTC) handles this by allowing any alignment. A per-column path such as “··cc·aa·t·” is read by collapsing repeated symbols and then removing blanks, giving “cat”. Many paths collapse to the same word, and the CTC loss is the negative log of the total probability of all of them, computed efficiently by dynamic programming. The blank also lets genuine double letters survive: “l·l” collapses to “ll”, while “ll” would collapse to “l”.

class CRNN(nn.Module):
    def forward(s, x):                                     # x (N, 1, 32, W): a word, 32 px high
        f = s.cnn(x)                                            # (N, 256, 2, W/4)
        f = f.permute(0, 3, 1, 2).flatten(2)                         # (N, W/4, 512): one vector per column
        return s.fc(s.rnn(f)[0])                                  # (N, W/4, 68): 67 characters + blank

Measuring OCR

The standard metrics are edit distances. Character error rate (CER) is the Levenshtein distance (insertions + deletions + substitutions) between prediction and truth, summed over the test set and divided by the number of true characters. Word error rate (WER) here is the share of words not read exactly. CER says how close; WER says how often the output is usable as is, and for invoice numbers or dates only exact matters.

The training data was rendered on the fly: 60,000 word images in six Windows fonts (Arial, Times, Courier, Verdana, Tahoma, Calibri), 60% common English words, 20% digit strings, 10% dates and 10% random letters, with small rotations and pixel noise. Test sets of 2,000 images each use the same fonts, three unseen fonts (Georgia, Consolas, Segoe UI), and the unseen fonts with blur, stronger rotation and heavy noise.

The CRNN (4.7 M parameters, 3.7 minutes of training) became almost perfect on what it was trained on: one character error in 5,000 on new words in the six training fonts. On three unseen fonts its CER rose to 6.2% and it got one word in six wrong, and its training loss had reached zero long before, while that unseen-font error stopped improving at about 5.5%: it had memorised the fonts. On the degraded images, with blur, rotations up to ±9° and heavy noise that it never saw in training, it collapsed to 97% CER, mostly emitting strings of 8s and gs. TrOCR’s raw scores looked terrible, 58–62% CER, until the predictions were inspected: it writes 99.6–100% of letters as capitals (“PITCHING” for “pitching”), because it was fine-tuned on receipts, which are printed in capitals. Scored ignoring case, it was the better reader on everything except the CRNN’s own fonts: 2.7% CER on unseen fonts and 12.8% on degraded images, where the CRNN read nothing. The lessons are the chapter’s: a from-scratch model can be excellent inside its training distribution and useless just outside it; and before believing a metric, look at the predictions.

End-to-end and document models

TrOCR (2021) replaces the CNN, LSTM and CTC with a transformer encoder-decoder: a ViT reads the image and a text decoder generates the characters one by one, as a language model would. It is larger and slower per word, and benefits from pre-training on hundreds of millions of synthetic and real text lines. Beyond lines of text, document understanding models read whole pages. LayoutLM adds each word’s box coordinates to a language model, so it knows that a number to the right of “Total:” is the total; Donut and Pix2Struct skip OCR entirely and generate structured output (JSON fields, table cells) straight from the page image. Multimodal assistants built on large language models now do the same with a vision encoder and a prompt.

Use case: invoice processing

An accounts-payable team receives thousands of PDF and photographed invoices a week. A typical system detects text, recognises it, and uses a layout-aware model to fill fields: supplier, invoice number, date, total, VAT. The error metric that matters is not CER but field-level exact match, because one wrong digit in an amount is a wrong payment; so systems validate (dates must parse, line items must sum to the total), send low-confidence fields to a person, and track the share of invoices processed with no human touch. The unseen-font and degraded results of this chapter are the reason for those checks: a recogniser that is nearly perfect on clean, familiar fonts can fail on a crumpled thermal-paper receipt.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Reporting only CER for fields that must be exact; report WER or field match too.
  • Testing on the training fonts and conditions: the CRNN’s CER was 0.02% on its training fonts, 6.2% on new fonts and 97% on degraded images.
  • Too few CTC columns: the output length must be at least the label length (plus blanks between repeated letters), or the loss is infinite; set zero_infinity=True and check widths.
  • Resizing word images to a fixed width, which squashes long words; keep the aspect ratio and pad.
  • Ignoring reading order on multi-column pages: perfect words in the wrong order are still wrong text.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What problem does CTC solve, and how is its output decoded?

How a strong answer sounds
A text recogniser outputs one prediction per horizontal slice of the image, many more slices than characters, and training labels give only the text, not where each character is. CTC adds a blank symbol and defines the probability of a text as the sum over all per-column paths that collapse to it, where collapsing merges repeated symbols and removes blanks; the loss is the negative log of that sum, computed by dynamic programming. Decoding takes the most likely symbol per column and applies the same collapse; beam search with a dictionary or language model improves it. The blank is what allows real double letters. In my CRNN trained on rendered words, a test word “9441” came out of the network as a 15-column path with 11 blanks, the two 4s separated by blanks, and decoded correctly.

Deeper: Your OCR model is excellent in testing but fails on customers’ receipts. What do you do?

How a strong answer sounds
First measure the gap on a labelled sample of real receipts with CER and field-level exact match, and slice errors by cause: detection misses, recognition errors, layout and reading order. For recognition, the usual cause is distribution shift: unseen fonts, thermal-print fading, blur, perspective and folds. Fixes are synthetic data with those degradations, fine-tuning on a few hundred real labelled crops, test-time preprocessing such as deskewing and contrast normalisation, a pretrained recogniser, and validation rules with human review for low-confidence fields. I saw both failure modes in one experiment: a CRNN trained on six clean fonts had 0.02% CER on them and 97% on blurred, rotated versions of other fonts, while pretrained TrOCR managed 12.8%; and TrOCR’s raw CER was 60% only because it outputs capitals, which I found by reading predictions, not metrics.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • OCR = text detection + recognition + layout; recognition reads cropped words or lines.
  • A CRNN predicts one symbol per image column; CTC sums over all alignments, and decoding collapses repeats and drops blanks.
  • Measured CRNN from scratch: CER 0.02% on training fonts, 6.2% on unseen fonts, 97% on degraded images.
  • Pretrained TrOCR, scored ignoring case (it writes capitals): 2.7% on unseen fonts and 12.8% degraded; look at predictions before trusting a metric.
  • Document models add layout (LayoutLM) or generate structured output from pixels (Donut).

Check your understanding

What does the CTC path “·hh·e·ll·l·oo” decode to?

Collapse repeats (hh → h, ll → l, oo → o), then drop blanks: h, e, l, l, o. The blank between “ll” and “l” keeps both l’s.

Why does CTC need a blank symbol?

Without it, “ll” could not be distinguished from a single “l” held over two columns.

What is the character error rate?

Insertions, deletions and substitutions all count.

What does a CRNN’s convolutional part produce for the recurrent part?

The image height is pooled away; width becomes the sequence axis.

What does LayoutLM add to a text model for documents?

Position is what tells “Total:” and its amount belong together.
