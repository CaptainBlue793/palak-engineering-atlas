Video Understanding

A video is a stack of images with time as a fourth axis, and most of what makes it hard follows from that. It is big: ten seconds at 30 frames per second is 300 images. It is redundant: neighbouring frames are nearly identical. And some of its meaning lives only in change over time: “opening a door” and “closing a door” can share every frame, in a different order.

This chapter compares the two ways to classify actions: run an image network on a few frames and average, or use a network whose convolutions span time. Both are tested on a 10-class subset of UCF101 with frozen features and a linear probe, then stress-tested with fewer frames and with the frames shuffled. The subset turned out to be easy enough to make a point of its own: a single frame through an image network already scored 89%, all three Kinetics-trained video networks scored 100%, and shuffling the frames of every clip changed nothing. On these classes the scene gives the action away, which is exactly why harder, motion-centred benchmarks exist.

Video as a tensor

A clip of T frames, each H × W with 3 channels, is a (T, 3, H, W) tensor, or (3, T, H, W) when time is treated as a spatial-like axis for 3D convolution. Nobody feeds a model every frame. A clip is built by sampling: pick T frames, usually 8–32, either evenly spaced across the video or as a short dense run (consecutive frames at a fixed stride), resize to a small resolution (112 × 112 is common for 3D CNNs), and at test time often average predictions over several clips. Video datasets are labelled at the clip or video level, “playing guitar”, “long jump”, and the benchmark families are Kinetics (400–700 classes from YouTube), UCF101 and HMDB51 (older, smaller), and Something-Something, built so that the object does not give the action away.

def read(path, n=16, size=112):
    cap = cv2.VideoCapture(path); frames = []
    while (ok_f := cap.read())[0]: frames.append(cv2.cvtColor(ok_f[1], cv2.COLOR_BGR2RGB))   # OpenCV decodes BGR (P3)
    idx = np.linspace(0, len(frames) - 1, n).round().astype(int)                      # n evenly spaced frames
    clip = torch.from_numpy(np.stack([frames[i] for i in idx])).permute(0, 3, 1, 2).float() / 255
    return center_crop(resize_short_side(clip, 128), size)                         # (T, 3, 112, 112)

Two ways to see time

The simplest video model ignores time altogether: run an ImageNet CNN on each sampled frame and average the features or the predictions. It is cheap to build and often strong, because many actions are recognisable from a single frame (a swimming pool says “swimming”). It cannot tell “opening” from “closing”.

A 3D CNN replaces each k × k kernel with a t × k × k kernel that also slides along time, so early layers can detect motion directly: an edge that moves right is a different pattern from one that moves left. C3D and I3D (which “inflated” ImageNet 2D weights into 3D) made this work at scale with Kinetics. Its cost is a third dimension in every convolution.

A (2+1)D block factorises each 3 × 3 × 3 convolution into a spatial 1 × 3 × 3 convolution followed by a temporal 3 × 1 × 1 one, with a nonlinearity in between. It has about the same number of parameters but twice the nonlinearities, and it is easier to optimise; on Kinetics the paper found it more accurate than full 3D. Video transformers (TimeSformer, ViViT, VideoMAE) tokenise space-time patches such as 2 × 16 × 16 and usually factorise attention into “within a frame” and “across frames” to keep the quadratic cost (Chapter 19) under control.

Measured: what each model gets from a clip

The test bed is a public 10-class subset of UCF101 (sayakpaul/ucf101-subset) with its own train and test split: 10 classes (from applying eye make-up to bench pressing), 300 training, 30 validation and 75 test videos, so each test video is worth 1.3 percentage points. Every model is frozen; only a linear classifier is trained on its features (Chapter 23’s probe), so the comparison is about the features. The 2D model is an ImageNet ResNet-50 at 224 pixels; the three video ResNet-18s were pre-trained on Kinetics-400 at 112 pixels.

A single still frame through an ImageNet network got 89% of these actions right, and averaging eight frames added three videos’ worth; the scene (a pool, a beam, a bench, a mirror) carries most of the label. The video networks got every test video right, but that does not show that modelling time is what helped: they were pre-trained on Kinetics-400, whose 400 classes include the same or closely related actions for most of these ten (archery, bench pressing, crawling baby, dunking basketball, marching), so their features were already tuned to exactly this kind of clip. With 75 test videos and a perfect score, the three architectures cannot be told apart here; the original paper’s Kinetics results (R(2+1)D ahead of R3D and MC3) need a much larger and harder test.

Does the model use time?

A model that looks at frames in order should be hurt when the order is scrambled; a model that only recognises scenes and objects should not. Shuffling the frames of every clip, train and test alike, is a cheap test of what a video model has actually learned.

Shuffling the 16 frames of every clip, for training and testing alike, left R(2+1)D-18 at 100%, and shuffling the 8 frames of the image baseline left it at 93.33%, unchanged. On these ten classes, frame order carries no information the models need: they recognise actions from what is in the frames. That is not a property of the models but of the dataset, and it is the reason the community built benchmarks like Something-Something, where “pushing something left” and “pushing something right” share every object and only the motion differs. Run this test on your own task: if shuffling does not hurt, your video model is an expensive image model.

FLOPs double exactly with the number of frames, as they must for a convolutional network. Time does not follow FLOPs across architectures: R(2+1)D-18 does the same arithmetic as R3D-18 (81 GFLOPs at 16 frames) but took more than twice as long, 5.3 against 2.3 ms, because splitting each 3D convolution in two doubles the number of layers and the separate spatial and temporal kernels use the GPU less efficiently than one fused 3 × 3 × 3 kernel. MC3-18 has a third of the parameters of R3D-18 for slightly more FLOPs. And the frame-averaged ResNet-50 at 224 pixels was in the same range as the video networks at 112: resolution costs as much as time does.

Use case: content moderation and sports highlights at scale

A video platform that must label every uploaded minute cannot afford a heavy model on every frame. Production systems cascade: a cheap image model on one frame per second flags candidate segments (a scene that looks like a fight, a goal-mouth scramble), and only those segments get a dense clip through a 3D or transformer model that can see the motion. The shuffled-frames test is part of their evaluation too: if a “violence” classifier scores the same on shuffled frames, it is recognising settings, not actions, and will fail on a boxing gym or a crowded bar.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Decoding video with OpenCV and forgetting BGR (P3): every frame’s colours are swapped.
  • Using ImageNet normalisation for Kinetics-trained models, or the reverse; each has its own mean and std.
  • Splitting clips from the same source video between train and test: UCF101 groups clips by original video for this reason.
  • Assuming a video model uses motion without testing it, for example by shuffling frames.
  • Comparing models at different frame counts or resolutions without accounting for compute.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How would you build a video action classifier, and what is a (2+1)D convolution?

How a strong answer sounds
Start with a baseline: sample 8 evenly spaced frames, run a pretrained image CNN on each, average the features and train a classifier; it is cheap and often strong because the scene gives many actions away. If the classes depend on motion, use a video model pretrained on Kinetics, a 3D CNN or a video transformer, on 16–32-frame clips at low resolution. A 3D convolution uses t × k × k kernels so early layers can respond to motion; a (2+1)D convolution factorises it into a spatial 1 × k × k convolution then a temporal t × 1 × 1 one with a nonlinearity between, with similar parameters, more nonlinearity, and easier optimisation. On a 10-class UCF101 subset I measured an ImageNet ResNet-50 at 89% from a single frame and 93% averaging 8 frames, while Kinetics-pretrained 3D ResNets reached 100%; shuffling the frames changed nothing, so on those classes the scene, not the motion, carried the label.

Deeper: Your video model scores well, but you suspect it ignores motion. How do you check?

How a strong answer sounds
Compare it with a single-frame or frame-averaged image model on the same data: if the gap is small, the dataset can be solved from appearance. Shuffle or reverse the frames at test time and see whether accuracy drops; a model using order should be hurt. Test on classes that differ only in direction or order (opening versus closing), or on a temporal benchmark such as Something-Something. Look at saliency over time. I ran exactly this on a UCF101 subset: a Kinetics-pretrained R(2+1)D-18 scored 100% with frames in order and 100% shuffled, so it was not using order there. I would never conclude a model uses motion without that test.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • A clip is T sampled frames as a (3, T, H, W) tensor; frame sampling and resolution set the cost.
  • Frame-averaged image CNNs are strong baselines because scenes often reveal the action.
  • 3D convolutions see motion; (2+1)D splits them into spatial and temporal parts.
  • Measured on 10 UCF101 classes: one frame through an ImageNet ResNet-50 scored 89%, 8 averaged frames 93%, Kinetics-pretrained 3D ResNets 100%.
  • Shuffling frames changed nothing on those classes; R(2+1)D-18 matched R3D-18’s FLOPs but took 2.3× as long.

Check your understanding

What does a (2+1)D convolution do?

Similar parameter count to full 3D, with an extra nonlinearity between space and time.

Why is a frame-averaged image CNN often a strong baseline for action recognition?

A pool suggests swimming and a guitar suggests playing guitar, without any motion.

What is shuffling the frames of a clip a test of?

If accuracy barely changes, order was not being used.

Which dataset was built so that objects and scenes do not give the action away?

Its classes are templates like “pushing something from left to right”, so motion is required.

Why do video transformers usually factorise attention into spatial and temporal parts?

16 frames × 196 patches is over 3,000 tokens; attending within and across frames separately is much cheaper.
