Schema Design & Normalisation

A schema decides which bugs are possible. Store the same fact in two places and, sooner or later, the two copies disagree: a customer with two addresses, an instructor in two offices, a product with two prices. Normalisation is the discipline of storing each fact once, so that the database's structure rules those bugs out. This chapter defines the anomalies it prevents, the functional dependencies it's built on, the normal forms from 1NF to BCNF with one worked example, and then the other half of the skill: when to break the rules on purpose.

  • Recognise update, insert and delete anomalies in a table
  • Write down the functional dependencies of a schema and find its keys
  • Decompose a table step by step into 1NF, 2NF, 3NF and BCNF
  • Tell redundancy from a deliberately stored historical fact
  • Denormalise on purpose, knowing what you must now keep in sync

Three anomalies

Take one wide table that records which students take which courses, with everything about the course and its instructor repeated on every enrolment row:

enrolments_flat (student_id, student_name, course_id, course_title, instructor_id, instructor_name, office, grade)

It's convenient to query, and it has three kinds of problem, named after the operation that exposes them:

  • Update anomaly: Dr Rao changes office. Her office is stored on every row of every enrolment in every course she teaches; update some and miss others and the database now holds two answers.
  • Insert anomaly: a new course is created before anyone enrols. There's no row to put it in, because every row needs a student.
  • Delete anomaly: the last student withdraws from Networks. Deleting their enrolment also deletes the only record that Networks exists and who teaches it.

All three come from one cause: the table stores several independent facts (about students, courses, instructors and enrolments) in the same rows.

Functional dependencies

A functional dependency X → Y says: any two rows that agree on X must agree on Y. It's a statement about the real world, not about today's data: "a course has one title" is a rule; "no two students currently share a surname" is a coincidence. For the flat table:

student_id            → student_name
course_id             → course_title, instructor_id
instructor_id         → instructor_name, office
(student_id, course_id) → grade

A key is a minimal set of columns that determines every other column. Here that's (student_id, course_id): from it you can reach everything by following the arrows. Normal forms are rules about which dependencies may exist in a table, relative to its keys.

The normal forms

FormRuleWhat breaks it hereFix
1NFEvery column holds one atomic value; no repeating groupsA courses column holding "C101, C102"One row per student–course pair
2NF1NF, and no non-key column depends on part of a composite keystudent_name depends on student_id aloneMove it to students(student_id, …)
3NF2NF, and no non-key column depends on another non-key columncourse_id → instructor_id → officeMove it to instructors(instructor_id, …)
BCNFFor every non-trivial X → Y, X is a superkeyOverlapping candidate keys (example below)Decompose on the offending dependency

BCNF: when 3NF isn't quite enough

3NF allows one awkward case: a dependency whose left side isn't a key, when its right side is part of a key. The classic example is tutoring. Each student has one tutor per subject, and each tutor teaches exactly one subject:

tutoring (student, subject, tutor)
  (student, subject) → tutor        -- key 1
  tutor              → subject      -- each tutor teaches one subject
  (student, tutor) is also a candidate key

The table is in 3NF (subject is part of a key), but "Dr Lee teaches Physics" is stored once per student Dr Lee tutors, so it can be updated inconsistently. BCNF requires every determinant to be a key, so you decompose into tutors(tutor, subject) and tutoring(student, tutor). The price: the rule "one tutor per subject per student" now spans two tables and can't be enforced with a simple unique constraint. BCNF decompositions don't always preserve every dependency, which is why many designers stop at 3NF and accept the rare BCNF violation knowingly.

Not every copy is redundancy

An order_items row stores unit_cents even though products has a price. That isn't a normalisation failure: the order line records the price the customer agreed to at that moment, which is a different fact from the product's current price, and must never change when the catalogue does. The test is the dependency: does product_id → unit_cents hold for order lines? No: the same product has different prices on different orders. The same reasoning applies to a shipping address copied onto an order, or a tax rate copied onto an invoice. Case Study 41 builds a whole schema on this distinction.

Denormalising deliberately

Normalised schemas make writes safe and reads need joins. Sometimes a read path is hot enough that you store a derived value: a comment_count on posts, a customer_name on an orders search table, a precomputed daily totals table. Each one is a second copy of a fact, so you take on the job of keeping it in sync. Ranked from most to least reliable:

  • Let the database maintain it: a materialised view (Chapter 33), a generated column, or a trigger in the same transaction.
  • Update it in the same transaction as the source change, in application code: UPDATE posts SET comment_count = comment_count + 1 next to the INSERT INTO comments.
  • Update it asynchronously (a queue, CDC into a search index or cache), accepting that reads can be briefly stale, and having a job that rebuilds it from the source of truth.
BEGIN;
INSERT INTO comments (post_id, author_id, body) VALUES (%s, %s, %s);
UPDATE posts SET comment_count = comment_count + 1 WHERE post_id = %s;   -- the copy moves with the fact
COMMIT;

The report that disagreed with itself

A common pattern in growing companies: a customers table and a separate accounts table both store the customer's country, because two teams built them at different times. Tax is calculated from one, the sales dashboard groups by the other. A customer moves from Ireland to Germany, support updates one table, and for a quarter that customer's revenue is reported in Germany while invoices carry Irish VAT. Nothing crashed, no test failed, and the fix (store country once and join to it, or derive the second copy from the first) is cheap compared with the reconciliation work. This is exactly the update anomaly, at company scale.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Comma-separated lists in a column (tags = 'a,b,c'): no constraints, no index, string parsing in every query. Use a child table (or an array with care).
  • Normalising by today's data instead of the rules. A dependency must hold for all valid data, not just the current rows.
  • Treating historical snapshots as redundancy and "fixing" an order's price to reference the current product price.
  • Denormalising without an owner for the copy. If nothing updates it in the same transaction or rebuilds it, it will drift.
  • Over-splitting: a separate table for every attribute "in case" makes simple reads into five-way joins for no integrity gain.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is normalisation and why does it matter?

How a strong answer sounds
Normalisation organises tables so that each fact is stored once, guided by functional dependencies: every non-key column should depend on the key, the whole key and nothing but the key. It prevents update anomalies (copies that disagree), insert anomalies (can't record a fact without an unrelated one) and delete anomalies (deleting one fact loses another). In practice I design to 3NF, then denormalise specific read paths deliberately, with a plan for keeping the copy in sync.

Deeper: The orders table stores product name and price on each order line. A colleague says that violates 3NF. Do you agree?

How a strong answer sounds
It depends on what the columns mean. If they're meant to mirror the current product, yes: product_id → name, price holds and they'll go stale. But usually they're a snapshot of what the customer saw and agreed to at checkout, a different fact. Then product_id doesn't determine them (the same product has different prices on different orders), so there's no violation, and keeping them is required for correct invoices, refunds and audits. I'd name the columns to make that explicit, like unit_price_at_purchase.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Redundant copies cause update, insert and delete anomalies.
  • Functional dependencies (X → Y) are business rules; keys are minimal sets that determine everything.
  • 1NF: atomic values. 2NF: no partial dependencies. 3NF: no transitive dependencies. BCNF: every determinant is a key.
  • A value copied as a historical fact (price at purchase) isn't redundancy.
  • Denormalise deliberately, and decide who keeps the copy correct: the database, the same transaction, or an async job with a rebuild.

Check your understanding

In enrolments(student_id, course_id, student_name, grade) with key (student_id, course_id), which normal form is violated?

student_id → student_name is a partial dependency on the composite key. Move student_name to students.

courses(course_id, title, instructor_id, instructor_office): what is the problem?

The office is a fact about the instructor, not the course. It belongs in instructors(instructor_id, office).

You can't record a new course until a student enrols in it. Which anomaly is that?

The fact "course C104 exists" can't be stored without an unrelated fact (an enrolment), because they share rows.

order_items stores unit_cents copied from products.price_cents at checkout. Is that a normalisation violation?

The same product has different prices on different orders, so product_id → unit_cents doesn't hold. Removing it would make old invoices change when prices change.

You add posts.comment_count for a fast feed. What's the most reliable way to keep it correct?

Changing the copy in the same transaction as the source fact keeps them consistent. An async or nightly approach can drift and needs a rebuild path.
