WAL & Crash Recovery

A database server can lose power at any instant, halfway through a transaction, halfway through writing one page. When it starts again, every transaction that was told "committed" must be there in full, and no trace of a transaction that wasn't may be visible, although at the moment of the crash the data files held some mixture of old pages and new. One mechanism achieves this, and replication, backups and point-in-time recovery are all built on the same mechanism: the write-ahead log. Chapter 10 gave the rule that COMMIT waits for the log. This chapter covers what is in the log, how a checkpoint bounds the work of recovery, how recovery decides what to replay, and one subtle cost, full-page writes, that shapes how much log a database produces.

Why write-ahead

A transaction changes pages in the buffer pool. Those pages reach the data files later, in whatever order suits the background writer and the checkpointer. So at any moment the files on disk are not a consistent state of the database. Some pages hold changes from transactions that haven't committed, and some lack changes from transactions that have. Two rules make this safe.

RuleStatementWhat it guarantees
Log before dataA changed page may not be written to its file until the log records describing its changes are safely on diskWhatever is found in a data file after a crash, the log can explain it, and recovery can complete it or disregard it
Log before commitCOMMIT doesn't return until the log, up to and including the commit record, is safely on diskEvery committed change can be reconstructed from the log, even if none of its pages had been written

With those two rules the data files need never be up to date. The log is the authoritative record, and the data files are a cache of its effects that happens to be stored on disk. Writing the log costs little, since it is one file written in sequence. Writing pages as they changed would be scattered and slow, and under these rules it can be left for later.

Log records and LSNs

The log is a sequence of records, each describing one small physical change: "in this file, on this page, at this offset, insert these bytes". A record's position in the log, counted in bytes from the beginning, is its log sequence number, or LSN. An LSN only ever increases, so it works as a clock for the whole database.

$ pg_waldump -p $PGDATA/pg_wal --start=0/3A4F2C10 --limit=5
rmgr: Heap    len: 71   tx: 8841  lsn: 0/3A4F2C10  desc: UPDATE off 12 … blkref #0: rel 1663/16384/16412 blk 207
rmgr: Btree   len: 64   tx: 8841  lsn: 0/3A4F2C58  desc: INSERT_LEAF off 88, blkref #0: rel 1663/16384/16420 blk 3114
rmgr: Heap    len: 71   tx: 8841  lsn: 0/3A4F2C98  desc: UPDATE off 4 …  blkref #0: rel 1663/16384/16412 blk 9031
rmgr: Btree   len: 64   tx: 8841  lsn: 0/3A4F2CE0  desc: INSERT_LEAF off 17, blkref #0: rel 1663/16384/16420 blk 870
rmgr: Transaction len: 34 tx: 8841  lsn: 0/3A4F2D20  desc: COMMIT 2026-10-07 09:14:02.118 UTC

That is the transfer of Chapter 10 as the log sees it: two row updates, the two index entries they required, and a commit, in 304 bytes. Every page records, in its header, the LSN of the last log record that changed it (Chapter 11). That one field does two jobs. Before writing a page out, the buffer manager checks that the log has been flushed at least as far as the page's LSN, which enforces the first rule. And during recovery, comparing a record's LSN with the page's LSN says whether the page already contains that change.

Checkpoints

If nothing else were done, recovery would have to replay the log from the day the database was created. A checkpoint bounds it. The checkpointer notes the current position in the log, which is called the redo point. It writes every page that was dirty at that moment to its file, spreading the work over several minutes. Then it writes a checkpoint record saying "everything before the redo point is in the data files".

SHOW checkpoint_timeout;     -- 5min    a checkpoint at least this often
SHOW max_wal_size;           -- 1GB     and sooner, if this much log has been written since the last

SELECT checkpoint_lsn, redo_lsn, checkpoint_time FROM pg_control_checkpoint();
--  0/3A12F7B8 | 0/39E0C4A0 | 2026-10-07 09:10:44+00

After a crash, recovery starts at the redo point of the last completed checkpoint, and log older than that can be recycled. The interval is a trade between two costs: frequent checkpoints mean short recovery and more page writes, and rare ones mean a long recovery and a large log.

Redo and undo

Recovery reads the log forward from the redo point. For each record, it finds the page the record refers to and compares LSNs.

Redo replays the changes of every transaction, including those that never committed, and something has to deal with those. Databases take two different approaches here.

The write-ahead log
    Each page shows its value and the LSN of the last change it contains. Log records in italics are still in memory and would be lost in a crash. "Write one dirty page" is the background writer, which may write a page at any time, including a page changed by a transaction that hasn't committed.

    Things to notice. Update, commit, and crash without writing any page: the data files still hold the old values, and recovery rebuilds the committed ones from the log. That is the second rule at work. Update without committing, write that dirty page, and crash: the data file now contains a change nobody committed, the log records the change and has no commit for it, and after recovery no query can see it. That is the first rule: the page could only be written once its log record was safe, so recovery knows whose change it was. Take a checkpoint, do more work and crash: recovery begins at the checkpoint's redo point and ignores everything before it. And where a page was written after the change a record describes, recovery compares LSNs and skips the record. The last statistic stays at zero however you arrange the crash.

    full_page_writes

    One failure escapes everything so far. A page is 8 kB and a disk writes in sectors of 4 kB or less. If power fails while a page is being written, the file can be left holding the first half of the new page and the second half of the old one. This is a torn page. A log record says "insert these 60 bytes at offset 4,120", and applying that to a page whose two halves disagree gives garbage. Redo needs a sound page to start from.

    PostgreSQL's answer is full_page_writes, which is on by default. The first time a page is modified after each checkpoint, the log record carries a complete copy of the page, all 8 kB of it, in place of the small change. If that page is later torn, recovery restores the whole image from the log and then applies the records after it. A page only needs this once in each checkpoint cycle, because recovery never goes back beyond the last checkpoint.

    📜
    Amazon Aurora: "the log is the database"

    When Amazon's engineers designed Aurora, a cloud database compatible with MySQL and PostgreSQL, they started from the observation this chapter has been making: if the log is the authoritative record and the data files are a cache of its effects, then the database engine need not write data pages at all. In a conventional replicated MySQL, they counted, a single change led to the same information being written several times: the log record, the page itself, the doublewrite copy that guards against torn pages, the binary log for replicas, and then all of those again on the standby. In Aurora, which they described in a paper in 2017, the engine sends only log records over the network, to a storage service spread over three data centres. That service stores the log durably, with a write acknowledged once four of six copies have it, and applies the records to pages itself, in the background, on the storage nodes. The engine never writes a page, so there are no checkpoints in the usual sense, no full-page images and no doublewrite, and a page can't be torn in transit because pages are never sent. Recovery after a crash doesn't replay the log before opening: the storage layer brings a page up to date from its log records at the moment someone asks for that page. The paper reported the engine doing several times fewer I/O operations for each transaction than MySQL on the same hardware. The design uses the recovery mechanism of this chapter as its normal mode of operation, with redo running continuously on the storage nodes.

    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Turning off fsync or full_page_writes for speed. The database is quicker until the first power cut, and may then be corrupted beyond repair.
    • Putting the WAL on storage that acknowledges writes it hasn't made: a consumer SSD without power-loss protection, or a controller cache with no battery.
    • Checkpoints that are too frequent. The log message "checkpoints are occurring too frequently" means max_wal_size is too small, and the cost is paid in full-page images.
    • Letting the WAL directory fill up. A stalled archive command or an abandoned replication slot stops old log being recycled, and a full disk stops the database.
    • Deleting files from pg_wal by hand to free space. They are the database's only record of recent commits.
    • Assuming recovery is instant. It replays everything since the last checkpoint. Measure it before an incident does.

    Interview drill

    Explain it simply: What is write-ahead logging, and how does it let a database survive a crash?

    How a strong answer sounds
    Every change is first described in a record appended to a sequential log, and two rules are enforced. A data page can't be written to disk until the log records for its changes have been flushed, and a commit doesn't return until the log up to its commit record has been flushed. Data pages themselves are modified in memory and written lazily. So at a crash the data files may be missing committed changes and may contain uncommitted ones, but the log has everything. On restart, the database finds the last checkpoint, a point at which all earlier changes were known to be in the data files, and replays the log forward from there. Each page carries the LSN of the last record applied to it, so recovery applies a record only if the page is older than the record, which makes replay idempotent. Transactions without a commit record are treated as aborted: in PostgreSQL their row versions are simply invisible, and in ARIES-style engines an undo phase reverses them. The gain is that commit costs one sequential log flush instead of many random page writes, and the same log feeds replication and point-in-time recovery.

    Deeper: What is a torn page, and how do databases protect against it?

    How a strong answer sounds
    A database page is typically 8 or 16 kB, but the storage device guarantees atomic writes only for a sector, 512 bytes or 4 kB. If power fails in the middle of writing a page, the on-disk page can contain part of the new version and part of the old: a torn page. Ordinary redo can't repair that, because log records are small deltas, "change these bytes at this offset", which assume a consistent starting page. PostgreSQL's protection is full-page writes: the first time a page is modified after a checkpoint, the entire page image is written to the WAL instead of a delta. If the page is later torn, recovery overwrites it with that image and then applies subsequent records; since recovery starts from the last checkpoint, one image per page per checkpoint cycle is enough. The cost is WAL volume, especially just after a checkpoint and for workloads that touch many different pages, which is why checkpoint spacing and wal_compression matter. InnoDB uses a doublewrite buffer instead: pages are first written sequentially to a dedicated area and fsynced, then written to their real locations, so a torn page can be restored from the doublewrite copy. Page checksums detect a torn or corrupted page in either system. On storage that guarantees atomic page writes, such as some copy-on-write file systems, the protection can be turned off, but only with certainty about that guarantee.

    🧠 Key takeaways

    • The log is the authoritative record. Data files are a lazily written cache of its effects.
    • Two rules: a page isn't written before its log records are on disk, and a commit doesn't return before its commit record is.
    • An LSN is a position in the log. Each page stores the LSN of its last change, and recovery redoes a record only if the page is older.
    • A checkpoint writes all dirty pages and sets the point from which recovery will start, which bounds recovery time and lets old log go.
    • PostgreSQL recovers by redo alone, leaving uncommitted row versions invisible. ARIES-style engines add an undo phase.
    • The first change to a page after a checkpoint logs the whole page, to survive torn writes, and this is a large share of all log written.

    Check your understanding

    A transaction commits, and the server loses power before any of the pages it changed have been written to the data files. What happens on restart?

    COMMIT returned only after the log was flushed, and the log is enough to rebuild the pages.

    Before the buffer manager writes a dirty page to its data file, what must be true?

    The page may hold uncommitted changes. What matters is that the log can explain whatever is on disk.

    During recovery, a log record has LSN 500 and the page it refers to has LSN 620. What does recovery do?

    The page was written after the change was made. Applying it again could be wrong, and the LSN test prevents that.

    What does a checkpoint achieve?

    Without checkpoints, recovery would have to replay the whole history of the database.

    Why does the volume of WAL rise sharply just after each checkpoint?

    An 8 kB image in place of a record of a few dozen bytes, once for each page in each checkpoint cycle.
