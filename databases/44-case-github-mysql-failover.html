Case Study: GitHub's MySQL Failover Incident

At 22:52 UTC on Sunday 21 October 2018, engineers replacing a failing piece of optical networking equipment cut the connection between GitHub's main data centre on the East Coast of the United States and the rest of its network. The connection was back 43 seconds later. In those seconds the system that manages GitHub's databases did what it had been built to do: it concluded that the primary databases had failed, and promoted replicas on the West Coast in their place. When the link returned there were two sets of databases, each holding writes the other had never seen. Putting that right took 24 hours and 11 minutes, during which the site showed stale data and millions of webhooks went undelivered. No component malfunctioned, and the automation followed its configuration exactly. GitHub published a detailed account, and it is the clearest real example of what Chapter 25 called split brain, and of how a failover can do more damage than the fault that triggered it.

The setup

GitHub stored its metadata, the issues, pull requests, comments, users and permissions, in several MySQL clusters. Git repositories themselves are kept in a separate system and weren't affected. Each cluster had one primary taking all writes and dozens of replicas serving reads. The primaries, and the application servers that wrote to them, were in the East Coast data centre. Replicas were spread across that site, a West Coast data centre and public cloud regions, with replication between the coasts asynchronous.

Failover was automatic, managed by an open-source tool called Orchestrator, whose author worked on GitHub's database team. Orchestrator watches the replication topology, and when a primary stops responding and its replicas agree that they can't reach it either, it chooses the most up-to-date replica and promotes it. To avoid acting on one node's mistaken view, Orchestrator itself ran as a group of nodes in different sites that had to reach a majority before acting.

The partition

Look at it from each side. From the West, the primary had vanished: no heartbeat, no replication stream. The Orchestrator nodes there and in the cloud were a majority of the group, so they were entitled to decide, and the rule they had been given was to promote the best replica available. They did, in every cluster. From the East, nothing had happened. The primaries were healthy, the application servers next to them could reach them, and writes went on being accepted. They were written to the East's binary logs and to nowhere else.

When the link came back, traffic was directed to the new primaries in the West. The East's old primaries now held a few seconds of committed writes that existed nowhere else, and the West was accumulating writes of its own. The two histories had forked.

Writes on both sides

A fork is what makes split brain so much worse than an outage. Had the East simply been behind, it could have been pointed at the West and caught up. Had the West taken no writes, the failover could have been reversed. With writes on both sides there were three options, and each was bad.

OptionWhat it costs
Fail back to the East at onceEverything written in the West since the failover is discarded: by the time the situation was understood, that was the best part of an hour of users' issues, comments and pushes
Stay in the West, and throw away the East's extra writesThose writes are lost. And the application servers are still in the East, now reaching their database across a continent for every query, which the application wasn't built to tolerate. The site is barely usable
Keep both: rebuild the East from backups, replicate the West's writes into it, fail back, and then reconcile the East's stranded writes by handNo data lost. Many hours of degraded service while terabytes are restored and replicas catch up

GitHub chose the third, and said why in so many words: the integrity of users' data mattered more than how quickly the site was back to normal. Why couldn't the old East primaries simply be re-attached as replicas of the West? Each held transactions the West's history didn't contain. A replica applies its primary's log on top of its own data, and that only works if the replica's data is a prefix of the primary's. So the East's servers had to be rebuilt from backups taken before the fork, and the stranded writes kept aside as binary logs for later.

Three things lengthened it, and each is a lesson from an earlier chapter. The restores took as long as copying terabytes takes, and nobody had needed to know that figure so urgently before (Chapter 35). The replicas couldn't catch up under daytime load, since a replica applying a backlog is also serving reads (Chapter 25). And the stranded writes had to be dealt with afterwards: GitHub reported that one of its busiest clusters had 954 writes in the affected window, and each had to be examined to see whether it could be applied safely or conflicted with something written in the West since.

The fault
    Assumptions: replication between the coasts is asynchronous; within the East there is a replica that confirms each commit; automatic failover acts after about 30 seconds; a person paged at night needs about 20 minutes to assess and decide. "Automatic, to any region" was GitHub's configuration in October 2018, and "automatic, within the region" is roughly what it changed to afterwards.

    Things to notice. No policy wins on all three faults. Promoting across regions is the best answer to losing a whole data centre: writes are back in about a minute where the other two policies need a person and twenty. It is the worst answer by far to a 43-second partition, where the other two do nothing and the incident is over before anyone is paged. The single dead server is the easy case, and both automatic policies handle it in half a minute with nothing lost. The trouble is that from the West, during the first thirty seconds, a brief partition and a lost data centre look exactly alike, and brief partitions are far more common. A policy tuned for the rare disaster will fire on the common blip. GitHub's change afterwards was to stop the automation promoting across regions, and to make that one decision a human's.

    Lessons

    LessonIn practice
    "Unreachable" doesn't mean "dead"A primary that can't be seen may be serving clients happily. Before promoting, a failover system must make sure the old primary can't accept writes: fence it, or have it step down by itself when it loses contact with a majority
    A majority decides who may act. It doesn't decide whether acting is wiseOrchestrator's quorum was correctly formed. The policy it carried out, promote across the continent after a short timeout, was the problem
    Failover has a cost, so the threshold should reflect itIf a wrong failover costs a day and waiting costs a minute, wait longer than 30 seconds for the expensive kind
    A failover target must be able to carry the loadThe West had the data. The applications were in the East, and couldn't work with a database 60 ms away
    Asynchronous replication means a failover can strand writesDecide in advance whether those writes are discarded or reconciled, and how
    Recovery time is restore timeKnow how long a full restore takes, and keep backups where they can be reached quickly
    PractiseGitHub committed to injecting faults deliberately, so that the next partition would be the hundredth one its systems had met

    GitHub's longer-term answer was structural: to run each site with its own complete stack, so that application servers and their databases fail over together, and losing any one site leaves the others whole.

    Four months earlier: the design, described by its authors

    In June 2018 GitHub's database team had published a long article on how its MySQL high availability worked, and read after the incident it shows how carefully the system had been thought through. It described Orchestrator running as a group of nodes that reach agreement by a consensus protocol, so that no single node's view of the network can trigger a failover. It described how a failover is announced to the rest of the system through a service-discovery store, and how proxies redirect writes to the new primary, with the whole process expected to take between ten and thirty seconds. It described semi-synchronous replication, in which the primary waits for at least one replica to confirm each commit before acknowledging it, used within a data centre so that a failover there loses nothing. And it discussed limitations openly, among them the case of a data centre cut off from the rest: the primary inside it might go on accepting writes from clients inside it while the outside world elected a replacement, and the team wrote that it was looking at ways to shut such a primary off. Every mechanism in the article worked as described on 21 October. The majority was formed properly and the promotion was clean, and the old primaries kept taking writes for those seconds exactly as the article had warned they might. What had received less attention was the cost of that scenario: the hours a restore takes, an application tier that couldn't follow its databases to another coast, and the reconciliation afterwards. A design review asks whether each mechanism is correct. An incident like this one adds a second question, which is what the system as a whole does when every mechanism is correct and the situation is one its designers ranked as unlikely.

    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Promoting a replica without fencing the old primary. If it is alive, there are now two.
    • A short failover timeout for an expensive failover. Most network faults end within a minute.
    • Failing over the database and not the application, leaving them a continent apart.
    • Re-attaching a diverged primary as a replica. It has to be rebuilt, or rewound to the fork.
    • Not knowing how long a restore takes until it is the only way out.
    • Blaming the tool. Orchestrator followed its configuration. The configuration encoded a choice nobody had examined closely.

    Interview drill

    Explain it simply: What is split brain in a replicated database, and how do you prevent it?

    How a strong answer sounds
    Split brain is when two nodes both believe they are the primary and both accept writes, so the data forks into two histories that can't be merged automatically. It usually comes from a network partition combined with automatic failover: the replicas and the failover manager can't reach the primary and promote a replacement, while the old primary is still alive and still reachable by some clients. Prevention has three parts. First, only a majority may promote: the failover decision is taken by a quorum of nodes in different failure domains, so that the minority side can't elect its own leader. Second, and this is the part that gets missed, the old primary must stop: either it is fenced from outside, by cutting its power, revoking its virtual IP or blocking it at the proxy, or it demotes itself when it can no longer reach a majority, typically with a lease that expires. A quorum alone doesn't stop an isolated primary from accepting writes from clients on its side. Third, with synchronous or semi-synchronous replication to a quorum, an isolated primary can't commit at all, since it can't get acknowledgements, which is how consensus-based databases avoid the problem by construction. If it happens anyway, one side's writes have to be chosen and the other's reconciled by hand.

    Deeper: You run a primary in one region with asynchronous replicas in another. Should failover to the other region be automatic?

    How a strong answer sounds
    I'd make it automatic within a region and a human decision across regions, and I'd explain it as a matter of asymmetric costs. Within a region, a replica can be kept synchronous or semi-synchronous, so promotion loses nothing; the application servers stay close to the new primary; and the failure it handles, a dead host, is common and unambiguous. Automating that gives recovery in tens of seconds at little risk. Across regions, three things change. Replication is asynchronous, so promotion strands whatever the old primary committed and hadn't shipped. The most likely cause of losing contact with a whole region is a network partition, not the region's destruction, so the old primary is probably alive, and unless it is reliably fenced I get split brain. And the application may not work well with its database in another region. So a wrong cross-region failover is very expensive, while a right one saves perhaps twenty minutes over a human decision, and the event is rare. With those figures I'd page a person, give them a tested runbook and a one-command failover, and accept twenty minutes of write outage in a real regional loss. If the business needs automatic regional failover, the honest route is a different architecture: synchronous replication across three regions with a consensus protocol, so that a minority side can't accept writes, and the application deployed in each region. That removes the split-brain risk at the price of cross-region latency on every commit.

    🧠 Key takeaways

    • A 43-second partition became a 24-hour incident, since automatic failover across regions created two primaries with different writes.
    • Split brain is worse than downtime: neither side can be discarded without losing data, and the two can't be merged mechanically.
    • A quorum decides who may promote. Something else must make sure the old primary has stopped.
    • A failover policy acts without knowing how long the fault will last. Set its threshold by what a wrong failover costs.
    • Fail over the application and the database together, or not at all.
    • Know the restore time, and decide in advance what happens to writes stranded on an old primary.

    Check your understanding

    During the 43 seconds, why did the old East Coast primaries keep accepting writes?

    Being unreachable from outside is different from being down. Nothing fenced them.

    After the link returned, why couldn't the old East primaries be attached as replicas of the new West primaries?

    A replica must start from a point on its primary's history. They had to be rebuilt from backups taken before the fork.

    Orchestrator's nodes formed a proper majority before acting. What does that show?

    The mechanism was correct. The policy it executed, and the lack of fencing, were the problems.

    Why did recovery take about a day and not an hour?

    Choosing to keep both sides' data meant rebuilding the East, and restore time set the pace.

    Which change most directly prevents this incident from recurring?

    The fork needed two things: a promotion on one side and a live primary on the other. Remove either.
