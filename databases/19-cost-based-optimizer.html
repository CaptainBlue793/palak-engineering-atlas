The Cost-Based Optimiser

The planner has to choose a plan before it has read a single row. It can't know how many orders are pending, so it estimates, from a small set of statistics that it collected at some earlier time by sampling the table. Each of its choices, index or sequential scan, nested loop or hash join, which table to start from, follows from those estimates by arithmetic. When the estimates are close the plans are good, and when a plan is terrible the cause is nearly always an estimate that was wrong by a factor of a hundred or more. This chapter covers what statistics the planner keeps, how it turns them into row counts, the three common ways those counts go wrong, and what to do about each one before reaching for a hint.

  • Explain why correlated columns, stale statistics and expressions produce wrong estimates

Cost is rows multiplied by prices

The planner gives every candidate plan a cost, and keeps the cheapest. A cost is a count of the work the plan will do, with each kind of work multiplied by a configured price.

SettingDefaultCharged for
seq_page_cost1.0Each page read in sequence. This is the unit that the others are relative to
random_page_cost4.0Each page fetched out of order, as an index scan does. On SSDs it should be about 1.1
cpu_tuple_cost0.01Each row processed
cpu_index_tuple_cost0.005Each index entry processed
cpu_operator_cost0.0025Each operator or function evaluated
 
-- A sequential scan of orders: 63,694 pages and 5,000,000 rows, with one condition to test
cost = 63694 × 1.0  +  5000000 × 0.01  +  5000000 × 0.0025  =  126194      -- the figure EXPLAIN printed in Chapter 17

The prices are fixed and the page counts are known. The uncertain inputs are the row counts: how many rows will pass this filter, how many will come out of that join. Every plan decision in Chapter 18 depended on them. So the quality of a plan comes down to one question: how good is the planner's estimate of the number of rows at each step?

Statistics and ANALYZE

The ANALYZE command reads a random sample of a table, 30,000 rows by default, and stores a summary of each column. Autovacuum runs it automatically once about 10% of a table's rows have changed.

Selectivity estimation

The selectivity of a condition is the fraction of rows that pass it. The estimated row count is the table's row count multiplied by the selectivities of the conditions.

ConditionHow the planner estimates itExample, on 5 million orders
status = 'pending'The value is in the common-values list: use its frequency0.0201 × 5 M = 100,500
customer_id = 4242Not a common value: share the remaining rows equally among the remaining distinct values1 ÷ 51,000 customers × 5 M = 98
total_cents > 50000Find where the value falls in the histogram, and take the fraction of buckets above it7 of 100 buckets: 350,000
a AND bMultiply the two selectivities, assuming the conditions are independent0.0201 × 0.07 × 5 M = 7,035
a OR bAdd them and subtract the product(0.0201 + 0.07 − 0.0014) × 5 M
lower(email) = 'x', or any expression it has no statistics forA fixed guess: 0.5% for an equality, 33% for an inequality25,000, or 1.67 million, whatever the data
A join on a.x = b.xThe product of the two inputs' row counts, divided by the larger of the two n_distinct valuesEvery order matches its one customer

Three of these rows are where estimates fail. The statistics describe the table as it was when last analysed. The rule for AND assumes that columns have nothing to do with each other. And an expression the planner can't see into gets a constant.

      The join costs are the model from Chapter 18: an index nested loop costs about 16 for each outer row, and a hash join about 25,000 plus a little for each row, three times as much on the I/O if its hash table spills. The planner picks whichever is cheaper for the number of rows it expects. "Real cost" is what the chosen plan costs for the rows that actually arrive.

      Things to notice. For the common value the estimate is within 1% and the plan is the right one. For a value that didn't exist when the table was last analysed, the planner expects 1 row and gets 300,000, picks a nested loop, and the query costs 82 times what it should. For the two correlated columns the planner multiplies 3% by 3% and expects 900 rows where there are 30,000, and again picks the loop, 19 times too slow. For the arithmetic it can't use the histogram at all, guesses a third of the table, and builds a hash join for 200 rows that an index would have fetched for an eighth of the cost. The fourth case is an overestimate, which costs much less than the underestimates do. With each remedy applied the estimate comes right, and the planner changes its choice without being told to.

      Correlated columns

      The independence assumption is wrong whenever one column tells you something about another: city and postcode, make and model, country and currency, a status and the date range in which rows have that status. Every row with currency JPY has country JP, so the second condition removes nothing, and the planner has multiplied by it anyway. Each extra correlated condition divides the estimate again, and a query with three of them can be out by a factor of thousands.

      CREATE STATISTICS orders_country_currency (dependencies, ndistinct, mcv)
  ON country, currency FROM orders;
ANALYZE orders;                             -- extended statistics are gathered by ANALYZE, like the others

      Extended statistics tell the planner to measure a group of columns together: how strongly one determines another, how many distinct combinations there are, and which combinations are most common. They cost a little at ANALYZE time and nothing at query time. PostgreSQL doesn't create them by itself, because it can't know which of the many possible column pairs matter. You do, from plans where two conditions on one table give an estimate far too low.

      Join ordering

      Chapter 18 showed that the planner searches for the cheapest order in which to join tables. That search runs on estimates of the size of each intermediate result, and each of those is made from the estimates below it. An error of 3× in each of five joins can leave the estimate for the final result out by 3⁵, which is 243. Errors that are tolerable for a single table compound into nonsense by the top of a large plan, and the planner then chooses join algorithms and an order for inputs it has badly mismeasured.

      🔬
      "How good are query optimizers, really?"

      In 2015 a group of researchers in Munich, led by Viktor Leis, published a study with that title, which measured something database developers had long argued about without data. They took a real dataset, the Internet Movie Database, whose columns are full of the correlations that real data has, and wrote 113 queries over it with between 3 and 16 joins each. They then asked PostgreSQL and four other database systems, commercial ones among them, to estimate the size of every intermediate result, and compared the estimates with the truth. For conditions on a single table the estimates were mostly good. For the results of joins they were poor in every system, and they got worse with each join added: for queries with many joins, estimates wrong by a factor of a thousand were ordinary, and most of the errors were underestimates, which is the dangerous direction. The authors then separated the two halves of the optimiser's job. When they gave PostgreSQL the true row counts, its plans were nearly always close to the best possible, even with its simple cost formulas. When they improved the cost formulas and left the estimates alone, little changed. Their conclusion was that the cost model matters far less than the row estimates, and the estimates fail because real columns are correlated. For an engineer reading a slow plan, the practical advice follows directly: look at the row estimates before anything else, and expect them to be worst in the deepest joins.

      When to override the planner

      PostgreSQL has no hints in its SQL, by deliberate choice: a hint fixes a plan for today's data and goes on being obeyed when the data has changed. The intended fix is to give the planner better information. The remedies below are in the order to try them.

      -- Before: the planner can't use the histogram for an expression
WHERE total_cents / 100 > 5000             -- a fixed guess: a third of the table
WHERE date_trunc('day', created_at) = '2026-10-07'

-- After: the bare column on one side
WHERE total_cents > 500000                 -- estimated from the histogram, and able to use an index
WHERE created_at >= '2026-10-07' AND created_at < '2026-10-08'
      ⚠️
      Common mistakes
      • Querying straight after a bulk load. Autovacuum hasn't analysed the new rows yet. Run ANALYZE as the last step of the load.
      • Wrapping a column in a function in WHERE. It hides the column's statistics, and its indexes too.
      • Reaching for enable_seqscan = off first. It doesn't fix the estimate, and it applies to the whole session.
      • Leaving random_page_cost at 4 on SSDs. The planner then avoids index scans it should be using.
      • Assuming temporary tables have statistics. Autovacuum can't see them. Analyse them yourself after filling them.
      • Testing on data without the production data's skew. Uniform test data has no correlations and no hot values, so every estimate is good and every plan is fine.
      🎤
      Interview drill

      Explain it simply: How does a database decide whether to use an index for a query?

      How a strong answer sounds
      It is a cost-based decision. The planner generates the candidate plans, a sequential scan, an index scan on each usable index, a bitmap scan, and estimates a cost for each as the sum of the page reads and row operations it will do, weighted by configured costs: sequential pages are cheap, random pages cost more, and each row processed costs a little. The key input is selectivity: what fraction of the rows the predicate will match. It estimates that from statistics that ANALYZE collects by sampling each column: the fraction of NULLs, the number of distinct values, a list of the most common values with their frequencies, and a histogram of the rest. If the predicate matches a small fraction, the index scan's few random reads are cheaper than reading the whole table; if it matches, say, a fifth of the table, the random reads would cost more than a sequential scan, and the planner correctly ignores the index. So "the database isn't using my index" usually means either that it estimated, perhaps rightly, that the index wouldn't help, or that its estimate is wrong.

      Deeper: A plan shows an estimate of 50 rows where 200,000 actually came back. What could cause that, and how do you fix each cause?

      How a strong answer sounds
      Four usual causes. First, stale statistics: the table has changed a lot since it was last analysed, for instance after a bulk load, or the queried value is new and isn't in the most-common-values list, so the planner treats it as rare. Fix: ANALYZE, and make autovacuum analyse that table more often. Second, correlated columns: the query has several predicates on the same table, such as city and postcode, and the planner multiplies their selectivities as if independent, underestimating by the product. Fix: CREATE STATISTICS on that column group, with dependencies and MCV, then ANALYZE. Third, a predicate the planner can't estimate: a function or arithmetic on the column, a cast, a LIKE with a leading wildcard, a comparison with a subquery result. It falls back to a default selectivity. Fix: rewrite so the bare column is compared with a constant or range, or create an expression index, which gathers statistics on the expression. Fourth, skew that the sample missed: a column with many distinct values where a few are very common but not captured in a 100-entry MCV list. Fix: raise the statistics target for that column. And if the misestimate is on a join result several levels up, it has probably compounded from a smaller error lower down, so I'd find the lowest node where estimate and actual diverge and fix that one. Only if none of these works would I constrain the planner, with a materialised CTE or a session-local setting for that query.

      🧠 Key takeaways

      • A plan's cost is page reads and rows processed, multiplied by fixed prices. The uncertain part is the row counts.
      • ANALYZE samples each column and stores its NULL fraction, distinct count, most common values, histogram and physical correlation.
      • Selectivity comes from those: a common value's frequency, a share of the remainder, a fraction of the histogram, and a product for AND.
      • Estimates fail when statistics are stale, when columns are correlated, and when a condition hides the column inside an expression.
      • Errors multiply through joins, so the deepest joins of a large query are the worst estimated. Underestimates do the most damage.
      • Fix the information before the plan: ANALYZE, statistics targets, extended statistics, a sargable rewrite. Force the planner only as a last resort.
