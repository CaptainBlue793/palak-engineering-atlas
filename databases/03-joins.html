Joins

Normalised data lives in several tables, so almost every useful query joins them back together: orders with their customers, posts with their authors, employees with their managers. Joins are also where most wrong answers come from. Not errors, wrong answers: customers who silently disappear, revenue counted twice, a LEFT JOIN that quietly became an inner join. This chapter covers every join type with a visualiser you can drive, then the two traps that catch experienced engineers: filters in the wrong clause, and fan-out.

  • Write inner, left, right, full outer and cross joins and predict exactly which rows each returns
  • Use self joins for hierarchies and pairs
  • Place outer-join filters in ON or WHERE deliberately, knowing the difference
  • Recognise fan-out (rows multiplied by a one-to-many join) and fix inflated aggregates
  • Use semi-joins and anti-joins (EXISTS / NOT EXISTS) when you only need to test for a match

Inner join

An inner join returns the pairs of rows, one from each table, for which the join condition is true. Rows with no partner don't appear at all:

SELECT c.name, o.order_id, o.total_cents
FROM   customers c
JOIN   orders o ON o.customer_id = c.customer_id      -- JOIN means INNER JOIN
ORDER BY c.name, o.order_id;

Give every table a short alias and qualify every column (c.name, o.total_cents): it makes the query readable and keeps it working when someone later adds a column with the same name to the other table. JOIN orders USING (customer_id) is a shorthand when the column names match; avoid NATURAL JOIN, which joins on every column with a shared name, so adding an innocent created_at to both tables changes its meaning.

Outer joins: keeping rows without a partner

A LEFT JOIN returns every row of the left table; where there's no match, the right side's columns are NULL. RIGHT JOIN is the mirror image (rarely used: swap the tables and use LEFT). FULL JOIN keeps unmatched rows from both sides. Drive the visualiser: the customers table has a customer with no orders (Sam), and the orders table has a guest order with no customer.

ON versus WHERE in outer joins

For an inner join it makes no difference whether a condition sits in ON or WHERE. For an outer join it changes the result, and getting it wrong is one of the most common SQL bugs. Suppose you want every customer with their 2026 orders, keeping customers who have none:

-- WRONG: the WHERE runs after the join. Customers without 2026 orders have o.created_at NULL,
-- "NULL >= '2026-01-01'" is unknown, so they're removed. This is now an inner join.
SELECT c.name, o.order_id
FROM   customers c
LEFT JOIN orders o ON o.customer_id = c.customer_id
WHERE  o.created_at >= '2026-01-01';

-- RIGHT: the condition is part of what counts as a match. Unmatched customers stay, with NULLs.
SELECT c.name, o.order_id
FROM   customers c
LEFT JOIN orders o ON o.customer_id = c.customer_id
                 AND o.created_at >= '2026-01-01';

The rule: conditions on the preserved side (the left table of a LEFT JOIN) go in WHERE; conditions on the optional side go in ON, unless you genuinely mean "only rows that matched", in which case say so with an inner join.

Self joins

A table can be joined to itself under two aliases. Hierarchies are the classic case: each employee row stores its manager's employee_id.

SELECT e.name AS employee, m.name AS manager
FROM   employees e
LEFT JOIN employees m ON m.employee_id = e.manager_id;    -- LEFT: the CEO has no manager

-- pairs of users in the same city, each pair once
SELECT a.user_id, b.user_id
FROM   users a
JOIN   users b ON b.city = a.city AND b.user_id > a.user_id;

The b.user_id > a.user_id condition removes self-pairs (Ana with Ana) and mirror pairs (Ana–Tom and Tom–Ana). One self join walks one level of a hierarchy; walking any number of levels needs a recursive CTE (Chapter 5).

Fan-out: when joins multiply rows

Joining along a one-to-many relationship repeats the "one" side for each of its "many". That's correct and expected: an order with three items appears three times when joined to its items. The trap is aggregating a column from the "one" side after such a join. Join orders to payments (one order, two payments) and SUM(o.total_cents) counts that order's total twice. Join to items and payments and you get items × payments copies. The query runs, the numbers look plausible, and they're wrong.

SELECT c.name, sum(o.total_cents) AS ordered, sum(p.paid_cents) AS paid
FROM   customers c
JOIN   orders o ON o.customer_id = c.customer_id
LEFT JOIN (SELECT order_id, sum(amount_cents) AS paid_cents     -- one row per order
           FROM   payments
           GROUP BY order_id) p ON p.order_id = o.order_id
GROUP BY c.name;

Semi-joins and anti-joins

When you only need to know whether a match exists, don't join and deduplicate; ask the question directly. EXISTS returns each outer row at most once however many matches it has (a semi-join), and NOT EXISTS returns the rows with no match (an anti-join). PostgreSQL plans both as dedicated join types that stop at the first match.

SELECT c.customer_id, c.name
FROM   customers c
WHERE  NOT EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM orders o WHERE o.customer_id = c.customer_id);   -- never ordered

Prefer NOT EXISTS to NOT IN (SELECT …): as P2 showed, a single NULL in the subquery makes NOT IN return nothing. How the database executes all these joins, by nested loops, hashing or merging, is Chapter 18.

📊
The revenue dashboard that grew 30% overnight

A typical analytics failure: a revenue dashboard is orders JOIN customers, summed by month, and it's right. Then a product manager asks for revenue by promotion, and someone adds LEFT JOIN order_promotions. Most orders have zero or one promotion, so the totals barely move in testing. But a seasonal campaign lets orders stack two or three promotions, and that month's revenue jumps 30%, which is celebrated for a week before finance's numbers from the payment provider disagree. The join multiplied every stacked order. The fix is to allocate revenue to promotions in a pre-aggregated subquery, and the habit that would have caught it is checking row counts per order_id whenever a join is added to a query that feeds a SUM.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Filtering the optional side of a LEFT JOIN in WHERE. It turns the outer join into an inner join.
  • Summing across a one-to-many join. Aggregate the many-side first, or join on pre-aggregated subqueries.
  • Patching duplicates with DISTINCT. Find the join that multiplies instead.
  • Forgetting the join condition (or writing FROM a, b): a Cartesian product.
  • COUNT(*) after a LEFT JOIN counts 1 for customers with no orders; count a right-side column, COUNT(o.order_id), which skips NULLs.
🎤
Interview drill

Explain it simply: Write a query for customers who have never placed an order, and explain why you chose that form.

How a strong answer sounds
SELECT c.* FROM customers c WHERE NOT EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM orders o WHERE o.customer_id = c.customer_id). It's an anti-join: the database stops at the first match per customer and never multiplies rows. The LEFT JOIN orders o … WHERE o.order_id IS NULL form is equivalent and also fine. I'd avoid NOT IN (SELECT customer_id FROM orders), because if any order has a NULL customer_id, for example a guest checkout, NOT IN returns no rows at all.

Deeper: A report sums order totals per customer and suddenly shows double after someone joined the shipments table. What happened and how do you fix it?

How a strong answer sounds
Fan-out: orders to shipments is one-to-many, so orders shipped in two parcels appear twice in the join and their totals are summed twice. I'd confirm by counting rows per order_id in the joined result. The fix depends on what's needed from shipments: if it's a fact like "number of parcels" or "last shipped date", aggregate shipments per order in a subquery and join that, one row per order; if it's only "has shipped", use EXISTS. I wouldn't add DISTINCT, which hides the cause and can merge genuinely distinct rows.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • INNER keeps matched pairs; LEFT also keeps unmatched left rows with NULLs; FULL keeps unmatched rows from both sides.
  • In outer joins, conditions on the optional side belong in ON; in WHERE they discard the NULL rows.
  • Self joins use two aliases of one table: managers, pairs, comparisons between rows.
  • One-to-many joins repeat rows: aggregate the many-side first before summing.
  • Use EXISTS / NOT EXISTS to test for matches; avoid NOT IN over nullable columns.

Check your understanding

customers has 4 rows; one customer has no orders. orders has 5 rows, all with a valid customer except one guest order (customer_id NULL). How many rows does customers c LEFT JOIN orders o ON o.customer_id = c.customer_id return?

The four orders with customers each produce a row, the customer with no orders adds one NULL-padded row, and the guest order (right side, unmatched) is dropped by a LEFT join: 5 rows.

What does adding WHERE o.status = 'paid' to customers c LEFT JOIN orders o ON … do?

For unmatched customers o.status is NULL, the comparison is unknown, and WHERE drops the row. Put the condition in ON to keep them.

An order of £100 has two payment rows. A query joins orders to payments and computes SUM(o.total). What does it report for that order?

Fan-out: the one-to-many join repeats the order row for each payment. Aggregate payments per order first, or don't join them.

In a self join listing pairs of users from the same city, why add b.user_id > a.user_id?

Without it you'd get (Ana, Ana) and both (Ana, Tom) and (Tom, Ana). The strict inequality keeps exactly one ordering of each pair.

After customers LEFT JOIN orders, which expression correctly counts each customer's orders, giving 0 for customers with none?

COUNT(column) skips NULLs, and unmatched customers have o.order_id NULL. COUNT(*) counts the NULL-padded row as 1.
