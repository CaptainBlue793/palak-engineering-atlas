Replication

A database on one machine is lost when that machine is, and can serve only as many reads as that machine can manage. Replication keeps complete copies of the database on other machines, continuously up to date, or very nearly. The words "very nearly" are where the difficulty lies. A copy that is a fraction of a second behind is good enough to take over after a failure, and it will then be missing the last fraction of a second of commits. A copy that is guaranteed to have everything makes every commit wait for it. This chapter covers how the copies are kept, the choice between waiting and not waiting, what happens to reads from a copy that is behind, and what has to go right when a copy takes over.

Replication does not protect against mistakes. A DROP TABLE on the primary is faithfully dropped on every replica a few milliseconds later. That is what backups are for (Chapter 35).

Streaming (physical) replication

Chapter 20 showed that a database can rebuild its data files by replaying the log after a crash. Streaming replication uses that directly. A standby starts from a copy of the primary's data files and stays permanently in recovery: the primary sends it the WAL as it is written, over an ordinary connection, and the standby replays it. The standby is the same database, byte for byte, a little behind.

-- On the primary: one row for each standby
SELECT application_name, state, sync_state, sent_lsn, flush_lsn, replay_lsn, replay_lag
FROM pg_stat_replication;
--  standby_a | streaming | sync  | 0/3A4F2D20 | 0/3A4F2D20 | 0/3A4F2D20 | 00:00:00.0021
--  standby_b | streaming | async | 0/3A4F2D20 | 0/3A4E1108 | 0/3A4E1108 | 00:00:00.4130

A standby can answer read-only queries while it replays. It can't accept writes of any kind, and it can't have different indexes or tables from the primary, since it is a physical copy.

Logical replication

Logical replication sends the same changes in a different form. The primary decodes its WAL back into row-level operations, "insert this row into orders, update the row with this key", and a subscriber applies them as ordinary writes to its own, independent database.

-- On the source
CREATE PUBLICATION orders_pub FOR TABLE orders, order_items;
-- On the destination, which already has those tables
CREATE SUBSCRIPTION orders_sub
  CONNECTION 'host=primary dbname=shop user=repl' PUBLICATION orders_pub;

MySQL's replication has always been of the logical kind: replicas apply a "binary log" of row changes, separate from the storage engine's own log. That is one reason MySQL has long allowed replicas of a different version, and one of the differences examined in Chapter 43.

Sync vs async

When the primary tells a client "committed", how many machines have that commit? The setting synchronous_commit decides, for each transaction if you wish.

synchronous_commitCOMMIT returns whenA committed transaction can be lost if
offThe record is in the primary's memoryThe primary crashes within the next fraction of a second
local, or on with no synchronous standbyIt is flushed to the primary's disk. Standbys receive it afterwards: asynchronous replicationThe primary is lost before the standby has received it
on, with a synchronous standbyIt is flushed on the primary and on the standbyBoth machines are lost together
remote_applyThe standby has also replayed it, so a read there will see itBoth machines are lost together

Synchronous replication has two costs. Every commit takes at least one round trip to the standby longer, which is under a millisecond in the same data centre and tens of milliseconds between regions (Networks, Chapter 38). And if the synchronous standby is unreachable, commits can't complete. A cluster with one synchronous standby is less available for writes than a single machine, since it now needs both to be up. The usual answer is to have two or more candidates and wait for any one:

synchronous_standby_names = 'ANY 1 (standby_a, standby_b)'     # a quorum: either one will do

Things to notice. Asynchronous, with the standby keeping up: commits take 1 ms, and losing the primary loses nothing. Asynchronous and lagging: commits are just as fast, a read from the standby is three transactions out of date, and a failover loses three transactions that their clients were told had committed. Asynchronous and disconnected: the primary carries on without complaint, and the standby falls further behind with every commit. Synchronous and healthy: 3 ms, and a failover loses nothing, by construction. Synchronous with a slow standby: nothing is lost and every commit takes 400 ms, because each one waits for the slow machine. Synchronous and disconnected: the commit doesn't return. Nothing is lost and nothing can be written either. Asynchronous replication can lose commits it has acknowledged, and synchronous replication stops acknowledging when the standby is unreachable. A second standby with a quorum is the usual way to have neither problem.

Replication lag

An asynchronous standby is always some distance behind, and the distance varies. It grows when the primary writes faster than the standby can replay: replay on a standby is largely done by one process, where the primary's writes came from hundreds of sessions at once. It grows when a query on the standby needs old row versions that replay wants to remove, and replay has to wait. And it grows after any single large transaction.

Lag causes a bug that every application with read replicas meets. A user saves a change, which goes to the primary. The page reloads, and that read goes to a replica which hasn't yet received the change. The user sees their old data and concludes that the save failed.

Failover and split brain

Promoting a standby is one command, and it takes a second or two. Everything round it is harder.

Split brain is the state in which two machines both believe they are the primary and both accept writes. It happens when the old primary wasn't dead, only unreachable from wherever the decision was made. Each now has commits the other lacks, and there is no automatic way to merge two diverged histories: somebody has to decide which writes to keep. Preventing it requires that at most one machine can ever hold the right to be primary, which is a consensus problem, and tools such as Patroni solve it by keeping a lock in a consensus store like etcd. Chapter 44 follows a real failover in which 43 seconds of network trouble led to a day of reconciling two sets of writes.

Facebook's remote markers: knowing when not to trust a replica

Facebook ran its main database with one region holding the primaries and other regions holding replicas, with a cache in front of the databases in each region. A user in a replica region who changed something, a profile picture for instance, had the write sent across the country to the primary, and their next page view was served from the local cache and the local replica. Replication between regions took a noticeable time, so the local replica often hadn't received the change yet. The user saw the old picture, and worse, the old value was put back into the local cache, where it could stay. In a paper published in 2013 on how they scaled their cache, Facebook's engineers described the fix. When a web server in a replica region performs a write that affects some key, it first sets a small remote marker for that key in the local cache, then sends the write to the primary region, then deletes the key from the local cache. A later request for that key finds nothing cached, looks for a marker, and if one is there sends its read to the primary region and not to the local replica. When the change arrives through replication, the replication stream itself removes the marker. The scheme costs some cross-region reads, only for keys that were just written and only for the short time until the replica has caught up. It is the first row of the table above, done for each key separately. Most systems with replicas need some rule of this kind, since the application can only know that a replica is behind for a particular key by keeping track of what it has just written.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Treating a replica as a backup. It reproduces mistakes and corruption within milliseconds.
  • One synchronous standby. Its failure stops all writes. Use a quorum of two or more candidates.
  • Reading from replicas without thinking about lag. Users will write, then read, and see the old value.
  • No alert on lag, or on unused replication slots. A slot whose standby has gone keeps WAL for ever and fills the primary's disk.
  • Automatic failover without fencing. A primary that was only slow comes back, and there are two.
  • Never rehearsing a failover. The first one will then happen during an incident, with untested tooling.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is the difference between synchronous and asynchronous replication?

How a strong answer sounds
It is about when the primary acknowledges a commit to the client. With asynchronous replication it acknowledges as soon as the commit is durable on its own disk, and ships the change to replicas afterwards. Commits are as fast as on a single node and don't depend on replicas being up, but a replica is always slightly behind, so if the primary is lost, the most recent acknowledged commits can be lost with it, and reads from a replica can be stale. With synchronous replication the primary waits until at least one replica confirms that it has the commit on disk before acknowledging. A failover then loses no acknowledged commit, at the price of an extra network round trip on every commit and a dependency: if the synchronous replica is unreachable, commits block. So in practice people configure a quorum, any one of two or more replicas, to keep both durability and availability, and they often choose per transaction: synchronous for payments, asynchronous for less critical writes. Note that synchronous in this sense still doesn't make reads on the replica current unless you wait for apply as well as for flush.

Deeper: After adding read replicas, users report that they sometimes don't see changes they have just made. What is happening, and what are your options?

How a strong answer sounds
That is replication lag breaking read-your-writes consistency. The write goes to the primary; the following read is routed to an asynchronous replica that hasn't yet replayed that write, so it returns the old state. Lag is normally milliseconds but grows under write bursts, large transactions, or when long queries on the replica delay replay. The options, roughly from cheapest: first, session stickiness to the primary after a write: for some seconds after a user writes, route that user's reads to the primary; simple and usually enough. Second, causal reads by position: capture the WAL position, the LSN, returned by the write, and have the read wait on the replica until it has replayed past that LSN, or fall back to the primary if it takes too long; precise, and it only delays reads that follow writes. Third, make specific commits wait for replica apply with synchronous_commit = remote_apply, which guarantees any replica read afterwards sees it, but slows those writes and ties them to replica health. Fourth, classify queries: anything where a user views their own recent data goes to the primary, and replicas serve content where staleness is harmless. I'd also monitor lag and take a replica out of rotation when it exceeds a threshold, and I'd watch for the nastier variant, monotonic reads, where consecutive reads hit replicas with different lag and data appears to go backwards.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • A streaming standby is a copy that replays the primary's WAL continuously. It is identical, read-only and a little behind.
  • Logical replication sends row changes for chosen tables to an independent database, which allows upgrades, partial copies and feeds to other systems.
  • Asynchronous commits are fast and can be lost in a failover. Synchronous commits survive it, cost a round trip, and stall if the standby is unreachable.
  • A quorum of standbys gives durability without depending on any single one.
  • Replicas lag, so a read straight after a write may not see it. Route such reads to the primary, or wait for the replica to reach the write's LSN.
  • Failover needs certainty that the old primary is out. Two primaries accepting writes is split brain, and has no automatic repair.
