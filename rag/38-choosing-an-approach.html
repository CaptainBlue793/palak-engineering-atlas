RAG, Long Context or Fine-Tuning: Choosing

There are three ways to give a model knowledge it was not trained on:

  • retrieve the relevant passages at question time (RAG);
  • put whole documents in a long context window;
  • train the knowledge into the model's weights.

Context windows have grown from thousands to millions of tokens, which makes "just put everything in the prompt" tempting, and fine-tuning has become cheap. Each approach suits different needs, and the choice turns on cost, freshness, scale and what you are really trying to change.

The lab measures the trade on a question the model can be graded on exactly: which of four definitions matches a term. A 3B model answers 150 such questions four ways: from its own knowledge, from 5 retrieved chunks, from the whole defining chapter, and from three chapters at once. Each way records accuracy, prompt tokens and time.

  • Separate adding knowledge from changing behaviour
  • Measure what long context costs in tokens, time and accuracy
  • Know what fine-tuning is good at, and what it is not
  • Combine approaches where each is strongest
  • Use a decision guide grounded in measurements

Knowledge vs behaviour

Start by naming what has to change. Knowledge means facts the model must use: your policies, documents, prices, today's data. Behaviour means how it answers: format, tone, following a schema, using your terminology, refusing out-of-scope questions. Retrieval and long context add knowledge at question time, with sources you can cite and update. Fine-tuning mainly changes behaviour. It can also add knowledge, but that knowledge is hard to update, hard to cite and prone to confident errors. Most products need knowledge from retrieval and, sometimes, behaviour from fine-tuning.

Long-context costs

Putting whole documents in the prompt avoids retrieval errors, provided the document is the right one and it fits. It pays in tokens on every question, in prefill time, and in attention spread across text that does not matter (Chapter 16). In the lab:

  • five retrieved chunks took about 1,030 prompt tokens;
  • the whole defining chapter took about 3,773 tokens;
  • three chapters took about 10,177 tokens.

The whole chapter was 100% right against 93% for retrieved chunks, at about 4 times the tokens. Adding two other chapters left it at 99%, for nearly three times the tokens again.

material = {
    "none":     "",                                         # the model's own knowledge
    "rag":      top_k_chunks(term, k=5),                  # ~1k tokens
    "chapter":  whole_chapter(defining_chapter),          # several thousand tokens
    "three":    two_other_chapters + defining_chapter,    # long and noisy
}[mode]
answer = llm(SYS, material + multiple_choice(term, right_definition, three_distractors))

Fine-tuning for format

Fine-tuning a generator pays off for behaviour: a strict output format, a house style, domain phrasing, consistent refusals, or a smaller model doing a narrow task as well as a larger one. It is weak as a way to store facts that change, because updating means retraining, and the model cannot cite where a fact came from. For retrievers it is often worthwhile; Chapter 33 gained 6 points of recall on held-out terms in minutes of training. The lab's "no context" condition shows the base model's own knowledge: with four distinct definitions to choose from it was right 91% of the time, because most of these terms are general engineering vocabulary. Knowledge specific to your organisation, such as its policies, products and numbers, is exactly what it would not have, and what retrieval supplies.

Things to try.

  • No context → retrieved chunks: accuracy rose from 91% to 93%, prompt tokens went from 142 to 1,030, and time per question from 0.4 s to 0.7 s.
  • Retrieved chunks → whole chapter: accuracy rose from 93% to 100%, prompt tokens went from 1,030 to 3,773, and time per question from 0.7 s to 2.4 s.
  • Whole chapter → three chapters: accuracy stayed about level (100%, then 99%), prompt tokens went from 3,773 to 10,177, and time per question from 2.4 s to 7.5 s.

Combining them

The approaches stack:

  • retrieval to find the right documents;
  • long context to read the most relevant whole sections instead of fragments (Chapter 24);
  • a fine-tuned embedder for domain vocabulary (Chapter 33);
  • a lightly fine-tuned generator for format and tone.

Long context also changes RAG itself, because with large windows you can afford more and bigger chunks. Retrieval quality still decides whether the right ones are among them, and the bill grows with every token.

A decision guide

  • Small, stable corpus that fits comfortably in context: long context with caching of the prompt prefix may be simplest.
  • Large or changing corpus, permissions, citations: RAG.
  • The model must follow a format or style, or a small model must do a narrow task well: fine-tune the generator.
  • Domain vocabulary defeats retrieval: fine-tune the retriever.

Always measure on your own questions. The lab's numbers show the direction, not your answer.

Use case: "Fine-Tuning or Retrieval?" and the long-context debate

In 2023 Oded Ovadia and colleagues at Microsoft published "Fine-Tuning or Retrieval? Comparing Knowledge Injection in LLMs". They tested both ways of teaching models facts they had not seen, including events after their training cut-off. Retrieval consistently beat unsupervised fine-tuning at knowledge injection, and models struggled to learn new facts from fine-tuning at all unless the facts appeared in many paraphrases. In 2024, as context windows grew to a million tokens, several studies compared long context with RAG. Long context often did well when everything fitted, while RAG stayed far cheaper per question and better suited to large, changing or permissioned corpora. Some proposed routing between the two per question. The lab's small experiment shows the same economics: every extra token of context costs time on every question.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Fine-tuning to add changing facts.
  • Stuffing the context because it fits.
  • Comparing approaches without counting tokens.
  • Assuming long context reads evenly.
  • Choosing one approach for every need.
  • Skipping the prompt.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: When would you use RAG, long context or fine-tuning?

How a strong answer sounds
Decide whether you need knowledge or behaviour. For knowledge that is large, changing, permissioned or needs citations, use RAG. For a small, stable set of documents that fits, long context can be simpler, but it costs tokens and prefill time on every question and models read long contexts unevenly. Fine-tune the generator for behaviour (format, style, a narrow task on a smaller model), not to store facts that change. Fine-tune the retriever when domain vocabulary defeats it. They combine, and each choice should be measured on accuracy, tokens and latency with your own questions.

Deeper: A stakeholder asks why not just put the whole knowledge base in a million-token context. How do you answer?

How a strong answer sounds
It may work for a small, stable knowledge base, but check four things. Cost: every question pays for every token, so a million-token prompt per question is orders of magnitude dearer than a few retrieved chunks, though prompt caching helps for a fixed prefix. Latency: time to first token grows with the prompt. Quality: models use long contexts unevenly, and in testing two extra, irrelevant chapters nearly tripled the prompt and the time for no gain. Operations: permissions, freshness and citations are much easier when you retrieve per question. I would measure accuracy, tokens and latency for both on our questions, and consider routing or using long context only for the retrieved top documents.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Retrieval and long context add knowledge; fine-tuning mainly changes behaviour.
  • Long context pays in tokens and time on every question, and reads unevenly.
  • Fine-tune retrievers for vocabulary, generators for format, not for changing facts.
  • The approaches stack; combine them where each is strongest.
  • Decide with measured accuracy, tokens and latency on your own questions.
