The RAG Interview Playbook

AI engineering interviews test RAG in four ways:

  • a design round ("build a support assistant over our help centre");
  • concept questions ("why rerank?");
  • debugging scenarios ("answers got worse after the migration");
  • sometimes a take-home project.

Strong candidates share a habit: they reason from measurements, name trade-offs, and say how they would check a claim instead of asserting it.

This chapter gives a framework for each format and collects the course's measured results in one place, 18 numbers pulled directly from the chapters' experiments, as evidence you can reason from. Every number links back to the chapter that measured it.

  • Run a RAG design round with a repeatable framework
  • Answer concept questions with mechanisms and trade-offs
  • Work through debugging scenarios stage by stage
  • Scope a take-home project that shows judgement
  • Use measured numbers as evidence, with their limits

RAG design rounds

A design round rewards structure. Spend the first minutes on requirements:

  • who asks what;
  • the size and change rate of the corpus;
  • permissions;
  • latency and cost targets;
  • what a wrong answer costs.

Then sketch the offline and online paths (Chapter 35) and go deep where the requirements bite. For a help centre, that is freshness and escalation. For legal documents, citations and abstention. For a multi-tenant SaaS, permissions and caches. Finish with evaluation, a golden set and online metrics, and with the failure modes. Interviewers listen for trade-offs stated with their costs.

1. Requirements   users, questions, corpus size and churn, permissions, latency, cost, risk of a wrong answer
2. Data           sources → extraction → chunking (structure-aware) → metadata and ACLs → embeddings → stores
3. Retrieval      hybrid (BM25 + dense) → filters inside the search → rerank if it measurably helps
4. Generation     numbered sources, citations, an "I don't know" path, a token budget, question at both ends
5. Evaluation     golden set (real questions), recall@k, faithfulness judge checked against humans, significance
6. Operations     versioned indexes, caches keyed by permissions, traces, feedback, fallbacks, cost per question

Concept questions

Answer concept questions with the mechanism, the trade-off and the evidence. "Why hybrid search?": keywords match exact terms, embeddings match meaning, and they fail on different queries. But fusion needs normalisation or a small RRF k, and on SciFact the textbook k = 60 lost to dense retrieval alone (Chapter 12). Every chapter of this course ends with two drill questions in that style. Revisiting them is the most efficient preparation.

Debugging scenarios

Debugging questions test method. Walk the pipeline backwards from the bad answer (Chapter 37):

  • Was the evidence in the corpus and extracted cleanly?
  • Was it retrieved, and ranked high enough?
  • Was it in the prompt, and where?
  • Did the model use it, and cite it?

Name the measurement that answers each question, and propose a fix together with the test that would catch a regression. Typical scenarios:

  • quality dropped after re-chunking or a model change;
  • small tenants get worse answers;
  • a reranker made things worse;
  • answers cite the wrong source;
  • cost doubled after adding "more context".

Take-home projects

A take-home RAG project should show judgement, not breadth:

  • a small but real corpus;
  • a labelled set of 50 to 100 real-looking questions;
  • a baseline and one or two measured improvements, with intervals (Chapter 19);
  • citations and an abstention path;
  • a short write-up of what did not work and why.

A README that says "hybrid search improved recall@5 from 0.76 to 0.87 on 120 questions (95% interval +0.07 to +0.14), while reranking did not help and was removed" beats a dozen unmeasured features.

A design framework

Across formats, the same six steps carry you: requirements, data, retrieval, generation, evaluation, operations. In each, state the default, the trade-off and how you would measure it. When you do not know a number, say how you would get it: a quick experiment on a sample, the shape you would expect, and what would change your mind. That is what this course has done in every chapter.

Use case: how AI engineering interviews changed

As retrieval-augmented systems moved into products from 2023 onwards, many companies added AI-system design rounds alongside classic system design. Candidates are asked to design a document assistant, a support bot or a code helper, and are then probed on evaluation, hallucination, permissions and cost. Interviewers report the same gap between candidates. Some list components: a vector database, an embedding model, an LLM. Others can say what they would measure, what usually goes wrong, and how they would know. This course's chapters each end in that second style, and its numbers come from experiments a candidate could rerun on a laptop, which is the strongest preparation of all.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Starting with the vector database.
  • Listing techniques without trade-offs.
  • No evaluation plan.
  • Quoting numbers as universal.
  • Ignoring permissions and deletion.
  • Over-building the take-home.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Design a question-answering assistant over a company's internal wiki.

How a strong answer sounds
Requirements: employees ask how-to and policy questions; the wiki has permissions and changes daily; answers must cite pages and say "I don't know" rather than guess; latency a few seconds. Offline: a connector syncs changed pages, extracts text by structure, chunks by section, copies page permissions into chunk metadata, embeds and writes to a hybrid index, versioned. Online: authenticate, retrieve with permission filters inside the search, rerank if it measurably helps, assemble a few numbered sources with the question at both ends, generate with citations, check citations and links. Evaluation: a golden set from real questions, recall@k and a faithfulness judge validated against human labels. Operations: freshness alerts, permission-scoped caches, traces with index versions, feedback turned into test cases, fallbacks for each dependency.

Deeper: The interviewer says "just add a reranker". How do you respond?

How a strong answer sounds
A reranker replaces the first stage's order with its own, so it helps only if it judges relevance better than the first stage on this data. I would measure it: recall@k and nDCG on the golden set, with and without, over each first stage we might ship, with per-query wins and losses and an interval. In testing, a popular MS MARCO cross-encoder lifted weak first stages but lowered a strong hybrid on scientific claims, and cost tens of milliseconds on a GPU or seconds on a CPU at depth 100. If it helps, choose the depth by the latency budget; if not, a domain-tuned reranker or spending the latency elsewhere may be better.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Design rounds: requirements first, then data, retrieval, generation, evaluation, operations.
  • Concept answers: mechanism, trade-off, evidence.
  • Debugging: walk the pipeline backwards with a measurement at each stage.
  • Take-homes: a baseline, measured changes with intervals, and an honest write-up.
  • Numbers are evidence of direction and size, not constants; say how you would measure yours.
