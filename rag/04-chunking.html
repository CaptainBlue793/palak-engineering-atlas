Chunking Strategies

Documents are too long to embed or to put in a prompt whole, so they are split into chunks, and each chunk is what gets embedded, retrieved and shown to the model. Chunk boundaries decide whether an answer arrives in one piece, split across two chunks, or buried in a chunk about something else. It is one of the most consequential choices in a RAG system, and one of the easiest to make by default without measuring.

This chapter covers why to chunk, fixed-size chunking with overlap, recursive and structural chunking, semantic chunking, and choosing a size. The lab chunks 266 chapters of the Atlas seven ways, embeds every chunk with bge-small, and runs the same 980 glossary queries against each.

Why chunk

Three limits force it. The embedding model reads at most a few hundred tokens and silently truncates the rest (P3). One vector per long document averages many topics into a blur, so specific questions match it weakly. And the prompt has a budget (P3), so sending whole documents leaves room for few of them. Smaller chunks are more precise and cheaper to include, but each carries less context, and an answer can be cut in half at a boundary. Larger chunks keep context together but match less precisely, cost more tokens, and past the embedding limit lose their endings entirely.

Fixed size and overlap

The simplest strategy cuts every N words or tokens. It is predictable and fast, and it ignores the document completely: a chunk can start mid-sentence and end mid-list. Overlap repeats the last part of each chunk at the start of the next, so an idea that straddles a boundary appears whole in at least one chunk, at the cost of more chunks to embed and store. Measure size in the embedding model's tokens, not characters, to stay under its limit.

def fixed_chunks(words: list[str], size: int = 200, overlap: int = 40) -> list[str]:
    step = size - overlap
    return [" ".join(words[i:i + size]) for i in range(0, max(1, len(words) - overlap), step)]

Recursive and structural chunking

Better boundaries come from the document itself. Structural chunking splits at headings, so each chunk is a section. Recursive chunking does that first and then, for any piece still over the limit, splits at the next level down: paragraphs, then sentences, then words. Each chunk also carries its section heading, which adds the context a paragraph often lacks ("Idempotency keys" above a paragraph that never repeats the phrase). Code splitters do the same with functions and classes (Chapter 27).

def recursive_chunks(sections: list[Section], limit: int = 200) -> list[str]:
    out = []
    for sec in sections:                                    # level 1: the document's own sections
        pieces = []
        for para in sec.paragraphs:                           # level 2: paragraphs; level 3: sentences
            pieces += split_sentences(para) if words(para) > limit else [para]
        cur = []
        for p in pieces:                                   # pack pieces up to the limit
            if cur and words(cur) + words(p) > limit:
                out.append(sec.heading + "\n" + "\n".join(cur)); cur = []
            cur.append(p)
        if cur: out.append(sec.heading + "\n" + "\n".join(cur))
    return out

Semantic chunking

Semantic chunking embeds every sentence and starts a new chunk where adjacent sentences are least similar, on the theory that topic shifts make good boundaries. It needs no structure, which helps with transcripts and badly formatted text, but it embeds the corpus twice (once per sentence, once per chunk), its boundaries depend on a threshold, and on documents that already have headings it rarely beats using them.

Things to try.

  • recursive, max 200: the best recall@1 and recall@5 with modest context, because chunks follow the document's sections and each carries its heading.
  • fixed 400: the worst result. 64% of its chunks exceed the 512-token limit, so their second halves were never embedded.
  • fixed 200 + overlap: overlap barely moved recall (0.521 → 0.533 at 1) while adding 21% more chunks. Boundaries in the right places matter more than boundaries repeated.
  • semantic: competitive recall@5, but its chunking alone took over four times as long as the others, for no gain over using the headings these documents already have.
  • The example: the fixed-size top chunk starts mid-sentence ("body. Keys shared across customers…"); the recursive one starts with its heading, "## Idempotency keys".

Choosing a size

Start from the structure of your documents and the embedding model's limit: recursive chunking along headings, a maximum comfortably below the limit (200 to 400 tokens for a 512-token model), each chunk prefixed with its title and section heading, and no overlap unless measurement shows it helps. Then measure on your own questions (Chapter 19), comparing two or three sizes on recall@k and the context they cost. Short factual questions favour smaller chunks; questions that need explanation favour larger ones, or small chunks retrieved and expanded to their parent section (Chapter 24).

Use case: Anthropic's contextual retrieval

In September 2024 Anthropic published "Introducing Contextual Retrieval", describing a fix for a problem this chapter's example shows: a chunk on its own often lacks the context that makes it findable, such as "the company's revenue grew by 3% over the previous quarter" without saying which company or quarter. Their method uses a language model to write a short, chunk-specific context for every chunk, situating it within the whole document, and prepends that context before embedding the chunk and before indexing it for BM25. They reported that this reduced the rate of failed retrievals (the top 20 missing the relevant chunk) by 49% when combining contextual embeddings with contextual BM25, and by 67% when a reranker was added. Prefixing each chunk with its title and section heading, as the recursive strategy here does, is the cheap version of the same idea; generating context with a model costs a call per chunk, which prompt caching makes affordable.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Chunks longer than the embedding model's limit, silently truncated.
  • Splitting by characters without regard to sentences or sections.
  • Dropping headings and titles, so chunks lose their context.
  • Large overlaps by default, paying for duplicates that do not help.
  • Choosing a size from a blog post instead of measuring on your questions.
  • Splitting tables and code blocks in the middle.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How would you chunk documents for a RAG system?

How a strong answer sounds
I split along the document's own structure: sections from the headings, and within any section that is too long, paragraphs, then sentences, packing pieces up to a maximum comfortably below the embedding model's token limit. Every chunk keeps its document title and section heading as a prefix, plus metadata such as source, page and position for citations. I avoid fixed-size chunks that cut sentences, and add overlap only if it measurably helps. Then I measure recall@k and the context cost per query on real questions for two or three sizes, and choose.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Chunk boundaries decide whether answers arrive whole; choose them deliberately.
  • Stay well under the embedding model's token limit, measured in its tokens.
  • Recursive, structure-aware chunking with headings prefixed beat fixed sizes here.
  • Overlap helped little; semantic chunking cost much more for no gain on structured text.
  • Measure recall@k and context cost on your own questions before choosing a size.
