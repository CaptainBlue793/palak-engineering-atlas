Embeddings in Practice

An embedding model turns a piece of text into a fixed-length vector, so that texts with similar meaning end up close together. That one property is what makes semantic search possible: "steps to change a forgotten password" finds a page titled "How do I reset my password?" with no words in common. It is also easy to over-trust. Embeddings capture topic and phrasing well, and they are surprisingly bad at the details that often matter most: "not", numbers and identifiers.

This chapter runs a real embedding model, bge-small-en-v1.5, and measures it: similarity for six kinds of sentence pair, throughput on CPU and GPU at different batch sizes, the storage cost of a corpus, and the nearest Atlas sections to a few questions.

  • Turn text into normalised embeddings with sentence-transformers
  • Use the query prefix the model was trained with
  • Estimate storage from dimensions and corpus size
  • Batch encoding sensibly on CPU and GPU
  • Know what embeddings miss, and what to use instead

Text to vectors

A bi-encoder embedding model reads one text and outputs one vector: here, 384 numbers. It was trained on pairs of texts that belong together (a question and its answer, a title and its body) so that matching pairs land close and others far apart. Documents are embedded once, when indexed; each query is embedded at search time with the same model, and similarity is the cosine between the two vectors, which after normalisation is just a dot product (P4).

from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer
model = SentenceTransformer("BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5", device="cuda")

docs = model.encode(chunks, batch_size=32, normalize_embeddings=True)          # shape (n, 384), float32
q = model.encode("Represent this sentence for searching relevant passages: " + question,
                 normalize_embeddings=True)                                  # bge's query prefix
scores = docs @ q

Prefixes and normalisation

Many retrieval models are trained with an instruction or prefix on one side. bge expects "Represent this sentence for searching relevant passages: " before short queries and nothing before passages; E5 models expect "query: " and "passage: ". Leaving prefixes off, or adding them to the wrong side, quietly lowers retrieval quality; the model card says which to use. Always normalise both sides (or let the model do it) so that inner-product indexes give cosine ranking.

Dimensions and storage

Storage is simply vectors × dimensions × bytes per number. The 4,230 Atlas sections at 384 float32 dimensions take 6.2 MB; a million chunks would take about 1.5 GB, and a 1,024-dimension model nearly three times that. Indexes add overhead (Chapter 7), and quantisation (Chapter 8) can cut it by 4 to 32 times. Larger models are usually more accurate and always slower; the right size is the smallest that meets your recall target on your data (Chapter 5).

Batching on CPU and GPU

Encoding in batches lets the hardware process many texts at once, but every text in a batch is padded to the longest one, and attention cost grows with the square of length. Measured here on an NVIDIA RTX 3050 Ti laptop GPU (4 GB): short sentences (about 30 tokens) went from 62 texts per second one at a time to 141 in batches of 32 on the CPU, and from 71 to 378 on the GPU. Long sections (about 287 tokens, capped at 512) did not speed up with batching at all: 42 per second at batch size 1 against 33 at 32 on the GPU, about three times the CPU's 15. Embedding all 4,230 sections took 154 seconds. Sort texts by length before batching (sentence-transformers does this for you), keep chunks well under the limit, and measure on your own hardware.

Things to try.

  • paraphrase: different words, same meaning, high similarity. This is what embeddings are for.
  • negation: "safe to upgrade" and "not safe to upgrade" score as high as the paraphrases. The vectors encode the topic; the "not" barely moves them.
  • numbers and identifiers: the highest scores of all. A 5-second timeout and a 500-second one, or two different error codes, look nearly identical to the model.
  • search the Atlas: the right chapters come first with no shared keywords needed, and the median section still scores around 0.5, so a fixed "relevance threshold" on raw cosine would be meaningless.

What embeddings miss

Embeddings summarise; details get averaged away. They are weak on negation and contradiction, on numbers, versions, dates and quantities, on identifiers such as error codes, order numbers and SKUs, on rare terms the model never saw, and on long texts beyond their input limit. The remedies come later in the course: keyword search for exact strings (Chapters 11 and 12), a cross-encoder reranker that reads query and passage together (Chapter 13), metadata filters for structured facts such as versions and dates (Chapter 9), and leaving the final judgement of negation and numbers to the generator reading the actual text.

Use case: Sentence-BERT

BERT, published by Google in 2018, was excellent at judging whether two sentences matched, but only by reading both together, which made searching a large collection impractical: comparing one query with ten thousand sentences meant ten thousand full model runs. In 2019 Nils Reimers and Iryna Gurevych at TU Darmstadt published Sentence-BERT, which fine-tuned BERT in a siamese set-up to produce one vector per sentence, so a collection could be embedded once and searched with fast vector similarity. They reported that finding the most similar pair in 10,000 sentences went from about 65 hours with BERT reading pairs to about 5 seconds. Their sentence-transformers library, used throughout this course, grew into the standard way to train and run such models, including the bge family. The trade-off they made is the one this chapter measured: one vector per text is fast but loses detail, which is why reading query and passage together survives as the reranker of Chapter 13.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Mixing models or versions between indexing and querying.
  • Forgetting the query prefix, or adding it to documents too.
  • Using raw cosine as a relevance threshold across queries or models.
  • Trusting embeddings with negation, numbers and identifiers.
  • Embedding texts longer than the model's limit.
  • Huge batches of long texts on a small GPU, which only pads and slows down.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is a text embedding and how is it used for search?

How a strong answer sounds
It is a fixed-length vector produced by a model trained so that texts with similar meaning have nearby vectors. For search, every document chunk is embedded once and stored; at query time the question is embedded with the same model, using its query prefix if it has one, and the nearest chunk vectors by cosine similarity are returned. It finds paraphrases with no words in common. Its weak spots are details: negation, numbers, identifiers and rare terms, which is why it is usually combined with keyword search and a reranker.

Deeper: Users report that searching "does version 3.2 support SSO" returns pages about version 2.1. What is going on, and how do you fix it?

How a strong answer sounds
Embeddings treat version numbers as minor details, so pages about SSO in any version look almost equally similar; the measured pairs that differed only in a version or a number scored above 0.9. I would store the product version as metadata and filter on it, extracting the version from the query by rules or a small model; add keyword retrieval so the exact string "3.2" counts; and use a cross-encoder reranker, which reads the query and passage together and is far more sensitive to such details. Then I would add version-specific questions to the evaluation set to keep it fixed.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • A bi-encoder embeds each text once; queries use the same model and its prefix.
  • Normalise both sides; storage is vectors × dimensions × 4 bytes for float32.
  • Batching helps short texts a lot and long texts little; measure on your hardware.
  • Embeddings find paraphrases and miss negation, numbers and identifiers.
  • Raw cosine scores are relative; unrelated texts still score well above zero.
