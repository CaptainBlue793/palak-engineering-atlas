What RAG Is & When to Use It

A language model knows what was in its training data, up to a cut-off date, and nothing else. It does not know your company's policies, last week's incident report, or the numbers in your own documents. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) fixes that at answer time: find the passages that bear on the question, put them in the prompt, and ask the model to answer from them. It is the most common way to build assistants over private or changing information, and it is often confused with two alternatives, fine-tuning and simply sending more text.

This chapter explains the idea and when to use it, then measures it: a 3-billion-parameter model running locally answers 24 questions closed-book, with the top three passages retrieved from the Atlas, and with three passages retrieved for a different question.

Closed-book answers

Asked a question with no supporting text, a model answers closed-book, from patterns learned in training. That works for common knowledge and fails in three ways for everything else: the information was never in its training data (private documents), it changed after the cut-off (prices, policies, incidents), or it is too specific to have been learned reliably (a number in one report). A model may then say it does not know, or it may hallucinate: produce a fluent, plausible and wrong answer. Small, well-instructed models often refuse; larger ones are more tempted to guess. Either way, the answer is not available without the text.

Retrieve, then generate

RAG adds a step before generation. The question is used to retrieve the most relevant passages from an index of your documents; those passages are placed in the prompt with an instruction to answer only from them; the model generates the answer, ideally citing which passage supports it. The model's training supplies language and reasoning; the retrieved text supplies the facts. Updating knowledge means updating the index, with no retraining, and every answer can point to its evidence.

def answer(question: str, k: int = 3) -> str:
    q = embedder.encode(QUERY_PREFIX + question, normalize_embeddings=True)
    top = np.argsort(-(index @ q))[:k]                                           # retrieve
    sources = "\n\n".join(f"[{i+1}] {chunks[j].title}\n{chunks[j].text}" for i, j in enumerate(top))
    system = "Answer the question using only the sources below. If they do not contain the answer, say you do not know."
    return chat(system, sources + "\n\nQuestion: " + question)                       # generate

RAG, fine-tuning or long context

Fine-tuning adjusts a model's weights on examples. It is good at changing behaviour: a format, a tone, a style of reasoning, a narrow task. It is a poor way to add facts: knowledge is absorbed unreliably, cannot be cited, is expensive to update and is hard to delete. Long context skips retrieval and puts whole documents in the prompt. It works when the relevant material is small and fixed, but costs grow with every token on every request, quality drops when the needed fact is buried in the middle (Chapter 16), and no window holds a large corpus. RAG suits large, changing, private corpora where answers need sources. They combine: fine-tune for format, retrieve for facts, use long context for the few documents that matter most. Chapter 38 compares them with measurements.

What RAG cannot fix

RAG moves the problem rather than removing it. If documents are wrong, out of date or contradictory, answers will be too. If the answer is not in the corpus, RAG can at best say so. If retrieval misses the right passage, the model cannot use it, which is why most of this course is about retrieval. If the question needs reasoning across many documents ("which of our 400 contracts renew next quarter?"), top-k retrieval is the wrong tool; that is a database query (Chapter 18). And models can still misread or overstate the sources they are given (Chapters 17 and 32).

Use case: the paper that named RAG

The term comes from a 2020 paper by Patrick Lewis and colleagues at Facebook AI Research, University College London and New York University, "Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks". They paired a sequence-to-sequence generator with a dense retriever over a Wikipedia index of 21 million passages, and trained the two together. On open-domain question answering the combined model beat larger models that relied on their parameters alone, its answers were more specific and more factual according to human raters, and its knowledge could be updated by swapping the index instead of retraining, which they demonstrated by replacing a 2016 Wikipedia snapshot with a 2018 one. Today's RAG systems rarely train the retriever and generator together, and use general-purpose instruction-following models instead, but the architecture, and the argument for it, are the same.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Fine-tuning to teach facts that change or need citing.
  • Judging the generator before checking that retrieval found the answer.
  • No way for the model to say "I don't know", which invites guessing.
  • Using RAG for aggregate questions that need a database query.
  • Stuffing the whole corpus into a long context because it fits today.
  • Assuming retrieved text is correct and current.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is retrieval-augmented generation?

How a strong answer sounds
It is a way to let a language model answer from information it was not trained on. When a question comes in, a retriever searches an index of your documents, usually with embeddings and often keyword search too, for the most relevant passages; those passages go into the prompt with an instruction to answer only from them and cite them; and the model writes the answer. Knowledge lives in the index, so updating it means re-indexing a document, not retraining, and every answer can show its sources. Its quality is bounded by retrieval: if the right passage is not retrieved, the model cannot use it.

Deeper: A team wants to fine-tune a model on their product documentation so it can answer support questions. What do you recommend?

How a strong answer sounds
RAG first, for the facts. Documentation changes often, answers need to cite the current page, and fine-tuning absorbs facts unreliably, is costly to repeat and cannot be undone for a single page. I would build retrieval over the docs with good chunking and hybrid search, a prompt that answers only from sources and says when they do not cover the question, and an evaluation set of real support questions. Fine-tuning can come later if needed for behaviour, such as the support team's tone or a structured reply format, and can be combined with retrieval. If the docs are small and stable, putting them in a long context is a simpler alternative worth measuring.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • A model alone knows only its training data; RAG supplies facts at answer time.
  • Retrieve, assemble, generate, cite: knowledge lives in the index.
  • Answer quality is bounded by retrieval: 14 of 16 right when the passage was retrieved, none when it was not.
  • Bad sources cost even what the model knew; let it say "I don't know".
  • Fine-tune for behaviour, retrieve for facts, use long context for a few documents.
