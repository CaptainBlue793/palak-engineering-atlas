Query Rewriting & Expansion

Users type short, vague or oddly phrased queries, and retrieval can only match what it is given. One fix is to let a language model work on the query first:

  • rewrite it into a clearer search;
  • generate several variants and merge their results (multi-query);
  • write a hypothetical answer and search with that instead (HyDE).

Each adds a model call before retrieval, so each must earn its latency.

This chapter measures all three with a local 3B model in front of bge-small, on SciFact's scientific claims and on short Atlas glossary terms. It reports confidence intervals, not just averages, because most of the differences turn out to be small.

Why user queries fail

Queries differ from documents in length, vocabulary and form. A user types "virtual env", a question, or a claim, while the answer sits in a paragraph of prose written for a different reader. In a conversation the query may be "what about the second one?", which means nothing without the history (Chapter 30). Embedding models narrow the gap but do not close it, and asymmetric models such as bge even use a special prefix for queries. Query transformation tries to move the query towards the documents before searching.

Rewriting

The simplest transformation asks the model to restate the query as one clear, specific search. It helps most with vague, misspelt or conversational input, and costs one short generation, about 0.2 s here. Its risks are drift, a rewrite that changes the meaning, and removal of the exact terms that keyword search needs. The lab hit both. Asked to rewrite bare Atlas terms for "software-engineering course chapters", the small model padded them with words from that description. "State" became "What are the key states in algorithm design and analysis?". Recall fell sharply. Keep the original query in the pipeline, and keep the rewriting prompt free of words you do not want copied.

rewrite = llm("Rewrite the user's query as one clear, specific search query. Reply with the query only.", query)
variants = llm("Write three different search queries that would find documents answering this, one per line.", query).splitlines()
hyde = llm("Write a two- to three-sentence passage that answers or explains this, in the style of the documents.", query)

v = embed(hyde, as_document=True) + embed(query, as_query=True)             # HyDE + original: average the two vectors
hits = index.search(v / norm(v), k=10)

Multi-query

Multi-query asks for several different phrasings, retrieves for each plus the original, and fuses the lists with reciprocal rank fusion (Chapter 12). The aim is recall: different phrasings reach different documents. The price is more retrievals and a longer generation. In the lab, three variants made little difference on either dataset. The variants a small model writes tend to be close to each other and to the original.

HyDE

Hypothetical document embeddings turn the problem around. Instead of embedding the question, ask the model to write a short passage that would answer it, and embed that passage as if it were a document. Even if its facts are wrong, a generated passage looks like the documents in vocabulary and form, so it lands nearer them in embedding space. Averaging the HyDE vector with the original query's vector keeps the query's intent as an anchor. On both datasets that combination was the only method whose 95% interval cleared zero.

Simulator: three transformations, two datasets

For every query, qwen2.5:3b (temperature 0) wrote a rewrite, three variants and a short passage. Things to try.

  • SciFact, HyDE alone: a smaller average gain whose interval includes zero. Without the original as an anchor, a wrong guess sometimes pulls retrieval off course.
  • Atlas glossary, every method: a plain rewrite was harmful, recall@5 falling from 0.763 to 0.607 (62 terms worse, 15 better). The model padded bare terms with words from its own instructions ("algorithms", "software engineering"), guessed the wrong field, and once refused. HyDE + original rose to 0.797, interval +0.003 to +0.063.
  • Multi-query on both datasets: three variants plus the original changed little. The variants a small model writes stay close to the original.
  • Seconds: from 0.2 s for a rewrite to 0.9 s for a HyDE passage, before any retrieval.

Costs and risks

Every transformation adds a model call to the critical path of each query: time, money with a hosted model, and one more component that can fail or change behaviour when the model is updated. Rewrites can drift from the user's meaning. HyDE passages are confident guesses, so they must never reach the user as answers. Multi-query multiplies retrieval load. Apply them selectively:

  • to short or conversational queries, detected by length or a classifier;
  • when the first retrieval scores poorly;
  • offline, by expanding documents instead of queries (Chapter 25).

Measure each on your own queries with intervals, as here, because averages of a few hundred queries move by a point or two from noise alone.

Use case: HyDE and zero-shot dense retrieval

In December 2022 Luyu Gao, Xueguang Ma, Jimmy Lin and Jamie Callan published "Precise Zero-Shot Dense Retrieval without Relevance Labels", which introduced HyDE (ACL 2023). Their problem was that dense retrievers need labelled query–document pairs to train, and these do not exist for most new domains. Their trick was to let an instruction-following model (InstructGPT) write a fake document for each query, and to embed that with an unsupervised encoder (Contriever). The encoder's "dense bottleneck" keeps what the passage is about and drops the invented details. Without any relevance labels, HyDE beat the unsupervised encoder alone by large margins on web search and several BEIR tasks, including SciFact, and was competitive with fine-tuned retrievers. The lab's gains are much smaller because bge-small was already trained on large amounts of query–passage data. That is the general lesson: query transformation helps most where the retriever is weakest.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Replacing the original query instead of adding to it.
  • Showing HyDE passages to users.
  • Judging by averages without intervals.
  • Transforming every query.
  • Forgetting the query prefix or using it on HyDE passages.
  • No timeout or fallback when the rewriting model is slow or down.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is HyDE, and why can it improve retrieval?

How a strong answer sounds
Hypothetical document embeddings: before searching, ask a language model to write a short passage that would answer the question, then embed that passage and search with it. Queries and documents look different (short versus long, question versus statement), and a generated passage looks like the documents, so its vector lands closer to the relevant ones even if some of its details are wrong. It is safest combined with the original query's vector, because it costs a model call per query and can mislead when the guess is off. The passage is only for retrieval, never shown to the user.

Deeper: Your team wants to add LLM query rewriting to every search. How do you decide?

How a strong answer sounds
Measure it first: on a labelled set of real queries, compare retrieval with and without each transformation, paired per query, with bootstrap intervals, because average changes of a point or two are often noise. Look at which queries gain and lose. Then weigh the cost: added latency on every query, model spend, a new failure point and drift risk. Usually the answer is selective: transform only short, vague or conversational queries, keep the original in the retrieval (fused or averaged), cap the latency with a timeout and fall back to the original. Re-check whenever the model or the index changes.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Query transformation moves the query towards the documents before retrieval.
  • Rewriting clarifies; multi-query widens recall; HyDE searches with a generated answer.
  • Here, HyDE averaged with the original was the only method to clear noise, on both datasets; a plain rewrite hurt the Atlas terms badly.
  • Every method adds a model call per query; apply them selectively.
  • Keep the original query in the loop and judge with paired intervals.
