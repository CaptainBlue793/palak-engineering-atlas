Measuring Search: Precision, Recall & Ranking

Every decision in this course, which chunk size, which embedding model, whether a reranker is worth its latency, is settled by measuring retrieval on questions whose right answers are known. That needs a handful of metrics, each answering a different question: of what came back, how much was relevant; of what was relevant, how much came back; how high was the first good result; and how good was the whole ordering. They often disagree, and choosing the wrong one can make a worse system look better.

This chapter defines precision, recall, recall@k, MRR and nDCG and computes them for real: two keyword rankers on SciFact, a public benchmark of 5,183 scientific abstracts and 300 claims, each labelled with the abstracts that support or refute it. You can step through individual queries and watch each metric react.

Relevance judgements

A metric compares a ranked list with a set of relevance judgements: for each test query, which documents are relevant (binary labels) or how relevant they are (graded labels, such as 0 to 3). They come from people reading results, from logs (clicks and dwell time, which are biased towards what was already shown), or from the structure of the data, as in P4 and P7, where each glossary term's defining chapter served as the answer. Labels are never complete: a good document nobody labelled counts as a miss, which lowers every system's score and can favour systems similar to the one that produced the labels. Treat absolute numbers with care and compare systems on the same labels.

Precision and recall

Precision is the fraction of returned results that are relevant; recall is the fraction of relevant documents that were returned. Search systems return ranked lists, so both are measured at a cut-off k: precision@k and recall@k. For RAG, recall@k usually matters most, because the generator only sees the top k chunks: if the evidence is not among them, nothing later can recover it. Precision@k has a trap: when a query has only one relevant document, precision@10 can never exceed 0.1, so it says more about the labels than the system.

def precision_recall_at_k(ranked: list[str], relevant: set[str], k: int) -> tuple[float, float]:
    hits = sum(1 for d in ranked[:k] if d in relevant)
    return hits / k, hits / len(relevant)

MRR and nDCG

Reciprocal rank is 1 divided by the position of the first relevant result (0 if none in the top k), and MRR is its mean over queries. It suits tasks where one good answer is enough. nDCG (normalised discounted cumulative gain) scores the whole list: each relevant result contributes its gain divided by log₂(position + 1), so results further down count less, and the total is divided by the best possible score for that query, giving 0 to 1. With graded labels the gain is the grade, so a highly relevant document near the top is worth more than a marginal one. It is the standard headline metric of benchmarks such as BEIR.

import math
def ndcg_at_k(ranked, relevant, k):
    dcg = sum(1 / math.log2(i + 2) for i, d in enumerate(ranked[:k]) if d in relevant)
    ideal = sum(1 / math.log2(i + 2) for i in range(min(len(relevant), k)))
    return dcg / ideal

Things to try.

  • Precision@10 is tiny for both (0.083 for TF-IDF), because most claims have exactly one relevant abstract; perfect retrieval could score at most 0.1 on those. Recall@10 (0.754) says much more.
  • Move k from 10 to 1 on a claim with several relevant abstracts: recall drops sharply while precision rises. That trade is why the k you will actually pass to the generator is the k to measure.

Use case: TREC and the Cranfield method

The way search systems are compared today goes back to experiments led by Cyril Cleverdon at the Cranfield College of Aeronautics in the 1960s: a fixed collection of documents, a fixed set of queries, and human judgements of which documents are relevant to each query, so that different indexing methods could be scored on equal terms. In 1992 the US National Institute of Standards and Technology started TREC, the Text REtrieval Conference, which scaled the method up: many research groups submit runs on shared tasks, NIST assessors judge a pool of the top results from all of them, and everyone is scored with the same metrics. Pooling made judging large collections affordable, at the cost of incomplete labels, the problem this chapter warns about. BEIR, from which SciFact comes, applies the same recipe to compare modern neural retrievers across many domains with nDCG@10, and every claim of "better retrieval" in this course is a small Cranfield experiment.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Reporting precision@10 when most queries have one relevant document.
  • Measuring at a different k from the one the generator receives.
  • Comparing numbers across different label sets or corpora.
  • Only looking at averages, never at the queries that got worse.
  • Treating unlabelled results as definitely irrelevant.
  • Evaluating on the questions you tuned on.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Which retrieval metric would you report for a RAG system, and why?

How a strong answer sounds
Recall@k at the k actually passed to the model, because the generator can only use evidence that is in those chunks; if the right passage is not retrieved, no prompt can fix it. I would add nDCG@k to capture ordering, since models use early context more reliably and rerankers mainly improve order, and MRR or hit@k if one good passage is usually enough. I would avoid precision@k as a headline when queries have few relevant documents, because its ceiling is set by the labels. And I would always compare systems on the same labelled questions and look at per-query wins and losses, not just the average.

Deeper: A new retriever improves average nDCG@10 by 0.02. Do you ship it?

How a strong answer sounds
Not on that number alone. First, is 0.02 bigger than the noise? I would bootstrap over queries or use a paired test, since per-query variation is large. Second, where does it come from: count queries that got better and worse, and read the worst regressions, especially for important query types. Third, check whether the labels are biased towards the old system, for example if they came from its results, which penalises a new system that finds unlabelled good documents; judge a sample of the new system's top results to check. Finally weigh latency and cost, and confirm with an end-to-end check that answer quality improved, since retrieval metrics are a proxy.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Metrics compare a ranked list with relevance judgements, which are always incomplete.
  • Precision asks how clean the results are; recall asks how complete; for RAG, recall@k matters most.
  • MRR rewards the first good result; nDCG rewards the whole ordering.
  • Precision@k is capped when queries have few relevant documents.
  • Compare systems on the same labels, at the k you use, per query as well as on average.
