Building an Evaluation Set

Chapter 19 assumed a golden set existed. Building one is the slow part, so many teams ask a model to write the questions: give it each chunk and ask what question that chunk answers. It is fast, it covers every chunk, and the labels come free, since the source chunk is the answer. It also produces questions unlike the ones real users ask, and an evaluation set that flatters the system and blurs the differences between options.

The lab writes two synthetic sets for 300 random Atlas chunks with a 3B model: plain questions, and short search-box queries with copying forbidden. It scores BM25, dense and hybrid retrieval on both, and compares them with the Atlas's real glossary questions.

  • Collect real questions and turn them into a labelled set
  • Generate synthetic questions, and know how they mislead
  • Plan coverage and difficulty deliberately
  • Label relevance efficiently and consistently
  • Keep the set honest as the corpus and users change

Real user questions

The best evaluation questions are the ones people actually ask: search logs, support tickets, chat transcripts, questions sent to experts. They carry real vocabulary, real vagueness and real distribution, with many short queries and some long. Before launch, ask the intended users to write questions for a week, or mine the questions people already send to colleagues. Remove personal data, deduplicate near-copies, and keep the rare but important ones even if they are few.

Synthetic questions

Generating questions from chunks gives instant scale. The lab shows its two biases. The questions borrow the chunk's own words: "What is Jepsen, and how does it find bugs in distributed databases?" was written from the Jepsen chunk. So they are easy, and retrieval scores on them were far higher than on real questions: 95.0% against 85.3% for the hybrid. They also narrowed the differences between methods. BM25 beat dense retrieval by 12.0 points on real questions, but by only 4.3 on plain synthetic ones and 3.0 on search-box ones. A team comparing retrievers on the synthetic set would underestimate the difference that mattered.

Things to try.

  • Plain synthetic vs real: every retriever scores higher on the synthetic set. The questions were written from the answer.
  • The BM25-over-dense gap: 12.0 points on real questions, 4.3 on plain synthetic ones.
  • Search-box queries: shorter (3 words on average against 17) and closer to real queries in form, but the model still copied. One query was a shell command lifted from the chunk.
  • The ranking of retrievers: hybrid ≥ BM25 > dense on all three sets. Synthetic sets got the order right and the sizes wrong.

Coverage and difficulty

Decide what the set must cover, then check it does:

  • every major topic and document type;
  • short keyword queries and long questions;
  • exact identifiers;
  • multi-part and multi-hop questions;
  • questions the corpus cannot answer, for testing abstention (Chapter 32);
  • questions that need recent documents.

Tag each question with its kind so results can be broken down, because an average over an unbalanced mix hides the weak spots. Keep a hard subset of questions the current system fails, and track it separately.

Labelling

Label by pooling (Chapter 19): run every candidate system, collect the union of their top results, and have reviewers mark each as relevant, partly relevant or irrelevant, with a short written guideline and examples. Double-label a sample to measure agreement. Low agreement means the guideline or the questions are ambiguous, not that the reviewers are careless. A model can pre-label to speed this up, but a person should confirm, especially on the hard cases a model is worst at (Chapter 20).

Keeping it current

Corpora change. Documents get edited, split, deleted and added, and labels pointing at old chunk ids go stale. Store labels against stable document ids and passages rather than positional chunk ids. Re-validate a sample after each re-index, and retire questions whose answers no longer exist. Add new questions from recent traffic every few weeks, so the set follows what users ask now. Version the set, and only compare systems measured on the same version.

Use case: MS MARCO's real queries and their synthetic successors

Microsoft built MS MARCO (2016) from real, anonymised Bing queries, with passages that human assessors marked as answering them. That realism is why models trained and tested on it transferred to real search. Later work went the other way. InPars (2022, Luiz Bonifacio and colleagues) and Promptagator (2022, Zhuyun Dai and colleagues at Google) generated synthetic queries for documents with large language models, to train retrievers for domains without labelled data. Both found synthetic data useful for training. They also found it needed filtering, keeping only questions that a retriever or reranker could actually connect back to their source. Training on synthetic questions and evaluating on them are different things: the lab shows how a synthetic test set can overstate quality and understate differences.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • An evaluation set made only of synthetic questions.
  • Questions written by the people who built the index.
  • No unanswerable questions.
  • Labels on positional chunk ids.
  • One overall number with no breakdown by kind.
  • Never refreshing.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How would you build an evaluation set for a new RAG system?

How a strong answer sounds
Start from real questions, from logs, tickets or a week of collection with target users, anonymised and deduplicated. Tag each by kind (keyword, natural question, multi-part, unanswerable) and check coverage across topics. Label relevance by pooling results from several retrievers and having reviewers judge them with a written guideline, double-labelling a sample to measure agreement. Use model-generated questions only to fill gaps, marked as synthetic, because they borrow the source's words and make retrieval look better than it is. Version the set and refresh it as the corpus and users change.

Deeper: Your team evaluated three retrievers on 2,000 synthetic questions and found them within two points of each other. Would you pick the cheapest?

How a strong answer sounds
Not on that evidence. Synthetic questions are written from the target chunk, share its vocabulary and inflate scores, which compresses differences. In testing, BM25's lead over dense retrieval was about 12 points on real questions but 3–4 on synthetic ones. I would assemble a few hundred real questions, label them by pooling, and re-run with paired significance tests and a breakdown by question kind. Synthetic sets did rank the retrievers in the right order there, so they are a useful smoke test, but sizes and close calls need real questions.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Real questions first; synthetic only to fill gaps, and always marked.
  • Synthetic questions borrow the source's words, inflating scores and shrinking differences.
  • Plan coverage by kind and difficulty, including unanswerable questions.
  • Label by pooling with guidelines and measured agreement.
  • Store labels on stable ids, version the set and refresh it.
