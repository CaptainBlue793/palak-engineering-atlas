Approximate Search: HNSW

When exact search becomes too slow (Chapter 6), the standard answer is to give up a little accuracy for a lot of speed: approximate nearest neighbour (ANN) search returns most of the true nearest neighbours while looking at only a small fraction of the vectors. HNSW, hierarchical navigable small world graphs, is the most widely used ANN index in vector databases and search engines, because it is fast, accurate and supports adding vectors without rebuilding.

This chapter explains how HNSW finds neighbours by walking a graph, what its three parameters do, and how to measure the recall you give up. The lab builds 30 HNSW configurations over 83,118 real sentence embeddings from the Atlas and compares each with exact search.

Navigable graphs

Connect every vector to a few of its near neighbours, and you have a proximity graph. To search it, start at some entry node, look at its neighbours, move to whichever is closest to the query, and repeat until no neighbour is closer: a greedy walk that homes in on the query's region while touching only the nodes along the way. Plain greedy walks can get stuck in a local optimum or take many short steps, so HNSW keeps a list of the best ef candidates found so far rather than just one, and explores from all of them; a larger ef explores more and misses less.

Layers

HNSW adds layers, borrowed from skip lists. Every vector is in the bottom layer; a random, exponentially decreasing fraction also appears in the layers above, so the top layer has only a handful of nodes with long-range links. A search starts at the top, takes a few big jumps to get near the query, drops a layer, refines, and repeats down to the bottom layer, where it does its careful ef-wide search. The result is search time that grows roughly with the logarithm of the number of vectors rather than linearly.

import hnswlib
index = hnswlib.Index(space="ip", dim=384)                     # inner product on normalised vectors = cosine
index.init_index(max_elements=n, M=16, ef_construction=200)        # graph degree and build-time effort
index.add_items(vectors, ids)
index.set_ef(100)                                                 # query-time effort: the recall dial
labels, distances = index.knn_query(query_vectors, k=10)

M and ef_construction

M is the number of links each node keeps (twice that on the bottom layer). More links make the graph better connected, so searches find neighbours more reliably, at the cost of memory, slower insertion and slightly slower queries. Values from 12 to 48 are common; 16 is a good default for embeddings of a few hundred dimensions. ef_construction is how widely the builder searches when choosing each new node's links: higher values build a better graph more slowly. Both are fixed when the index is built; changing them means rebuilding.

ef_search

ef_search (often just ef) is how many candidates a query keeps while walking the bottom layer, and it must be at least k. It is the dial you tune in production, without rebuilding: raise it until recall@k against exact search meets your target, then stop, because each step costs latency. Measure on queries like your real ones, since recall varies with how clustered the data is.

Simulator: 30 configurations on real embeddings

83,118 sentences from the Atlas were embedded with bge-small and indexed with hnswlib for every combination of M (8, 16, 32), ef_construction (100, 200) and ef_search (10 to 200). One thousand queries (700 glossary terms with bge's prefix and 300 SciFact claims) were searched for their top 10, and recall@10 is the share of the exact top 10 that HNSW returned. Exact search, batched and single-threaded, is the reference.

Things to try.

  • M 16, ef_construction 200, ef_search 100: about 95% recall@10 at about 0.35 ms, roughly fifteen times faster than exact search.
  • ef_search 10 → 200 at any M: recall climbs steeply at first and then flattens, while latency keeps rising, roughly doubling with each doubling of ef_search. The last few percent are the expensive ones.
  • M 8 vs 32: at the same ef_search, more links mean higher recall and somewhat slower queries; M 32 reached 0.989 recall at ef_search 200. On the chart, plotted against latency, the M 16 and M 32 curves almost coincide: for the same time budget the extra links bought little here, which is one reason 16 is a common default.
  • ef_construction 100 vs 200: a better-built graph gains a point or two of recall at the same query cost, for roughly twice the build time.

Recall versus speed

Every ANN index trades recall for speed, and the right point depends on the rest of the pipeline. If a reranker (Chapter 13) re-scores the top 50, recall@50 matters and small misses at the bottom are harmless; if the top 3 go straight to the model, recall@3 matters more. HNSW's other costs are memory, since the whole graph plus vectors must sit in RAM (134 MB here against 122 MB of vectors), slower inserts than a flat index, and deletes that are usually only marked and cleaned up by rebuilding. Use case: from a 2016 paper to every vector database

Yury Malkov and Dmitry Yashunin published "Efficient and robust approximate nearest neighbor search using Hierarchical Navigable Small World graphs" in 2016, building on Malkov's earlier navigable small world work. The hierarchy fixed the main weakness of flat proximity graphs, slow progress across a large dataset, and on the public ANN benchmarks HNSW became one of the fastest methods at high recall. It was simple to implement and supported incremental inserts, and the reference implementation, hnswlib, was small and easy to embed. Within a few years HNSW was the default or main index in faiss's graph indexes, Lucene (and so Elasticsearch, OpenSearch and Solr), pgvector for PostgreSQL, and most dedicated vector databases. Its known costs, memory for the graph and awkward deletes, are what quantisation and disk-based indexes such as DiskANN later went after.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Never measuring recall against exact search.
  • Leaving ef_search at a library default such as 10.
  • Setting ef_search below k.
  • Using HNSW for a corpus where exact search is fast enough.
  • Forgetting the graph's memory on top of the vectors.
  • Using L2 space on unnormalised vectors when the model expects cosine.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How does HNSW find nearest neighbours quickly?

How a strong answer sounds
It builds a graph in which each vector is linked to some of its nearest neighbours, arranged in layers like a skip list: every vector is in the bottom layer, and progressively fewer appear in higher layers with longer-range links. A query starts at the top, greedily moves to whichever neighbour is closest to the query, drops down a layer when it can get no closer, and at the bottom layer explores a candidate list of size ef_search. It looks at only a tiny fraction of the vectors, so it is much faster than exact search, at the cost of sometimes missing a true neighbour. M controls links per node and memory; ef_construction controls build quality; ef_search trades recall for latency at query time.

Deeper: Your HNSW index returns slightly worse results than the prototype that used exact search. How do you diagnose and fix it?

How a strong answer sounds
Measure recall@k of the HNSW index against exact search on a sample of real queries, at the k the pipeline uses. If recall is low, raise ef_search first, since it needs no rebuild, and watch latency; if recall plateaus too low, rebuild with a higher M or ef_construction. Check the metric matches the embedding model (inner product on normalised vectors for cosine), and that filters are not shrinking the candidate set (Chapter 9). Then confirm the end-to-end effect on retrieval metrics, because a recall@10 of 0.95 against exact may change answers very little if a reranker follows.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • HNSW walks a layered proximity graph, touching a tiny fraction of the vectors.
  • M and ef_construction shape the graph at build time; ef_search is the query-time recall dial.
  • Recall rises steeply then flattens with ef_search, while latency keeps climbing.
  • Always measure recall@k against exact search, at the k your pipeline uses.
  • The graph costs memory on top of the vectors.
