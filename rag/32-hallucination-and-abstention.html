Hallucination & Knowing When Not to Answer

RAG reduces hallucination; it does not remove it. When retrieval misses, the model fills the gap from memory. When the context is close but not quite right, it bends the context to fit the question. And it rarely says "I don't know" unless asked to. A trustworthy system needs to recognise insufficient context, and to decline, ask or escalate instead of guessing.

The lab asks a 3B model 150 questions twice: once with the source that answers them among ten, and once without it. It compares a lenient prompt with one that allows "I don't know". It then tests two confidence signals as gates: the best retrieval similarity, and the model's own probability that the sources contain the answer. The result is the trade-off curve between answering more and making things up less.

  • Distinguish kinds of hallucination in RAG
  • Detect insufficient context before generating
  • Design abstention: decline, ask, or hand over
  • Evaluate confidence signals as gates
  • Choose an operating point on the coverage–accuracy curve

Kinds of hallucination

In RAG, hallucinations come in a few kinds:

  • Ungrounded answers, from the model's memory when retrieval found nothing useful.
  • Unfaithful answers, which contradict or go beyond the retrieved context (Chapter 20).
  • Mis-attributed answers, which are true but cite the wrong source (Chapter 17).
  • Confabulated specifics: numbers, names and dates that sound right.

The first is the most common in practice. It happens whenever the question falls outside the corpus, and the model gives no sign that anything is wrong.

Insufficient context

The model cannot know the corpus lacks an answer unless the prompt lets it say so, and even then it often does not. With a lenient prompt ("answer using the sources, citing them"), it answered 109 of 150 unanswerable questions. Only 41 times did it say it did not know. Adding an explicit "If the sources do not contain the answer, reply exactly: I don't know" cut that to 23. But the same instruction made it decline 31 answerable questions, and its right citations fell from 47 to 30.

Abstention

Abstaining is a product decision, not just a prompt. Choose among:

  • saying plainly that the sources do not cover it;
  • showing the closest passages without a generated answer;
  • asking a clarifying question;
  • handing over to a person or a ticket.

Make each one visible and logged, because abstentions point to gaps in the corpus (Chapter 36). Decide by the cost of a wrong answer: a legal or medical assistant should abstain far more readily than a recipe search.

ok = p_yes(judge, sources + question + "Do the sources contain the answer to this question? Answer Yes or No.")
if ok < THRESHOLD:                                   # tuned on labelled answerable and unanswerable questions
    return abstain(question, top_passages)            # say so, show passages, or hand over
return generate(sources, question)

Confidence signals

A gate needs a score that separates answerable from unanswerable questions. Retrieval similarity is free but weak: here the best source's cosine similarity told them apart with AUC 0.63, barely better than chance, because a near-miss passage scores almost as high as the right one. Asking the model directly whether the sources contain the answer, and reading the probability of "Yes", did much better: AUC 0.85. It costs one more short call. Combinations and trained classifiers can do better still. Always measure on your own answerable and unanswerable questions.

Simulator: answering less to hallucinate less

Each of 150 glossary questions was asked with ten sources: once including the section that defines the term (answerable), once with it replaced by the next nearest section (unanswerable). Answers used the lenient prompt. A "supported" answer cites the defining source. An "unsupported" answer is any answer to an unanswerable question, or an answer to an answerable one that does not cite its source; some of the latter may be right from memory. The gate answers only when its signal clears a threshold.

Things to try.

  • Model signal, all → about 60%: unsupported answers fall from 211 to 132, while supported ones fall only from 47 to 44.
  • Retrieval similarity at the same coverage: far more supported answers lost for the same reduction. Similarity cannot tell a near miss from a hit.
  • About 10%: very few unsupported answers, and very few answers at all. Every operating point is a trade.

Measuring the trade-off

Plot supported answers kept against unsupported answers served as the threshold moves, on a labelled mix of answerable and unanswerable questions in realistic proportions. Then pick the point by cost: how bad is a wrong answer compared with an unnecessary "I don't know"? Track the answer rate in production as well. A falling rate can mean the corpus no longer covers what users ask, and a rising one after a model change can mean the gate stopped working.

Use case: selective prediction

Machine learning has studied "knowing when to abstain" for decades under the name selective classification: a model with a reject option trades coverage for accuracy, and the risk–coverage curve shows the trade, as in Yonatan Geifman and Ran El-Yaniv's work in 2017. For question answering, the same idea appears as "answerability". SQuAD 2.0 (Pranav Rajpurkar, Robin Jia and Percy Liang, 2018) added over 50,000 unanswerable questions written to look answerable, and models that had looked strong on SQuAD 1.1 scored much worse once they had to recognise when the passage did not contain the answer. RAG systems face the same test every day with real users, and the same tool applies: a calibrated signal, a threshold and a measured curve.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • No way to say "I don't know".
  • Testing only answerable questions.
  • Gating on retrieval similarity alone.
  • One threshold for every product.
  • Silent abstention.
  • Not tracking the answer rate.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How do you stop a RAG assistant from making things up when it does not know?

How a strong answer sounds
Give the model an explicit path to decline, and enforce it with a gate before or after generation: score whether the retrieved context contains the answer, for example by asking a model for the probability it does, and abstain below a threshold chosen on labelled answerable and unanswerable questions. Abstain visibly: say the sources do not cover it, show the closest passages, ask a clarifying question or hand over. Measure both sides, wrong answers served and right answers lost, and log abstentions as corpus gaps.

Deeper: Your team proposes abstaining whenever the top retrieval score is below 0.7. What would you check?

How a strong answer sounds
Whether retrieval similarity actually separates answerable from unanswerable questions on our data. Near-miss passages often score almost as high as the right one; in testing, top similarity separated them with AUC only about 0.63, against about 0.85 for asking the model whether the context contains the answer. I would build a labelled mix of both kinds, plot supported answers kept against unsupported answers served for each signal across thresholds, and choose by the cost of each error. I would also check that the threshold survives model and index changes, since raw similarity scales shift.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Without an "I don't know" path, the model answered most unanswerable questions.
  • The instruction cut those sharply, but also declined answerable ones.
  • Retrieval similarity is a weak gate; asking the model about sufficiency was much stronger.
  • Choose the threshold from a measured curve and the cost of each kind of error.
  • Abstain visibly and log abstentions as gaps.
