Privacy, Retention & Deletion

Corpora contain people: names in tickets, email addresses in meeting notes, phone numbers in on-call rotas. A RAG system copies that text into several stores (chunk store, vector index, caches, logs, evaluation sets, backups), makes it searchable by meaning, and can quote it to anyone who asks the right question. When a person asks to be forgotten, or a retention period ends, deletion has to reach every copy. A deleted vector, it turns out, is not always gone.

This chapter measures three things on the lab's machine:

  • what "delete" does to an HNSW index and its saved file;
  • how much a leftover embedding reveals about its text;
  • how easily a person's record is found by name, and what simple redaction leaves behind.
  • Find personal data in a corpus before indexing it
  • List every place a RAG system copies text
  • Delete in a way that actually removes data, and prove it
  • Explain what embeddings can reveal

Personal data in corpora

Before indexing, find out what personal data the sources contain and whether the RAG use is allowed for it. Detection combines several layers:

  • Patterns for structured identifiers: emails, phone numbers, card and account numbers.
  • Named-entity recognition for names and addresses.
  • Source-level rules: HR folders are not indexed for everyone.

In the lab, 200 synthetic personal records were mixed into the Atlas chunks: support tickets, meeting notes, an on-call rota. Searching for a person by name returned their record in the top 5 87% of the time. A regex redactor removed every email and phone number, and every name was still there. Pattern matching alone is not redaction.

EMAIL = re.compile(r"[\w.+-]+@[\w-]+\.[\w.]+")
PHONE = re.compile(r"\+?\d[\d ()-]{7,}\d")
text = EMAIL.sub("[email]", PHONE.sub("[phone]", text))       # catches structured identifiers only
# names, addresses and free-text descriptions need an NER model, and still a human-reviewed sample

Copies everywhere

A single source paragraph typically ends up in many places:

  • the extracted-text store;
  • chunks and their metadata;
  • vectors in the index;
  • the keyword index;
  • parent-section and summary stores (Chapter 24);
  • answer and retrieval caches (Chapter 28);
  • request logs and traces with retrieved context (Chapter 36);
  • evaluation sets (Chapter 21);
  • fine-tuning data (Chapter 33);
  • backups of all of the above.

Keep a data map of these stores, each keyed by source document id. Deleting a document must be a single operation that fans out to every store and reports what it removed.

Deletion that works

Vector indexes are built for search, not erasure. In the lab, 1,000 of the Atlas's 83,118 sentence vectors were marked deleted in an HNSW index, which then never returned them in search (0% came back). But the saved index file still contained the bytes of 100% of them, and unmarking restored 100% of them exactly, to anyone holding the file. Marking hides data from queries; it does not remove it. Real deletion means:

  • rebuilding or compacting the index;
  • overwriting or expiring old files and snapshots;
  • purging caches and logs;
  • checking afterwards, with a search for the deleted text and a scan of the stores for its id.

Embedding inversion

Embeddings are not anonymised data. With access to the original texts, an embedding identifies its text by lookup, as the lab showed. Without them, research has shown that text can be reconstructed from embeddings alone, closely and sometimes exactly (see the use case). Treat stored vectors as the personal data they were computed from. Apply the same access control, deletion and retention as to the text, and do not send embeddings of sensitive text to third parties on the assumption that they are opaque.

Retention policies

Set a retention period for every store, tied to its purpose:

  • index content lives as long as its source document;
  • answer caches for hours or days;
  • request logs with retrieved text for as short a time as debugging allows, then without the text;
  • evaluation sets reviewed for personal data before they are kept.

Backups need expiry too, or deletion never completes. Automate retention and record what was deleted when, since regulations such as the GDPR's right to erasure expect you to show it.

Use case: "Text Embeddings Reveal (Almost) As Much As Text"

In 2023 John Morris and colleagues at Cornell published Vec2Text at EMNLP. They trained a model to reconstruct text from its embedding, guessing a text, embedding the guess, comparing it with the target vector and correcting it over several rounds. For short inputs embedded with a popular open model, they recovered most texts exactly, and they recovered full names from embeddings of clinical notes. Their conclusion was that embeddings should be protected with the same care as the text they come from. The lab's simpler measurement points the same way: anyone with an embedding and a plausible pool of candidate texts can tell which text it was.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Treating "marked deleted" as deleted.
  • Forgetting caches, logs and evaluation sets.
  • Regex-only redaction.
  • Treating embeddings as anonymous.
  • Backups with no expiry.
  • Indexing first, asking later.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: A customer asks you to delete all their data from your RAG system. What do you do?

How a strong answer sounds
Use a data map of every store that holds copies — extracted text, chunks, vector and keyword indexes, parent and summary stores, caches, logs and traces, evaluation and training sets, backups — all keyed by source document id. Trigger one deletion job that removes the customer's documents from each, rebuilds or compacts the vector index because marked deletes still leave the vectors on disk, purges caches and logs, and lets backups expire on a documented schedule. Then verify by searching for their content and scanning stores for their ids, and record what was removed and when.

Deeper: A colleague argues embeddings are safe to share with a vendor because they are just numbers. How do you respond?

How a strong answer sounds
Embeddings encode meaning precisely enough to be matched back to their text: anyone with a candidate pool can identify the exact text by nearest-neighbour lookup, and research such as Vec2Text reconstructed short texts, including names, from embeddings alone. So they are personal data whenever their source text is. Treat them with the same contracts, access control, retention and deletion as the text. If sharing is necessary, share embeddings of redacted text, minimise what is sent, and get legal review.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Corpora contain people; detect personal data before indexing, with more than regex.
  • A RAG system copies text into many stores; deletion must reach all of them.
  • Marked deletes hide vectors from search but leave them on disk: rebuild and verify.
  • Embeddings reveal their text; protect them like the text.
  • Every store needs a retention period, backups included.
