Conversations & Follow-Up Questions

People do not ask one question; they have a conversation. The second message is often "can you give me an example of it?", which means nothing to a retriever: "it" matches every document equally badly. Conversational RAG has to turn each follow-up into something searchable, decide whether a turn needs retrieval at all, and carry the right amount of history without drowning the prompt.

The lab builds 300 two-turn conversations from Atlas glossary terms: a first question about a term, its answer, then a follow-up that says "it" or "that". It retrieves for the follow-up three ways, and then asks the model whether 300 follow-ups, half of them "thanks" or formatting requests, need a new search at all.

  • Explain why follow-up questions retrieve badly
  • Condense history into a standalone query
  • Decide when a turn needs retrieval
  • Manage conversation memory within a token budget
  • Evaluate multi-turn conversations, not just single questions

Follow-ups that fail

A follow-up leans on the conversation: pronouns ("it", "that one"), ellipsis ("and in Postgres?"), and references to earlier answers ("the second option"). Embedded on its own, it carries almost no topic. In the lab, follow-ups alone found the defining chapter in the top 5 1% of the time. That is essentially never, because "Why does that matter in practice?" is equally close to every chapter.

Condensing history

Two fixes. The simple one prepends the previous question to the follow-up before retrieval: 67% here. The usual one asks a model to condense the conversation into a standalone query, replacing pronouns with what they refer to: 74% here, with the term named outright in 84% of rewrites. Condensation costs a short model call (0.28 s here) and carries the drift risks of Chapter 14. Keep the original follow-up for display and logs, and search with the condensed form. Hybrid retrieval of both is also an option.

SYS = ("Rewrite the user's last message as one standalone search query that makes sense without the conversation. "
       "Replace pronouns like \"it\" or \"that\" with what they refer to. Reply with the query only.")
history = render(last_turns(conversation, n=3))                       # user and assistant turns, trimmed
query = llm(SYS, history + "\nUser: " + message)                    # "What problems can a ballot number cause in Paxos?"
hits = retrieve(query)

When to retrieve

Not every turn needs a search. "Thanks", "make that shorter" and "as a bullet list" can be answered from the conversation, and searching for them wastes time and can drag in irrelevant text. A classifier can decide: rules for common patterns, or a model judgement. In the lab the 3B model, asked to reply SEARCH or NO, said SEARCH for all 150 chit-chat and formatting messages. Simple rules for thanks and formatting requests would have done better. Measure any such gate both ways: skipping a search that was needed is worse than one wasted search.

Conversation memory

Long conversations exceed any context budget. Keep the last few turns verbatim, summarise older ones into a running note, and store facts the user stated in a structured memory (preferences, their product, their region). Retrieval results from earlier turns rarely need to be re-sent; re-retrieve for the current turn instead. Memory is also personal data (Chapter 39): give it a retention period, and let users see and clear it.

Evaluating dialogues

Single-question evaluation misses conversational failures. Build multi-turn test cases from real sessions: a first question, then follow-ups of each kind (pronoun, ellipsis, correction, topic switch, thanks), each labelled with what the answer should draw on. Score retrieval and answers per turn, and track the turns where the system lost the thread. Topic switches are a classic trap: condensation that wrongly carries the old topic into a new question.

Use case: TREC CAsT and query rewriting for conversational search

From 2019 to 2022, TREC ran the Conversational Assistance Track (CAsT). Systems answered information-seeking conversations whose later questions depended on earlier ones, such as "What about its side effects?". The track's findings shaped today's practice. Rewriting each turn into a self-contained query, at first manually as an upper bound and then with trained models such as T5-based rewriters, closed much of the gap between conversational and one-shot search. The remaining errors clustered around topic shifts and long-range references. The "condense question" step in today's RAG frameworks is the same idea with a general language model doing the rewriting.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Searching with the raw follow-up.
  • Sending the whole conversation to the retriever.
  • Searching on every turn.
  • Trusting a model gate unmeasured.
  • Carrying the old topic into a new question.
  • Unbounded memory.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How do you handle follow-up questions in a RAG chatbot?

How a strong answer sounds
A follow-up like "can you give an example of it?" has no searchable topic on its own, so before retrieval the system rewrites it into a standalone query using the recent conversation, replacing pronouns with what they refer to, typically with a short model call. It retrieves with that query, answers with the last few turns in context, and keeps older turns as a summary. It may also skip retrieval for turns like "thanks" or "make it shorter". Multi-turn test cases, including topic switches, check that the rewriting does not drift.

Deeper: Users complain the bot "forgets what we were talking about" in long chats, but also sometimes answers a new question as if it were about the old topic. What is going on?

How a strong answer sounds
Both are history-handling failures in opposite directions. Forgetting suggests the condensation or memory only sees the last turn, or older turns were dropped without a summary. Answering about the old topic suggests the condenser blends previous topics into a new, self-contained question. Build a multi-turn evaluation set with pronoun follow-ups, ellipsis and explicit topic switches; measure retrieval per turn for raw, history-prepended and condensed queries; then tune the condensation prompt to leave already-complete questions unchanged, keep a running summary, and consider a topic-shift check.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Raw follow-ups retrieve almost nothing; turn them into standalone queries.
  • Condensing with a model beat prepending the previous question here, but not on every kind of follow-up.
  • Gate retrieval for chit-chat, and measure the gate both ways.
  • Keep recent turns verbatim, summarise older ones, and bound memory.
  • Evaluate conversations turn by turn, including topic switches.
