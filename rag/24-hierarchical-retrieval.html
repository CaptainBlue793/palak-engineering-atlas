Long Documents: Hierarchical & Parent–Child Retrieval

Chunk size forces a trade-off. Small pieces match a query precisely, because a sentence about one idea has a vector about that idea, but they carry too little context to answer from. Large pieces carry context but blur the match. Hierarchical retrieval tries to have both: search at one level, return another. It also offers a top-down route, picking the right document first and then searching inside it.

The Atlas already exists at four levels: 83,118 sentences, 9,379 chunks, 4,230 sections and 616 chapters. This chapter searches and returns at each level and combines them. For each combination it measures how often the right chapter is returned and whether the returned text contains the term, against how many words it costs.

Small to big

Small-to-big retrieval indexes small units for matching and returns the larger unit each belongs to. Search sentences, return the paragraph or chunk around the best ones, merging hits that share a parent. The small unit's vector is sharp, and the returned text is enough to answer from. In the lab, searching sentences and returning their chunks had the term in the first result 74.8% of the time, against 64.4% for searching chunks directly. It also found the right chapter in the top 5 80.6% of the time against 72.2%. Returning bare sentences matched almost as often but gave the model about 64 words to work with for five results.

hits = sentence_index.search(q, k=50)
parents = []
for h in hits:                                   # best sentences first; keep each parent once
    p = parent_of[h.id]
    if p not in parents: parents.append(p)
    if len(parents) == 5: break
context = [chunk_text[p] for p in parents]

Parent–child chunks

The general form stores two or more levels with links between them: child chunks for search, parents for context. Children can be sentences, a sliding window of a few sentences, or small chunks. Parents can be chunks, sections or whole short documents. Store the link in the child's metadata, and fetch the parent text from a document store rather than the vector index. Returning sections instead of chunks gave little extra recall here (74.3% against 72.2% for the right chapter in five) at about three times the words (2,312 against 751). Bigger parents are not free.

Section summaries

Another hierarchy adds summaries: write a summary of each section or document, index the summaries, and use them either as retrievable units for broad questions or as a first stage that narrows the search. RAPTOR (below) builds a whole tree of them. Summaries help questions about a whole document ("what is this chapter about?") that no single chunk answers. They cost a model call per section at indexing time, and they must be regenerated when the text changes (Chapter 22).

Things to try.

  • Search sections: recall in between, at more than twice the words of chunks.
  • Route to 3 chapters: the right chapter falls to 48.4%. A wrong routing decision cannot be recovered, and a chapter's average vector says little about every term it defines.
  • Route to 10 chapters, or route by title: wider routing helps (56.5%), and titles alone are worse (39.0%).

Document routing

Routing first and searching second is attractive for very large collections, and for questions that clearly belong to one document, such as a contract, a manual, or one customer's files. It fails when documents cover many topics. A chapter about caching also defines TTL, eviction and stampedes, and the chapter's summary vector is close to none of them. Use routing only where the routing signal is reliable: metadata, an explicit product or tenant, or a classifier measured as in Chapter 15. Route to several candidates, not one, and keep a global search as a fallback.

Trade-offs

Every level you add is something to keep in sync: links, summaries and parent texts must change when the source does (Chapter 22). Sentence-level indexes are large. Here, 83,118 sentence vectors against 9,379 chunk vectors means about nine times the index memory (Chapter 8). Pick the smallest hierarchy that fixes a measured problem.

Use case: parent-document retrievers and RAPTOR

Small-to-big became a standard building block in RAG frameworks. LangChain's ParentDocumentRetriever indexes small child chunks and returns their parent documents. LlamaIndex offers a sentence-window parser, which indexes single sentences and returns a window of neighbours, and an auto-merging retriever, which returns a parent when enough of its children match. At the other end, RAPTOR (Parth Sarthi and colleagues at Stanford, presented at ICLR 2024) builds a tree bottom-up. It clusters chunks, has a model summarise each cluster, clusters the summaries, and repeats, then retrieves from every level of the tree at once. On long-document question answering, where answers need the gist of many passages, it beat flat chunk retrieval. Both designs follow the lab's lesson: separate what you match on from what you hand to the model.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Returning the matched sentence alone.
  • Returning whole documents.
  • Routing to one document by an averaged vector.
  • Duplicate parents.
  • Stale summaries.
  • Ignoring index growth from sentence-level indexes.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is small-to-big retrieval and why use it?

How a strong answer sounds
You index small units, such as sentences or short chunks, because their embeddings match a specific question sharply, but you return the larger unit each belongs to, such as the surrounding chunk or section, because the model needs context to answer. Each child stores its parent's id; at query time you search children, take the best distinct parents and send their text. It gets the precision of small chunks and the context of large ones, at the cost of a bigger index and links to maintain.

Deeper: Your corpus is 50,000 long manuals. A teammate proposes routing each question to the single best manual by its summary embedding, then searching inside it. What are the risks, and what would you measure?

How a strong answer sounds
Routing errors are unrecoverable: if the router picks the wrong manual, no amount of good in-document search helps. A summary or average embedding of a long, multi-topic document is close to its main theme and far from the details people ask about. In testing, routing to three chapters by mean vector cut the right-chapter rate in the top five from about 0.72 to under 0.5. Measure routing accuracy and end-to-end recall on labelled questions against a global search. Prefer reliable routing signals (product, model number, tenant), route to several candidates, and fall back to global search when the router is unsure.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Match on small units, return their larger parents: small-to-big.
  • Bigger parents add words faster than they add recall.
  • Routing by averaged or title vectors lost many right chapters; route only on reliable signals.
  • Summaries help whole-document questions but cost calls and maintenance.
  • Every level is an index to size and a link to keep in sync.
