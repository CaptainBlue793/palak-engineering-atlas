Choosing & Using Embedding Models

There are hundreds of embedding models, and leaderboards rank them by fractions of a point. Choosing among them matters less than most teams think, and using one correctly matters more: the right query prefix, a sensible input length, normalisation, numeric precision, and above all whether its strengths suit your queries. A small model used well on the right data can beat a large one used carelessly, and keyword search can beat both.

This chapter explains what benchmarks measure and how to choose, then measures the choices around one model, bge-small-en-v1.5, on two very different test sets: SciFact's scientific claims and the Atlas corpus's glossary terms, with BM25 from Chapter 11 as the baseline.

  • Read an embedding benchmark and know what it does not tell you
  • Trade model size against speed, memory and quality
  • Apply query prefixes where they help, and check that they do
  • Use reduced precision and input length to save time safely
  • Test domain fit against a keyword baseline before committing

What benchmarks measure

The Massive Text Embedding Benchmark (MTEB, 2022) scores models on dozens of datasets across tasks: retrieval, reranking, clustering, classification and semantic similarity, in many languages. Its retrieval part is largely the BEIR collection, scored by nDCG@10. A leaderboard average is a useful shortlist, but it mixes tasks you may not care about, it can reward models trained on data close to the test sets, and small differences are within noise. What it cannot tell you is how a model does on your documents and your users' questions. Shortlist from the benchmark's retrieval scores in your language, then measure on your own labelled set (Chapter 19).

Size, dimensions and speed

Embedding models range from about 20 million parameters (MiniLM, bge-small: 384 dimensions) through base models (about 110 million, 768 dimensions) to large and LLM-based models with billions of parameters and 1,024 to 4,096 dimensions. Bigger models are usually better on benchmarks and always slower to run and costlier to store: embedding time scales with parameters and tokens, index memory with dimensions. For a corpus that is re-embedded rarely and searched often, query latency and index size usually matter more than one-off encoding time. Some newer models are trained so that their vectors can be truncated to fewer dimensions with little loss (Matryoshka embeddings), which helps with the memory side.

Query and passage prefixes

Many retrieval models were trained with an instruction on one or both sides, and expect it at query time. bge's card recommends "Represent this sentence for searching relevant passages: " before short queries and nothing before passages; E5 expects "query: " and "passage: "; instruction-tuned models accept a task description. The lab shows why "follow the card" is the start rather than the end: the prefix helped one kind of query and hurt another. Check it on your own questions.

Things to try.

  • default: the model beats BM25 on SciFact (0.708 vs 0.663) and loses to it on the Atlas terms (0.763 vs 0.853). Claims are paraphrased sentences; glossary terms are short exact phrases. Domain and query type decide.
  • no query prefix: Atlas recall drops (0.731) but SciFact rises (0.724). The prefix suits short keyword-like queries; SciFact's queries are full sentences that already look like passages.
  • not normalised: identical, because this model's last layer already normalises (every vector had length 1.000). Check your model rather than assuming.
  • fp16: identical scores, encoding 2.5× faster. max 128 tokens is faster still but clearly worse, because it reads only the start of each passage.
  • The overlap: 271 Atlas queries were found only by BM25 and 97 only by the embedding model. Neither is a superset of the other, which is the case for hybrid search (Chapter 12).

Domain fit

Embedding models learn what "similar" means from their training pairs, mostly web questions and answers, scientific papers and forum posts. They transfer well to text like that and less well to specialised vocabulary, internal acronyms, product codes and short keyword queries, where BM25 often wins. The queries BM25 alone found here were things like sys.path, 2^53 limit and dependency_overrides; the ones only embeddings found were general words like "Transaction" and "Authorization" whose defining sections use other phrasing. If your domain is far from the training data, consider fine-tuning a small model on your own pairs (Chapter 33) or a multilingual model for multilingual content, and always keep keyword search alongside.

Use case: BEIR and the zero-shot gap

In 2021 Nandan Thakur and colleagues at the University of Waterloo and TU Darmstadt published BEIR, a benchmark of 18 retrieval datasets from different domains, including SciFact, to test retrievers on data they were not trained for. Their finding surprised a field that had moved quickly to neural retrieval: dense retrievers trained on the large MS MARCO question set often failed to beat BM25 once moved to other domains, and BM25 remained one of the most robust systems across the collection, while rerankers and late-interaction models generalised better. BEIR reshaped how embedding models are trained and judged. Later models, including bge and E5, trained on far more diverse pairs and now beat BM25 on many BEIR datasets, as bge-small did on SciFact here. But the lesson stands, and the Atlas set shows it again: a model's average score says little about your domain and query style, so test it against BM25 on your own data.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Picking the top of the leaderboard without testing on your data.
  • Skipping the BM25 baseline, so a weaker dense setup goes unnoticed.
  • Applying prefixes blindly, or forgetting them entirely.
  • Cutting the input length to save time without measuring the loss.
  • Changing the model without re-embedding the whole corpus.
  • Ignoring query latency and index size when comparing models.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How would you choose an embedding model for a new RAG system?

How a strong answer sounds
Shortlist a few models from the MTEB retrieval scores in the right language and size range, then evaluate them on a labelled set of real questions from the domain, using recall@k and nDCG, with BM25 as a baseline. I would check each model's recommended query and passage prefixes and verify they help, keep chunks within its input length, consider fp16 for speed, and weigh query latency, index size and hosting cost. Usually a small or base model plus hybrid search and a reranker beats choosing the largest model alone.

Deeper: A new embedding model scores 3 points higher on MTEB. Should you switch your production index to it?

How a strong answer sounds
Only after measuring on our own evaluation set: an average across dozens of benchmark datasets may not reflect our domain or query style, and some benchmark gains come from training data that overlaps the test sets. If it wins on our data by more than the noise, I would also weigh its size, query latency and dimensions, since index memory and cost change. Switching means re-embedding the entire corpus, so I would build a new index in parallel, compare online with a slice of traffic or shadow queries, and keep the old index for rollback (Chapter 35).

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Benchmarks shortlist; your own questions decide.
  • Always compare against BM25: here it won on short exact terms and lost on claims.
  • Prefixes help some query types and hurt others: measure.
  • fp16 kept rankings identical at 2.5× the speed; very short input limits cost quality.
  • Dense and keyword retrieval find different queries; combine them.
