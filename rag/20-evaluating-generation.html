Evaluating Answers: Faithfulness & Relevance

Good retrieval does not guarantee a good answer. The model can ignore the context, mix in its own memory, change a number, or answer a different question. Answer evaluation asks two things. Is the answer faithful: is everything it says supported by the retrieved context? And is it relevant: does it address what was asked? Grading thousands of answers by hand is slow, so teams use a model as the judge, and the judge needs evaluating too.

The lab gives each of 146 glossary questions three candidate answers: the correct definition; a fluent definition of a different term; and the correct definition with one fact changed ("always" to "never", "add" to "remove", "24" to "23"). Three judges score all of them against the source. A 3B model is then tested as a pairwise judge for position and length bias.

  • Define faithfulness and answer relevance, and how each is checked
  • Measure a judge against answers whose correctness you control
  • Recognise position and length bias in model judges
  • Choose between embedding, cross-encoder and model judges
  • Plan human review alongside automatic judges

Faithfulness

An answer is faithful if every claim in it is supported by the context it was given, whether or not the claim is true in the world. Faithfulness is the RAG-specific quality: an unfaithful answer is a hallucination even when it happens to be correct, because nothing in the system guarantees it. Check it claim by claim. Split the answer into statements, and ask whether the context entails each one, with a natural-language-inference model, a model judge, or a person.

Answer relevance

A faithful answer can still miss the question by summarising the context instead of answering it, or by answering a neighbouring question. Relevance judges whether the answer addresses what was asked, usually by a judge reading the question and answer together. One common trick is to have a model generate the question the answer seems to respond to, and compare it with the real one. In the lab, the 3B model asked "does this answer address the question?" said yes to only 18% of correct definitions. A judge that strict is unusable without calibration.

JUDGE = "Context: {c}\n\nAnswer: {a}\n\nIs every claim in the answer supported by the context? Answer Yes or No."
p = probs(judge_model, JUDGE.format(c=context, a=answer))           # read P(Yes) from the log-probabilities
score = p["yes"] / (p["yes"] + p["no"])                        # a sortable score, not a coin flip

Model as judge

A model judge reads the context and answer and gives a verdict or a score. It is cheap and scalable, and only as good as its agreement with careful humans. Measure it the same way as any classifier: build answers whose status you know, and check how well its scores separate them. In the lab, every judge spotted a swapped answer, another term's definition, with AUC around 0.97–0.99. Even an embedding similarity did that. Only the model judge did better than chance on subtle errors, a single changed fact, and only modestly: AUC 0.63 against 0.54 for embedding similarity and 0.53 for the cross-encoder.

Things to try.

  • Any judge, another term's definition: AUC near 1. Off-topic answers are easy to catch.
  • Embedding similarity, one fact changed: AUC 0.54, chance. "Always kept" and "never kept" embed almost identically.
  • Model judge: faithful?: it said "supported" to 51% of correct answers and 34% of subtly wrong ones. It is better than chance, but strict and noisy.
  • Bias: position: asked which of two answers was better supported, it picked the second one 82% of the time, whichever answer was there.
  • Bias: length: it preferred the padded copy of the correct answer 90% of the time, in either position.

Judge bias

Model judges have systematic biases. Studies of models judging other models found them favouring the first or second position, longer answers, and answers written by models like themselves. In the lab the small judge picked the second answer far more than the first. It was consistent across the two orders only 34% of the time when choosing between the right answer and its subtly wrong twin. It also preferred a padded copy of a correct answer to the plain one, consistently. Defences:

  • Judge each pair in both orders and keep only consistent verdicts.
  • Prefer pointwise scores against a rubric over pairwise preferences.
  • Control or penalise length.
  • Use a stronger judge than the model being judged.
  • Check the judge against human labels before trusting it.

Human review

Automatic judges scale; humans set the standard. Keep a small, regularly refreshed sample of real answers graded by people with domain knowledge, using a written rubric: faithful, relevant, complete, harmful. Measure how often two reviewers agree, because if humans disagree, no judge can be calibrated. Use that sample to calibrate and monitor the automatic judges, and send them the cases they are unsure about. Review is also where new failure types are found first.

Use case: "Judging LLM-as-a-Judge" and RAGAS

In 2023 Lianmin Zheng and colleagues, the team behind Vicuna and Chatbot Arena, published "Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena". They found that strong models as judges agreed with human preferences about as often as humans agreed with each other. They also documented position bias, verbosity bias and self-enhancement bias, and proposed swapping positions and using reference answers to reduce them. The same year, the RAGAS framework (Shahul Es and colleagues) made model-judged faithfulness and answer relevance standard RAG metrics: faithfulness by splitting answers into statements and checking each against the context, relevance by generating questions from the answer. The lab's small judge shows why those safeguards matter. The weaker the judge, the larger its biases.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Trusting a judge you have not evaluated.
  • Testing only off-topic wrong answers.
  • Pairwise judging in one order.
  • Rewarding length.
  • Using similarity as a faithfulness metric.
  • No human anchor.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How do you evaluate the answers of a RAG system, not just its retrieval?

How a strong answer sounds
Two main properties: faithfulness, whether every claim is supported by the retrieved context, and relevance, whether it answers the question. Faithfulness is checked claim by claim with an entailment model or a model judge; relevance by a judge reading question and answer. Automatic judges must themselves be evaluated against answers with known status and against human labels, because they have biases: position, length, self-preference. A small human-reviewed sample with a rubric anchors the whole thing and catches new failure types.

Deeper: Your team uses a model judge for faithfulness and reports 95%. How would you check that number?

How a strong answer sounds
Build a validation set where faithfulness is known: correct answers, answers from the wrong source, and answers with one fact subtly changed; measure the judge's AUC and its false-acceptance rate on each kind. Easy errors like off-topic answers inflate scores; subtle numeric or negation errors are what judges miss. In testing, a small judge separated off-topic answers almost perfectly but subtle ones only at AUC around 0.6. Also compare against human labels on real answers, test pairwise judgments in both orders for position bias, check for length bias, and consider a stronger judge model than the generator.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Faithfulness asks whether the context supports every claim; relevance asks whether the question was answered.
  • Evaluate judges on controlled answers: off-topic errors are easy, single-fact errors are hard.
  • Embedding similarity cannot see a flipped fact.
  • Small model judges showed strong position and length bias; test both orders, control length.
  • A human-reviewed sample anchors every automatic judge.
