Reranking with Cross-Encoders

Retrieval has to be fast over millions of chunks, so it compares precomputed vectors. A reranker gets more time per candidate. It reads the query and each of the top few dozen chunks together and scores how well they match, then reorders them. Reranking is one of the most recommended upgrades in RAG, and it often helps. Whether it helps you depends on one comparison: is the reranker better than your first stage, on your data?

This chapter puts a popular small cross-encoder behind three first stages (BM25, dense and the hybrid from Chapter 12) on two datasets. It lifted the weak first stages and pulled the strongest one down. It also compares a 3B language model as reranker and measures what each costs in latency.

Bi- vs cross-encoders

An embedding model is a bi-encoder. It encodes the query and each document separately into vectors, so documents can be encoded once, indexed, and compared with any query by a dot product. That speed has a price: the document's vector is fixed before the model ever sees the question. A cross-encoder feeds the query and one document into the transformer together, so every word of the question can attend to every word of the passage, and it outputs one relevance score. It is more accurate per pair, but nothing can be precomputed. Every query–document pair is a full forward pass, so it is used only on a shortlist.

from sentence_transformers import CrossEncoder
reranker = CrossEncoder("cross-encoder/ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2", max_length=512)
candidates = first_stage(query, k=50)                                   # retrieve wide
scores = reranker.predict([(query, doc.text) for doc in candidates])
top = [d for _, d in sorted(zip(scores, candidates), key=lambda p: -p[0])][:5]  # rerank narrow

Retrieve wide, rerank narrow

The usual pipeline retrieves the top 20 to 100 cheaply, reranks them, and passes the best 3 to 10 to the model. The first stage's job changes. It no longer needs the right chunk at rank 1, only somewhere in the shortlist, which is recall@N (Chapter 19). In the lab, 86% of the relevant SciFact abstracts sat in the hybrid's top 10 and 96% in its top 100. That is the most any reranker could recover. Deeper shortlists raise that ceiling, but they cost more latency and give the reranker more chances to promote a wrong chunk.

Latency budgets

Reranking cost grows linearly with depth, because each candidate is a separate forward pass of up to 512 tokens. On the lab's 4 GB laptop GPU the MiniLM cross-encoder took about 45 ms for 20 candidates in half precision and 177 ms for 100. Full precision took about 2.5 times as long. On the CPU, 20 candidates took 1,563 ms and 100 took 7.0 seconds, too slow for interactive use at that depth. Truncating passages, using a smaller model, batching and half precision all help. The budget decides the depth.

LLM rerankers

A general language model can rerank too. Ask it whether each passage answers the query, and read off the probability of "Yes" rather than the generated word, so the scores can be sorted. LLM rerankers can follow domain instructions and handle unusual queries, but they cost far more per pair. In the lab, qwen2.5:3b took about 3.3 seconds per query to judge 20 passages, against tens of milliseconds for the cross-encoder. Larger hosted models are better rankers and slower still. Listwise prompts, which ask the model to order all candidates at once, reduce calls but are harder to parse and limited by context length.

Measuring the lift

The lab's lesson is that a reranker replaces your first stage's ordering with its own. It helps when its judgement on your data is better than the first stage's, and hurts when it is worse. This model was trained on web search questions. On scientific claims it scored about 0.68 whatever it reranked, close to the 0.688 that the BEIR benchmark reports for the same kind of model over BM25 on SciFact. Measure before and after on your own labelled queries (Chapter 19), count per-query wins and losses, and try the reranker over each first stage you might ship. Rephrasing the Atlas terms as questions ("What is …?") made no useful difference. Stronger or domain-tuned rerankers (Chapter 33) are the next lever.

Use case: BERT reranking on MS MARCO, and BEIR's warning

In January 2019 Rodrigo Nogueira and Kyunghyun Cho posted "Passage Re-ranking with BERT". They fine-tuned BERT as a cross-encoder on Microsoft's MS MARCO passage dataset, real Bing queries with human-marked answers, and used it to rerank BM25's top 1,000. It took first place on the leaderboard by a wide margin, nearly doubling BM25's MRR@10. Reranking a cheap first stage with a cross-encoder became the standard recipe, and small distilled versions such as the MiniLM model in this chapter made it affordable. In 2021 the BEIR benchmark (Nandan Thakur and colleagues) tested retrievers and rerankers zero-shot on 18 different datasets. On average, BM25 plus a cross-encoder generalised better than dense retrievers trained on MS MARCO. But the gains varied widely by dataset, and on some the reranker barely moved or lowered the score. The lab reproduces both halves: big lifts over weak first stages, no lift or a loss over a strong one.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Adding a reranker without measuring it on your own data.
  • Reranking too few candidates.
  • Reranking too many on a CPU.
  • Using a reranker trained on one kind of query for another.
  • Comparing only averages.
  • Generating "yes"/"no" with an LLM instead of reading its probability.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is a cross-encoder reranker and why use one?

How a strong answer sounds
Retrieval uses a bi-encoder: query and documents are embedded separately, so documents can be indexed in advance and searched fast, but the model never sees them together. A cross-encoder reads the query and one document side by side and outputs a relevance score, which is more accurate because the words can attend to each other, but nothing can be precomputed. So you retrieve a shortlist of 20 to 100 cheaply, rerank that with the cross-encoder, and send the best few to the model. It costs latency proportional to the shortlist, and it only helps if the reranker is better than the first stage on your data.

Deeper: You added a well-known cross-encoder and offline nDCG went down. What happened, and what do you do?

How a strong answer sounds
Reranking replaces the first stage's order with the reranker's. If your first stage is already strong, for example a tuned hybrid, and the reranker was trained on a different kind of query, such as web questions when your users type product codes or scientific claims, its judgement can be worse than what it replaces. Check per-query wins and losses, try it over each first stage, and try other depths, since deeper can be worse. Then try a stronger or domain-specific reranker, fine-tune one on your own labelled pairs (Chapter 33), or keep the first stage and spend the latency elsewhere. Ship only what wins on your labelled set and in an online test.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Cross-encoders read query and passage together: more accurate, but only affordable on a shortlist.
  • A reranker can only reorder; recall@N of the first stage is its ceiling.
  • It lifted weak first stages and lowered the strongest one: measure on your data.
  • Latency grows with depth; half precision on a GPU cut it by about 2.5 times.
  • LLM rerankers can match a cross-encoder but cost seconds per query.
