Prompting with Retrieved Context

Retrieval finds the right chunks; the prompt decides whether the model uses them. The same chunks can yield a cited, grounded answer or a confident paragraph from the model's own memory. Which one you get depends on:

  • how the sources are labelled;
  • where the question sits;
  • where the right chunk sits;
  • how many chunks surround it;
  • whether the model has been told it may say "I don't know".

This chapter measures each of those choices with a local 3-billion-parameter model on 60 questions about Atlas glossary terms. Each question comes with sources in which exactly one mentions the term, so whether the model cites that source is an exact test of grounding.

  • Write a prompt template that labels sources and asks for citations
  • Explain how the right chunk's position changes the answer
  • Give the model explicit permission to say "I don't know", and measure its effect
  • Choose how many chunks to send within a token budget
  • Grade grounding automatically with citations

Prompt templates

A RAG prompt has four parts:

  • the instructions, usually in the system message;
  • the sources, each with a number and a title;
  • the question;
  • sometimes an answer format.

The instructions should say three things plainly: answer only from the sources, cite them, and what to do when they do not contain the answer. Number the sources so citations are short and machine-checkable. Include each source's title so the model can tell a definition from a passing mention. Keep the template in code, versioned like any other code, because small wording changes move the results.

# the template used in the lab ("grounded, question last")

Ordering chunks

Retrievers return chunks in rank order, and most pipelines paste them in that order, best first. Research on long-context models has found that they use information at the start and end of a prompt better than information in the middle (Chapter 16 covers this "lost in the middle" effect). The lab measured something more specific for this model. With the question after the sources, the model cited the right source 65% of the time when it came first, 55% at fifth of ten, and only 13% when it came last, just before the question. Order is worth testing on your own model rather than assuming. Best first is a sound default, because the best chunk is usually the right one.

Asking for citations

Citations serve two purposes. They let readers check an answer. They also let you grade grounding automatically: if you know which source holds the answer, a missing or wrong citation is a measurable failure. The lab's bare template, sources and question with no instructions, produced fluent answers with no citation in 60 of 60 cases. Many of those answers were right, but nothing showed whether they came from the sources or from the model's training. Asking for citations is cheap. Checking that the cited source supports the claim is Chapter 17's subject.

Permission to say "I don't know"

A model fills silence. When the right source was removed and only nine near-miss sources remained, the bare template still answered 55 of 60 questions, mostly from memory, phrased as though the Atlas said so. With the grounded template, which says exactly what to reply when the sources fall short, it abstained in 44 of 60. The cost of that permission shows up when the source is present: the same instruction made the model say "I don't know" in some cases it could have answered, especially with the question placed first. Treat abstention as a dial to measure in both directions, not a switch.

Things to try.

  • 1st → 5th → last of 10: citations of the right source fall from 65% to 55% to 13%, and "I don't know" rises to 43% when the right source sits last.
  • 3 → 10 → 20 sources: prompts grow from about 593 to 3,557 tokens. At 20 sources the model mostly stops citing (39 of 60 answers had no citation), although many answers are still right in substance.
  • Bare template: no citations at all, and answers about 2.8 s long against about 1.3 s, because without instructions the model writes more.
  • Question first: the same instructions with the question before the sources made the model abstain 33 times out of 60, against 10 with the question last.
  • Right source missing, grounded vs bare: 44 against 5 honest "I don't know" answers.

Token budgets

Every source costs prompt tokens. Here each 120-word source was about 120 tokens, so 20 sources made a 3,500-token prompt. That cost shows up as prefill time and, with hosted models, as money (Chapter 29). A small model's attention also spreads thinner as the prompt grows, as the 20-source result shows. Set the number of chunks with a budget in mind:

  • enough to include the right one most of the time, which is what retrieval recall@k tells you (Chapter 19);
  • few enough that the model still uses them;
  • with a reranker (Chapter 13) to put the best few first.

Always set the context length explicitly. Ollama's default silently truncated a long prompt in Prerequisite 2.

Use case: "Lost in the Middle" and long-context prompting

In 2023 Nelson Liu and colleagues at Stanford published "Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts" (later in Transactions of the ACL, 2024). They gave models a question and a set of retrieved documents, one of which held the answer, and moved that document through every position. Accuracy formed a U shape: highest when the answer was at the start or the end, and lowest in the middle. In some settings it fell below the model's accuracy with no documents at all. The paper's practical advice matches this chapter's: rerank, send fewer and better chunks, and test where your model looks. The lab's 3B model showed a different shape, with the end worst rather than the middle, which is the paper's real lesson: position effects are real, model-specific and worth measuring.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Pasting chunks without numbers or titles.
  • No instruction for when the sources fall short.
  • Measuring abstention only on unanswerable questions.
  • Sending every retrieved chunk "to be safe".
  • Assuming another model's position effects apply to yours.
  • Leaving the context length at a default that silently truncates.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What goes into a good RAG prompt?

How a strong answer sounds
Instructions that say to answer only from the given sources, to cite them by number, and exactly what to say if they do not contain the answer. Then the sources, numbered and titled, in rank order, limited to the best few within a token budget. Then the question last. Numbered citations make answers checkable by readers and by automatic tests, and the explicit "I don't know" path stops the model filling gaps from memory. The template lives in version control, and each choice — order, count, wording — is checked against an evaluation set, because effects differ by model.

Deeper: After adding "say I don't know if the sources don't contain the answer", your RAG assistant refuses far more often, including on questions it used to answer correctly. What do you do?

How a strong answer sounds
Measure both sides on a labelled set: answerable questions, where abstention is a failure, and unanswerable ones, where it is correct. Then check whether the right chunk was actually in the prompt for the new refusals. If it was missing, the problem is retrieval, and the abstention is correct. If it was present, look at its position, how many chunks surround it, whether the question comes before or after the sources, and the exact wording of the instruction, since all of these moved abstention in testing. Try putting the question last, sending fewer, reranked chunks, or softening the wording. Pick the variant with the best balance of correct answers and correct refusals for the product's risk.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Number and title the sources, ask for citations, and give an exact "I don't know" path.
  • Without instructions the model answered every question, even when the right source was missing.
  • Where the right chunk sits changed citation accuracy from 65% to 13% for this model.
  • More chunks cost tokens and, past a point, attention; rerank and send the best few.
  • Measure abstention on answerable and unanswerable questions alike.
