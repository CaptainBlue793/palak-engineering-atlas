Hybrid Search & Rank Fusion

BM25 and embeddings fail on different queries. BM25 misses paraphrases; embeddings miss rare exact terms, codes and names. Chapter 5 found 271 Atlas questions that only BM25 answered and 97 that only the embedding model did. Hybrid search runs both and merges the two ranked lists. It is the default in most production RAG systems, but how you merge matters: the common default, reciprocal rank fusion with k = 60, did not beat the better single retriever on either dataset here.

This chapter fuses BM25 (Chapter 11) and bge-small (Chapter 5) on SciFact, where embeddings lead, and on the Atlas glossary set, where BM25 leads. It compares rank fusion and three kinds of weighted score fusion, choosing weights by cross-validation so the gains are not flattered by tuning on the test set.

Why combine

A keyword index matches the exact words of the query, weighted by how rare they are, so it shines on identifiers, error messages, product codes, names and jargon. An embedding model matches meaning, so it shines on paraphrases and questions phrased differently from the text. Their failures overlap only partly, so a merged list can contain the right document more often than either list alone. The merge is the hard part: the two retrievers score on unrelated scales.

Score normalisation

Here, the median top BM25 score was 28 on SciFact, falling to 7 by rank 100. The median top cosine similarity was 0.79, falling to 0.67. Add them raw and BM25's spread of about 21 points swamps cosine's 0.12, so the "hybrid" is BM25 with a little noise. Fix it by putting both on a common scale before combining, separately for each query:

  • Min-max maps each retriever's candidate scores to the range 0 to 1.
  • Z-score subtracts the mean and divides by the standard deviation.

Either way, a weight α decides how much the dense score counts: final = α·dense + (1 − α)·keyword.

def rrf(rankings, k=60):                       # rankings: lists of doc ids, best first
    score = defaultdict(float)
    for ranking in rankings:
        for rank, doc in enumerate(ranking, start=1):
            score[doc] += 1 / (k + rank)
    return sorted(score, key=score.get, reverse=True)

def weighted(dense, keyword, alpha):          # dicts doc -> score over the union of candidates
    z = lambda s: {d: (v - mean(s.values())) / stdev(s.values()) for d, v in s.items()}
    zd, zk = z(dense), z(keyword)
    return sorted(zd, key=lambda d: alpha * zd[d] + (1 - alpha) * zk[d], reverse=True)

Reciprocal rank fusion

Reciprocal rank fusion (RRF) ignores scores and uses only ranks. Each document gets 1/(k + rank) from every list it appears in, and the sums decide the order. It needs no normalisation and no training data, which is why vector databases and search engines offer it as the default hybrid mode. The constant k controls how steeply the reward falls with rank. With k = 60, the value from the original paper, ranks 1 and 10 earn 1/61 and 1/70: nearly the same. A document ranked moderately in both lists then beats one ranked first in a single list. With k = 1, the top of each list dominates. The lab found the smaller k better on both datasets.

Weighted fusion

Weighted fusion uses the scores, normalised, with a tuned α. It can exploit the fact that one retriever is better on your data: α of 0.5 to 0.6 on SciFact, nearer 0.3 (more BM25) on the Atlas set. The catch is that α must be tuned on labelled queries. Picking the best α on the same queries you report on overstates the gain. The lab therefore repeatedly chose α on a random half of the queries and scored the other half.

Things to try.

  • RRF, k 60: 0.702 on SciFact against dense alone at 0.708, and 0.843 on the Atlas set against BM25 alone at 0.854. The textbook default loses to the better retriever on both.
  • RRF, k 1: 0.721 and 0.869. Rewarding the top of each list wins on both.
  • Weighted, raw scores, α 0.5: indistinguishable from BM25, because BM25's scores are spread more than a hundred times wider. Only at α 0.9 or above does the dense score count.
  • Weighted, z-score, α 0 → 1: an arch on both datasets. Its peak is at α 0.6 on SciFact and α 0.3 on the Atlas set, where BM25 is stronger.
  • The cross-validated gain shown in the stats: z-score fusion adds about 0.024 nDCG on SciFact and 0.020 recall on the Atlas set. That is smaller than the best α suggests, but real.

When hybrid does not help

Hybrid search helps most when both retrievers are reasonably good and fail on different queries. It helps little, or hurts, in three situations:

  • One retriever is much weaker, and equal-weight fusion lets its noise in.
  • The merge ignores scale, as raw-score sums do.
  • RRF's k flattens rank differences that matter.

With RRF at k = 60, per-query results here moved in both directions. On SciFact it beat dense on 49 claims and lost on 51, and the 95% interval of the difference straddled zero (-0.033 to +0.020). Measure on your own labelled queries before and after, as Chapter 19 describes. Remember that a reranker (Chapter 13) after either list may matter more than the fusion method.

Use case: reciprocal rank fusion, from TREC to vector databases

Gordon Cormack, Charles Clarke and Stefan Büttcher introduced reciprocal rank fusion at SIGIR 2009 in a short paper titled "Reciprocal Rank Fusion outperforms Condorcet and individual Rank Learning Methods". They combined many ranked lists submitted to TREC evaluations, using k = 60 from a pilot experiment, and found the simple formula beat more elaborate fusion methods. Its appeal for hybrid search is that it needs no score calibration. Elasticsearch and OpenSearch offer it for combining keyword and vector queries, and several vector databases use it as their hybrid default. Others offer weighted fusion instead; Weaviate's hybrid search, for example, exposes an alpha parameter from pure keyword (0) to pure vector (1). The lab shows why both exist, and why k = 60, tuned for fusing many TREC runs, is not necessarily right for fusing two retrievers.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Adding raw BM25 and cosine scores.
  • Tuning α on the evaluation queries and reporting that score.
  • Assuming hybrid always helps.
  • Keeping RRF's k = 60 without testing.
  • Fusing too few candidates, such as the top 5 of each.
  • Normalising over the whole corpus rather than each query's candidates.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is hybrid search, and how do you merge the results?

How a strong answer sounds
Hybrid search runs a keyword retriever such as BM25 and a vector retriever together, because keywords catch exact terms, names and codes while embeddings catch paraphrases. The scores are on different scales, so you either merge by rank with reciprocal rank fusion, summing 1/(k + rank) across lists, or normalise each retriever's scores per query (min-max or z-score) and take a weighted sum with a tuned α. RRF needs no tuning; weighted fusion can do better when one retriever is stronger, but α must be tuned on held-out labelled queries.

Deeper: You added hybrid search with RRF and the offline metric went down slightly. Why might that be, and what would you try?

How a strong answer sounds
RRF gives equal weight to both lists. If one retriever is clearly weaker on your data, its mid-ranked noise displaces good results from the stronger list, and with k = 60 the reward for rank 1 is barely larger than for rank 10, so consensus beats confidence. Check per-query wins and losses with a confidence interval, not just the mean. Then try a smaller k, weighted fusion with α tuned by cross-validation, or using the weaker retriever only to add candidates for a reranker rather than to order them. Also confirm that both retrievers return enough candidates, and that their tokenisation and query prefix are right.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Keyword and vector retrieval fail on different queries; hybrid search merges them.
  • Raw scores are on different scales; normalise per query or fuse by rank.
  • RRF's k matters: k = 60 lost to the better retriever here, and k = 1 won.
  • Weighted z-score fusion gained most, measured with α chosen on held-out queries.
  • Check per-query wins and losses, not only the average.
