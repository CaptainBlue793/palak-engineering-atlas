Vector Databases & Metadata Filters

An index such as HNSW answers one question: which vectors are closest to this one? Real RAG queries nearly always add conditions. Only this customer's documents. Only English. Only policies in force after 2024. Only the course the student is reading. A vector database keeps metadata beside each vector and combines conditions with similarity. How it combines them decides whether a filtered search returns the right ten results, three results, or none.

This chapter explains what a database adds to an index, then measures the three ways of filtering on the 83,118 Atlas sentence embeddings from Chapters 7 and 8. Each sentence is tagged with its course and chapter, giving filters that keep from 16% of the data down to 0.16%.

Index vs database

faiss and hnswlib are indexes: they hold vectors and integer ids in memory and answer nearest-neighbour queries. A vector database wraps an index with what an application needs around it:

  • Storage of the original text and metadata.
  • Inserts, updates and deletes that become searchable without a rebuild.
  • Persistence and backups, and replication and sharding.
  • Access control.
  • Filtering on metadata, which this chapter is about.

That can be a dedicated product, a search engine with vector fields, or an ordinary relational database with a vector extension such as pgvector for PostgreSQL.

Metadata

Each chunk should carry the fields that queries will filter or display on:

  • The source document id, title and URL, for citations.
  • The tenant or owner and the access groups, for permissions (Chapter 23).
  • Language, document type and product.
  • Created and updated dates.
  • The position within the document, for fetching neighbours (Chapter 24).

Store filterable fields as typed values, not inside the text, and index the ones queries filter on, as you would in any database. A filter's selectivity is the share of vectors it keeps. As the lab shows, selectivity decides which filtering strategy works.

-- pgvector: similarity plus a metadata condition in one SQL query
SELECT id, text FROM chunks
WHERE course = 'databases' AND lang = 'en'
ORDER BY embedding <=> :query_vector      -- cosine distance
LIMIT 10;

Pre-filtering

Pre-filtering finds the matching vectors first, using an ordinary index on the metadata, and then searches only those. With exact search over the matches, recall is perfect by construction, and the cost is proportional to the number of matches. That makes it the cheapest option for selective filters and the most expensive for broad ones. Pre-filtering an approximate index is harder, because the graph or lists were built over all the vectors, and that is what the third strategy addresses.

Post-filtering

Post-filtering searches the whole index for the top k′ and then throws away the results that fail the filter. It is simple and works with any index, but if the filter keeps 10% of the data, a top 10 contains about one match on average. You must over-fetch, asking for 100 or 1,000 to keep 10, and for selective filters no reasonable k′ is enough. The third strategy, used by most modern vector databases, applies the filter during the graph walk. The search still moves through non-matching nodes to navigate, but only matching ones can enter the result list. It keeps recall high, at the price of exploring more of the graph when matches are rare.

Things to try.

  • DSA (16%), post-filter top 10: recall 0.14, and 96% of queries got fewer than 10 results. Most of the top 10 belonged to other courses.
  • Post-filter top 100 → top 1,000 on a course filter: recall rises to about 0.97, but over-fetching 1,000 costs about 3.2 ms, about eight times the latency of fetching 100.
  • One chapter (0.16%): post-filtering fails at every k′ (0.16 at top 1,000). Filtering during the walk still reaches 1.00, but explores far more of the graph (53 ms). Pre-filtering just scores the ~136 matches exactly in 0.04 ms.
  • Walk the filter seg from DSA to one chapter with "Filter during the walk": recall stays near 1, while latency grows as matches thin out.

Choosing a store

The lab gives the rule most query planners use. Use pre-filtering with exact search when the filter is selective enough that the matches are few, filtering during the walk for everything broader, and post-filtering only with generous over-fetch for broad filters. For tenants that are always filtered on, separate collections or partitions per tenant avoid the problem altogether. When choosing a store, weigh the following against your data size and team:

  • Filtered-search support, and how it handles selective filters.
  • Update and delete behaviour.
  • Hybrid keyword search (Chapter 12).
  • Operations: backups, scaling and monitoring.
  • Whether you already run it.

🗄️
Use case: filtered search in pgvector and its peers

pgvector adds vector columns and HNSW and IVF indexes to PostgreSQL. Early versions filtered an approximate index search after the fact. The HNSW scan returned ef_search candidates (40 by default) and PostgreSQL then applied the WHERE clause, so a filter that kept 10% of rows left about four results on average. That is the post-filtering failure measured above. Version 0.8.0 in 2024 added iterative index scans, which keep scanning the index until enough rows pass the filter. PostgreSQL's planner can also choose a plain index on the metadata and an exact scan when the filter is selective, which is pre-filtering. Dedicated stores took the in-graph route: Qdrant adds extra graph links for indexed payload fields so filtered walks stay connected, and the ACORN method (SIGMOD 2024) for predicate-agnostic filtered HNSW search was adopted by Weaviate. The common thread is the trade-off in this lab: selectivity decides which strategy is right, and the database's planner needs to know it.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Post-filtering a small top k and getting fewer results than asked for.
  • Testing filters only on broad values.
  • Putting filter fields inside the text instead of typed metadata.
  • Leaving metadata fields unindexed.
  • Filtering permissions in the application after retrieval (Chapter 23).
  • Choosing a dedicated vector database before the existing database has been tried.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is the difference between pre-filtering and post-filtering in vector search?

How a strong answer sounds
Post-filtering runs the nearest-neighbour search over everything and then drops results that fail the metadata condition, so if the filter keeps 10% of the data, a top 10 holds about one match. You must over-fetch, and very selective filters still come back short. Pre-filtering applies the condition first and searches only the matching vectors, which is exact and cheap when there are few matches but expensive for broad filters. Most vector databases also filter during the graph walk, checking the condition as the search moves, which keeps recall high for most selectivities.

Deeper: Users of a multi-tenant RAG product complain that small customers get fewer and worse answers than large ones. What is going on, and how do you fix it?

How a strong answer sounds
The tenant filter is very selective for small customers. If the store post-filters an approximate search, most of the top candidates belong to other tenants and are dropped, so small tenants get a few, poorer results; large tenants pass the filter often and are fine. Confirm by measuring recall against exact filtered search per tenant size. Fixes: a store that filters during the walk or switches to exact search over the tenant's vectors when they are few; larger over-fetch as a stopgap; or partitions or collections per tenant, often a separate small index for small tenants and shared indexes with filtering for large ones. Keep the tenant check in the store, not after retrieval, so documents from other tenants never reach the prompt.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • A vector database adds storage, updates, metadata and filtering to an index.
  • Post-filtering a top 10 leaves about selectivity × 10 results; over-fetch or avoid it.
  • Filtering during the graph walk keeps recall high but slows as matches thin out.
  • For very selective filters, exact search over the matches is fastest and perfect.
  • Measure filtered recall per selectivity, not just on broad filters.
