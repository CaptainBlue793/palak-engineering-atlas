Evaluating Retrieval

Every decision in Chapters 4 to 18 relied on the same tool: a set of questions with known relevant documents, a metric, and a fair comparison. This chapter makes that tool explicit. It covers how to build a golden set and how to label relevance. It explains which metric answers which question. And it covers how to tell whether pipeline B really beats pipeline A, or only got lucky on the questions you happened to ask.

The lab scores three retrievers (BM25, dense and the hybrid) query by query on SciFact and the Atlas glossary set. It tests each comparison for significance, and measures how often an evaluation of 10, 25, 50, 100 or 200 questions would have crowned the same winner as the full set.

  • Build a golden set of questions with relevance labels
  • Choose between recall@k, nDCG and MRR
  • Compare two pipelines with paired, per-query differences
  • Use bootstrap intervals and permutation tests
  • Estimate how many questions an evaluation needs

Golden questions

A golden set is a list of realistic questions, each with the documents or chunks that answer it. Start with real questions from logs, support tickets or the people the system is for, not ones invented by the team who built the index. Cover the kinds of question users ask:

  • short terms and long questions;
  • exact identifiers and paraphrases;
  • common topics and rare ones.

Version the set and keep it separate from anything used for tuning. Chapter 21 covers building and maintaining one.

Labelling relevant chunks

Label at the level the pipeline retrieves. If it returns chunks, a document-level label cannot tell a good chunk from a bad one in the same file. Labels are almost always incomplete. SciFact has about 1.1 relevant abstracts per claim, and the Atlas set marks one defining chapter per term, although other chapters often explain the same idea. Unlabelled relevant results count as misses, so scores are underestimates. Two systems that find different unlabelled relevant documents can be ranked unfairly. Pool the top results of every system you compare and label those too, as the TREC evaluations do. Grade relevance (0, 1, 2) when partial answers matter.

recall@k and nDCG

Pick the metric that matches how results are used:

  • recall@k asks whether the relevant item is anywhere in the first k. It suits a first stage that feeds a reranker or a prompt of k chunks.
  • MRR rewards putting the first relevant item high.
  • nDCG@k rewards ranking every relevant item high, with graded relevance.

Report recall at the k you actually use, and look at the whole curve. In the lab, BM25 beat dense retrieval at every k on the Atlas set, while the hybrid's lead over BM25 was largest around k = 3 to 5.

d = scores_b - scores_a                                   # per-query differences, same questions
boot = [d[rng.integers(0, n, n)].mean() for _ in range(2000)]
ci = np.percentile(boot, [2.5, 97.5])                        # paired bootstrap interval
flips = rng.choice([-1, 1], size=(5000, n))
p = np.mean(np.abs((flips * d).mean(1)) >= abs(d.mean()))      # permutation test: swap labels at random

Comparing pipelines

Compare pipelines on the same questions and look at the per-query differences, not two separate averages. A paired comparison removes the noise of easy and hard questions, because each question is its own control. Count how many queries got better and how many got worse, and read examples of each. In the lab, the hybrid beat BM25 on the Atlas set on average and significantly, yet it was better on 298 terms and worse on 303. Its wins were simply larger than its losses. An average hides that; a list of losses shows it.

Significance

Two tools cover most needs. A paired bootstrap resamples queries with replacement and reports an interval for the mean difference. If it excludes zero, the difference is unlikely to be luck in which questions were asked. A permutation test randomly swaps which pipeline each query's score belongs to, and asks how often a difference as large as yours appears by chance. Neither tells you whether a difference matters. A significant +0.01 may not be worth a slower pipeline, and a non-significant +0.03 on 50 questions may deserve a bigger test. The lab's sample-size column is the practical warning: with a few dozen questions, close calls are coin flips.

Use case: TREC and the Cranfield paradigm

The way this chapter evaluates retrieval (a fixed document collection, a set of topics, relevance judgements and a metric) is called the Cranfield paradigm, after experiments on indexing aeronautics papers led by Cyril Cleverdon at the College of Aeronautics, Cranfield, in the 1960s. The US National Institute of Standards and Technology scaled it up from 1992 with TREC, the Text REtrieval Conference. TREC solved the incomplete-labels problem by pooling: every participating system submits its top results, and assessors judge the union. Studies of TREC data by Ellen Voorhees, Chris Buckley and others showed that around 50 topics are needed for stable system rankings, and that close differences between systems often do not survive a change of topics. BEIR and MS MARCO, used throughout this course, are descendants of that work.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Tuning and reporting on the same questions.
  • Comparing averages from different question sets.
  • Declaring a winner from 20 questions.
  • Using a metric that does not match the pipeline.
  • Treating unlabelled results as wrong without pooling.
  • Never reading the per-query losses.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How do you evaluate the retrieval part of a RAG system?

How a strong answer sounds
Build a golden set: real user questions, each labelled with the chunks that answer it, ideally pooled from several retrievers so labels are not biased to one. Run each pipeline over the set and compute per-query metrics: recall@k at the k the prompt or reranker uses, plus nDCG or MRR for ranking quality. Compare pipelines on the same questions using per-query differences, with a bootstrap interval or permutation test, and look at which questions got better or worse. Keep the set versioned and separate from anything used for tuning.

Deeper: A teammate shows that a new retriever beats the old one by 0.02 nDCG on 40 hand-picked questions. Do you ship it?

How a strong answer sounds
Not yet. Forty questions is small: in testing, a set of 25 to 50 named the same winner as 300 questions only 60 to 70% of the time for differences of this size. Hand-picked questions may also be biased towards the new system. Ask for a paired comparison with a bootstrap interval or permutation test on a larger, representative set, labelled by pooling both systems' results. Look at wins and losses per question type, and weigh the gain against latency and cost. If the interval includes zero, the honest conclusion is "no detectable difference", and the decision rests on cost.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • A golden set of real, labelled questions underpins every retrieval decision.
  • Labels are incomplete: pool results from every system you compare.
  • Use the metric and k that match how results are consumed.
  • Compare pipelines per query, with intervals and wins/losses, not two averages.
  • Small evaluation sets often pick the wrong winner for close calls.
