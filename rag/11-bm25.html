Keyword Retrieval with BM25

BM25 is the keyword ranking function behind Lucene, Elasticsearch, OpenSearch and most search boxes, and it is the baseline every new retriever is compared with. It is TF-IDF with two fixes, each controlled by one parameter: repeating a word should help less and less (k1), and long documents should not win just by being long (b). It needs no model and no GPU, it is hard to beat on some kinds of query, and it is one half of the hybrid search in Chapter 12.

This chapter derives BM25 from the TF-IDF of P7 and measures what its parameters do: 25 combinations of k1 and b, on SciFact's 300 scientific claims and on the Atlas corpus's 1,922 glossary queries, against TF-IDF.

From TF-IDF to BM25

TF-IDF scores a document by summing, over query terms, a term-frequency weight times the term's rarity. BM25, from Stephen Robertson and colleagues' Okapi system in the 1990s, keeps that shape and changes both parts. Its IDF, log(1 + (N − df + 0.5) / (df + 0.5)), comes from a probabilistic model of relevance and behaves much like TF-IDF's. Its term-frequency part is the important change:

def bm25_term(tf: int, doc_len: int, avg_len: float, df: int, N: int, k1: float = 1.2, b: float = 0.75) -> float:
    idf = math.log(1 + (N - df + 0.5) / (df + 0.5))
    norm = k1 * (1 - b + b * doc_len / avg_len)            # b: how much length counts
    return idf * tf * (k1 + 1) / (tf + norm)               # k1: how fast repetition saturates

score = sum(bm25_term(tf[d][t], length[d], avg_len, df[t], N) for t in query_terms if t in tf[d])

Saturation: k1

With raw term frequency, a document that repeats "cache" 40 times scores ten times one that says it 4 times, though it is hardly ten times as relevant. BM25's tf × (k1 + 1) / (tf + k1) rises quickly and then levels off, approaching k1 + 1. A small k1 saturates almost at once, so a term's presence is nearly all that counts; a large k1 keeps rewarding repetition, approaching raw counts. k1 = 0 is the extreme: term frequency is ignored entirely, leaving only which terms are present and how rare they are. Typical values are 1.2 to 2.0.

Length normalisation: b

Long documents contain more of every word, so with no correction they win. BM25 scales k1 by (1 − b + b × length / average length): with b = 0 length is ignored; with b = 1 term frequency is fully normalised by length, so a long document needs proportionally more occurrences to score the same. The default 0.75 is a compromise. The right value depends on whether long documents in your corpus are long because they cover more (and deserve some credit) or because they ramble (and deserve none). That is a question to measure, not to guess.

Things to try.

  • k1 = 1.2, b = 0.75, the usual default: SciFact nDCG@10 0.663 against TF-IDF's 0.629, and Atlas recall@5 0.853 against 0.835. BM25 wins on both without any tuning.
  • k1 = 0: every b gives the same result, because there is no term frequency left to normalise. On the Atlas corpus recall@5 falls to 0.599: how often a term appears does carry information.
  • b from 0 to 1 on the Atlas corpus: the first result's median length falls from 625 to 155 indexed terms, and recall peaks in between, at b = 0.25 for k1 = 1.2 (0.860).
  • The best settings differ by corpus: SciFact's best is k1 = 1.2, b = 1 (0.669), the Atlas's is k1 = 0.5, b = 0.25 (0.863). The gains over the default are small, a few hundredths, which is typical: tune last, and only with a labelled set.

Where BM25 wins

BM25 is strong exactly where embeddings are weak: exact identifiers, error codes and product numbers; names and rare technical terms the embedding model never saw; jargon and new vocabulary in your domain; and queries whose important word is rare in the corpus. On BEIR, the benchmark SciFact comes from, BM25 remained competitive with or better than many neural retrievers on several datasets when the benchmark was published in 2021, especially outside the domains those retrievers were trained on. It loses on paraphrase and synonyms, where the query and the document use different words for the same idea. Chapter 12 measures the two side by side and combines them.

Analysers and stemming

In Lucene-based engines the tokenising pipeline is called an analyser: a tokeniser plus filters for lowercasing, stop words, ASCII folding (é to e), synonyms and stemming. Stemming ("connections", "connected" → "connect") improves recall for natural-language queries and hurts precision for exact terms; English stemmers such as Porter or Snowball are standard choices, and lemmatisers are gentler. Whatever you choose, apply the same analyser to documents and queries, keep identifiers intact (a separate keyword field is common), and measure on your own queries before and after.

Use case: Okapi BM25 at TREC

BM25 came out of the Okapi retrieval system at City University London, where Stephen Robertson, Steve Walker and colleagues tested a series of ranking functions derived from the probabilistic relevance framework in the early TREC evaluations of the 1990s (P8 described how TREC works). The function they called BM25, "best match" number 25, performed strongly at TREC-3 in 1994 and became the standard baseline of information retrieval research for the next three decades. Its success was practical: two interpretable parameters, robust defaults, no training data and fast evaluation over an inverted index. In 2016 Lucene made BM25 its default similarity in place of its classic TF-IDF formula, which carried it into Elasticsearch, OpenSearch and Solr. When dense retrievers arrived, BM25 was the bar they had to clear, and on out-of-domain data it often still clears them.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Dropping keyword retrieval once embeddings are added.
  • Tuning k1 and b without a labelled evaluation set.
  • Different analysers for indexing and querying.
  • Stemming identifier fields, so codes no longer match exactly.
  • Comparing BM25 scores across queries or indexes: they are only meaningful within one ranking.
  • Treating a tiny tuning gain as real without checking it on held-out queries.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How does BM25 differ from TF-IDF?

How a strong answer sounds
Both score a document by summing, over query terms, a term-frequency part times an inverse-document-frequency part. BM25 changes the term-frequency part in two ways. It saturates: each additional occurrence helps less, controlled by k1, so repeating a word forty times does not make a document ten times as relevant. And it normalises for document length, controlled by b, so long documents do not win simply by containing more words. The defaults, k1 around 1.2 and b around 0.75, work well, and in practice BM25 is a strong, cheap baseline, especially for exact terms and identifiers.

Deeper: Your team wants to replace BM25 with an embedding model for a technical documentation search. What do you advise?

How a strong answer sounds
Measure before replacing anything. Build a labelled set from real queries, including identifiers, error messages and API names as well as natural-language questions, and compare BM25, the embedding model and a hybrid on recall@k and nDCG. Typically embeddings win on paraphrased questions and lose on exact strings and new jargon, and a hybrid with rank fusion beats both, so I would expect to keep BM25 as one leg of hybrid retrieval rather than remove it. I would also check that the analyser keeps code identifiers intact, and look at per-query wins and losses before deciding.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • BM25 is TF-IDF with saturating term frequency (k1) and length normalisation (b).
  • The defaults k1 ≈ 1.2, b ≈ 0.75 beat TF-IDF on both corpora with no tuning.
  • Tuning gains were small and corpus-specific: tune last, on a labelled set.
  • BM25 shines on identifiers, names and rare terms; it misses paraphrase.
  • Use the same analyser for documents and queries, and keep identifiers intact.
