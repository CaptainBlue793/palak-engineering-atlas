Fine-Tuning Embeddings & Rerankers

General-purpose embedding models are trained on web text and question–answer pairs. Your domain has its own vocabulary, abbreviations and notion of what is relevant. Fine-tuning teaches a small model your corpus's version of "these two belong together", with training pairs you can often mine from data you already have. It is cheaper than it sounds: the lab's runs took minutes on a laptop GPU. Its risks are overfitting to the training questions and forgetting general ability.

The lab fine-tunes bge-small on 1,345 pairs of Atlas glossary terms and their defining passages, with and without mined hard negatives. It tests on 577 terms it never saw, and on SciFact to check what general ability is lost.

  • Decide when fine-tuning a retriever is worth it
  • Build training pairs from data you already have
  • Mine hard negatives and know their risk
  • Train a small embedding model with a contrastive loss
  • Measure the gain on held-out data and the loss elsewhere

When to fine-tune

Fine-tune when retrieval is the measured bottleneck (Chapter 37), when cheaper fixes have been tried, and when you have, or can mine, a few thousand relevant pairs. Cheaper fixes include hybrid search, better chunking and query handling. Fine-tuning is a strong lever for specialised vocabulary: product codes, internal acronyms, legal or medical terms the base model never saw. It is a weak lever when questions are already well served, or when the corpus changes faster than you can retrain.

Training pairs

Each training example is an anchor (a query) and a positive (a passage that answers it). Good sources:

  • search logs with clicks;
  • support tickets linked to the articles that resolved them;
  • FAQ pages;
  • titles with their sections;
  • questions generated from passages, filtered for quality (Chapter 21).

The lab used each glossary term as the query and, as the positive, the chunk of its defining chapter closest to its definition. Split by query, so no test term is ever trained on, and keep a general benchmark to check what the model forgets.

model = SentenceTransformer("BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5")
train = Dataset.from_list([{"anchor": QP + term, "positive": chunk, "negative": hard_negative} for ...])
loss = losses.MultipleNegativesRankingLoss(model)            # every other positive in the batch is a negative too
args = SentenceTransformerTrainingArguments(num_train_epochs=3, per_device_train_batch_size=32, learning_rate=2e-5, fp16=True)
SentenceTransformerTrainer(model=model, args=args, train_dataset=train, loss=loss).train()

Hard negatives

With in-batch negatives, every other example's positive serves as a negative. These are mostly easy, unrelated passages. Hard negatives are passages the current model ranks high but that are wrong, mined from its own top results. They teach finer distinctions. Their risk is false negatives, relevant passages wrongly labelled as wrong. The lab excluded every chunk from the term's defining chapter when mining, but other chapters that also explain a term remain a risk. Hard negatives also make each training step heavier: here a step took several times longer.

Training a small model

Small embedding models fine-tune quickly on one consumer GPU. Practical settings:

  • a low learning rate (around 2e-5) and a few epochs;
  • the largest batch that fits, since in-batch negatives improve with batch size;
  • half precision;
  • the same query prefix in training and use.

Keep the base model's tokenizer and pooling. Save checkpoints and evaluate each one, and remember that re-embedding the corpus with the new model is part of the cost (Chapter 22). Rerankers fine-tune the same way, as binary relevance classifiers on (query, passage, label) triples, with your hard negatives as the zeros.

Measuring the gain

Measure on held-out queries that resemble real traffic, with a paired comparison and intervals (Chapter 19). Also measure on a general benchmark you care about, to see what was forgotten, and on the training sample, to see how much was memorised. Then decide with the full cost in view: training is minutes, but re-embedding, a new index version and a rollout plan come with every retrained model.

Use case: Sentence-BERT and in-batch negatives

In 2019 Nils Reimers and Iryna Gurevych published Sentence-BERT, which fine-tuned BERT in a siamese set-up so that sentence embeddings could be compared with cosine similarity, and released the sentence-transformers library used throughout this course. Its MultipleNegativesRankingLoss builds on the idea, from Matthew Henderson and colleagues' 2017 work on email reply suggestion at Google, of using every other example in a batch as a negative. That makes training on plain (query, positive) pairs effective and cheap. Dense Passage Retrieval (Vladimir Karpukhin and colleagues, 2020) then showed that adding one mined hard negative per pair, from BM25, improved open-domain question answering markedly. The lab follows the same recipe on a laptop: pairs, in-batch negatives, optional hard negatives, and a held-out test.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Testing on training queries.
  • Splitting by passage instead of by query.
  • False hard negatives.
  • Forgetting the general case.
  • Changing the query prefix.
  • Not re-embedding the corpus with the new model.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How would you fine-tune an embedding model for your company's documents?

How a strong answer sounds
First confirm retrieval is the bottleneck and cheaper fixes are done. Mine query–passage pairs from data we have: search clicks, tickets linked to articles, FAQ, titles with sections, or filtered synthetic questions. Split by query into train and test. Train a small model with a contrastive loss such as multiple-negatives ranking, where other in-batch passages act as negatives, optionally adding mined hard negatives while guarding against false negatives. Evaluate on held-out queries with intervals and on a general benchmark for forgetting, then re-embed the corpus as a new index version with a rollback plan.

Deeper: Your fine-tuned embedder scores much better on its evaluation but users see no improvement. What went wrong?

How a strong answer sounds
Likely leakage or mismatch. If evaluation queries overlap the training queries, or were generated the same way from the same passages, the score measures memorisation: and only a query-level held-out split tells the two apart. The evaluation set may not resemble real traffic (synthetic questions borrow the passage's words). Or the deployed index was not re-embedded with the new model, or the query prefix differs between training and serving. Check the split, evaluate on real held-out queries, confirm the index and serving path use the new model consistently, and look at per-query wins and losses.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Fine-tune when retrieval is the bottleneck and vocabulary is specialised.
  • Mine pairs from existing data; split by query; keep a general benchmark.
  • In-batch negatives are cheap; hard negatives cost more and can be false.
  • Measure held-out gain, training-set memorisation and general-domain forgetting.
  • Deploying means re-embedding the corpus as a new index version.
