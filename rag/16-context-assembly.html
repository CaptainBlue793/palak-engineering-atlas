Context Assembly: Order, Size & Lost in the Middle

Between retrieval and generation sits a step that is easy to skip: deciding exactly what goes into the prompt. How many chunks, in what order, whether near-copies are removed, and whether long chunks are trimmed to their useful sentences. Chapter 10 showed that the right chunk's position and the number of chunks changed whether a small model used it. This chapter measures those effects more finely and tests the usual remedies.

The lab reuses Chapter 10's setup on 100 questions, with exactly one source holding the answer. It moves that source through 20 positions, repeats the question at both ends of the prompt, and compresses each source to its two most relevant sentences. It then measures how often retrieval fills the top 10 with near-duplicates, and what diversifying them does.

  • Choose how many chunks to assemble for a given model and budget
  • Explain and measure "lost in the middle" for your own model
  • Order chunks to reduce position effects
  • Remove near-duplicates and diversify with MMR
  • Compress context extractively, and check what it costs

How many chunks

More chunks raise the chance that the answer is in the prompt (retrieval recall@k, Chapter 19). They also spend tokens, add prefill time, and give a small model more to be distracted by. The right number is where those curves cross for your model, and it is usually smaller than the context window allows. In Chapter 10, going from 10 to 20 sources more than halved how often this model cited the right one. Here, with 20 sources, about 3,568 prompt tokens, the answer's position mattered as much as its presence.

Order effects

Pipelines usually paste chunks best first. Because the model reads a long prompt unevenly, the order decides which chunk gets attention. Two common strategies are best first, and best at both ends, which alternates the top-ranked chunks between the start and the end so the weakest sit in the middle. Repeating the question before and after the sources is another cheap remedy. Both should be measured on your own model; the lab measured the repeated question.

def both_ends(chunks):                     # ranked best first → best at the edges, weakest in the middle
    front, back = [], []
    for i, c in enumerate(chunks):
        (front if i % 2 == 0 else back).append(c)
    return front + back[::-1]

prompt = f"Question: {q}\n\nSources:\n{render(both_ends(chunks))}\n\nQuestion: {q}"   # question at both ends

Lost in the middle

Liu and colleagues (Chapter 10's use case) found that accuracy forms a U over the answer's position, high at the ends of a long context and low in the middle. The shape varies by model. For this 3B model and 20 sources, the right source was cited 41 times out of 100 at position 1, 26 at 5, 8 at 10, 6 at 15 and 3 at 20. The curve falls almost steadily from the first position to the last, with no recovery at the end: for this model the start of the prompt is the only place it reliably looks. With the question placed after the sources, the last source sits right next to it and still did worst, so recency did not help.

Things to try.

  • 1st → 20th: citations of the right source go 41 → 26 → 8 → 6 → 3 out of 100.
  • 10th vs 10th with the question at both ends: 8 against 34. Putting the question before the sources as well as after them was the largest single improvement in the lab, and answers without any citation fell from 71 to 19. Wrong citations rose too, from 14 to 33: the model now always tried to cite, and did not always pick the right source.
  • 10 sources, full vs compressed: prompts shrink from about 1,818 to 915 tokens, and right citations go from 56 to 57. Half the tokens and 1.2 s instead of 1.9 s per answer for the same accuracy, but wrong citations rose from 13 to 21: with less context around each claim, the model attributed more freely.
  • Top 10: similarity vs MMR: 22% of terms had at least one near-duplicate pair in their top 10. MMR with λ 0.7 cut that to 3.5% and raised the distinct chapters in the top 10 from 5.1 to 8.1, for a recall@5 of 68.0% instead of 70.5%. λ 0.5 removed every duplicate but recall@5 fell to 63.7%.

Deduplication

Retrieval returns whatever is most similar, and similar chunks are often similar to each other. Examples are the same paragraph quoted in two chapters, a glossary entry and the chapter it came from, or overlapping chunk windows. Near-duplicates waste the token budget and can make one source look more authoritative than it is. Remove exact copies by hash at indexing time (Chapter 22). Remove near-copies at query time by dropping candidates too similar to one already kept, or with maximal marginal relevance (MMR). MMR picks each next chunk by λ·relevance − (1 − λ)·similarity to those already chosen.

Context compression

Compression shrinks what is sent. Extractive methods keep the sentences most relevant to the question. Abstractive methods ask a model to summarise each chunk, which costs a model call and risks losing or altering facts. Token-dropping methods such as LLMLingua remove words a small model predicts are unimportant. Every compression method risks dropping the sentence that mattered, so measure answer quality, not just tokens saved.

Use case: MMR, from 1998 search results to RAG context

Maximal marginal relevance was introduced by Jaime Carbonell and Jade Goldstein at SIGIR 1998, in "The Use of MMR, Diversity-Based Reranking for Reordering Documents and Producing Summaries". Their problem was search result lists and multi-document summaries full of near-identical items. Their answer was to pick each next item for its relevance minus its redundancy with what was already picked, weighted by one parameter. The same idea now sits in the retrievers of LangChain, LlamaIndex and several vector databases as a "diversity" or MMR search option, because RAG has exactly their problem: a top-k that spends its budget on one fact said several ways. As the lab shows, it is a trade. Diversity bought at too low a λ pushes out relevant chunks.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Filling the context window because it is there.
  • Assuming one model's position curve fits another.
  • No deduplication.
  • Compressing without measuring answers.
  • Setting MMR's λ too low.
  • Splitting the question from the sources across distant parts of a long prompt.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is "lost in the middle", and what do you do about it?

How a strong answer sounds
Language models use information unevenly across a long prompt: research found accuracy highest when the relevant passage is at the start or end and lowest in the middle, and the exact shape varies by model. In RAG it means a correct chunk can be ignored because of where it sits. Mitigations: send fewer, better chunks after reranking; put the strongest chunks at the edges; repeat the question before and after the sources; remove duplicates so good chunks are not pushed into the middle; and measure accuracy by position on your own model rather than assuming a curve.

Deeper: Your RAG system sends 20 chunks, and answers are slow and often ignore the best source. How would you redesign context assembly?

How a strong answer sounds
Measure first: recall@k of retrieval to see how many chunks are needed to include the answer, and answer accuracy by the right chunk's position and by k. Then rerank the candidates and keep the smallest k that holds most answers, often 3 to 8. Remove near-duplicates or use MMR with a high λ. Order best first or best at both ends, and put the question at both ends. Optionally compress extractively, checking that accuracy holds. Cap tokens with a budget, set the context length explicitly, and re-measure latency, since prefill time grows with every chunk.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Fewer, better chunks beat filling the window, for small models especially.
  • Position effects are real and model-specific: measure your own curve.
  • Repeating the question and ordering best at the edges are cheap remedies to test.
  • Deduplicate by hash at indexing and by similarity or MMR at query time.
  • Compression saves tokens; check it does not drop the sentence that mattered.
