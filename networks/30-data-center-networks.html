Data-Centre Networks: Clos & Leaf–Spine

A large data centre holds a hundred thousand servers, and any one of them may need to talk to any other at ten or a hundred gigabits a second. No switch that big exists. For years the answer was a tree, with ever larger and more expensive switches towards the root, and it failed as applications changed: most traffic stopped flowing in and out to users and started flowing sideways, between servers. The design that replaced it comes from a 1953 paper about telephone exchanges. It builds an enormous network from many small, identical, cheap switches, wired so that there are always many equally good paths. Every big cloud is built this way, and the same pattern explains two things you meet from a long way up the stack: why a rack is a failure domain, and why a traceroute between two of your servers shows a different path each time.

Three-tier and leaf–spine

The classic design was a tree in three layers. Servers plugged into access switches, those into a pair of large aggregation switches, and those into a pair of very large core routers. It suited the traffic of its time, which went north–south: a request came in from the internet, went down the tree to a server, and the answer went back up.

Modern applications don't behave like that. One page view fans out into hundreds of calls between internal services, each of which reads from a cache, a database and a storage system that are themselves replicating to their peers. Most of the bytes in a data centre now travel east–west, from server to server. In a tree, two servers in different rows can only reach each other by climbing to the aggregation or core layer and coming down again, through a pair of switches that every other row is also using.

Clos networks

In 1953 Charles Clos, at Bell Labs, showed how to build a telephone exchange of any size out of small switching elements arranged in stages, such that a free path could always be found. The data-centre version folds his design in half and has two kinds of switch:

  • Leaf switches sit at the top of each rack. Every server in the rack plugs into its leaf.
  • Spine switches connect the leaves. Every leaf has one link to every spine. Leaves don't connect to each other, and neither do spines.

That one wiring rule gives the fabric its properties. Any two servers in different racks are exactly three switches apart, so latency is uniform and predictable. Between any two racks there are as many paths as there are spines, all the same length. To add servers, add a rack and its leaf. To add capacity between racks, add a spine. When the spines run out of ports, the pattern repeats with another layer on top, and the largest fabrics have five stages and hundreds of thousands of ports, still made of the same small switches.

ECMP

The fabric is routed at layer 3 all the way down to the leaf. Each leaf's routing table (Chapter 5) therefore holds, for every other rack's subnet, not one next hop but several of equal cost, one through each spine:

leaf1# show ip route 10.4.7.0/24
10.4.7.0/24  [20/0]  via 10.255.0.1, Ethernet1/49     # spine 1
                     via 10.255.0.2, Ethernet1/50     # spine 2
                     via 10.255.0.3, Ethernet1/51     # spine 3
                     via 10.255.0.4, Ethernet1/52     # spine 4

Using them all is equal-cost multipath routing. For each packet the switch computes a hash of the 5-tuple and uses it to pick one of the next hops:

Hashing by flow has consequences worth knowing:

  • Balance is statistical. With thousands of small flows the spines carry almost equal shares. A few very large flows, "elephants", can hash to the same uplink and overload it while its neighbours sit idle. The switch doesn't look at load.
  • Different connections take different paths. Two traceroutes between the same pair of servers can show different spines, because each probe is a different flow. A fault on one spine affects only the flows that hash to it, which shows up as "some connections are slow and others are fine".
  • Failure is handled by routing. When a spine or a link dies, its next hop is withdrawn and the flows that used it are hashed over the rest, within about a second.

Oversubscription

A leaf with 48 servers at 25 Gbit/s could, in principle, be asked to carry 1,200 Gbit/s out of the rack. Giving it that much uplink would double the cost of the fabric to cover a case that almost never happens. The ratio of what could be offered to what can be carried is the oversubscription ratio:

oversubscription  =  server-facing capacity / spine-facing capacity
                  =  (48 × 25) / (4 × 100)  =  1,200 / 400  =  3 : 1

A ratio of about 3:1 is common for general-purpose racks. Storage and machine-learning clusters, where every server really does send flat out at once, are built at 1:1, which is called non-blocking.

Servers have 25 Gbit/s links and each leaf has one 100 Gbit/s link to each spine. Rack 1 is sending 240 Gbit/s to other racks in both traffic patterns. Each flow is placed on an uplink by a fixed hash of its addresses and ports, as a switch would.

Things to notice. With four spines and 48 servers the ratio is 3:1, and 24 ordinary flows spread themselves evenly, 60 Gbit/s on each uplink. Switch to four large flows carrying the same total. Two of them hash to the same spine, and that uplink is asked for 120 Gbit/s while another carries nothing. Traffic is being dropped in a fabric that is less than two-thirds full, and no amount of spare capacity elsewhere helps those two flows. With eight spines the same four flows happen to land on different uplinks. More paths make collisions rarer and do not rule them out. Now fail a spine. With eight, an eighth of the capacity goes and nothing else changes. With two, half goes, and 240 Gbit/s no longer fits in what remains. The number of spines sets the capacity and also sets how much a single failure costs.

East–west traffic and failure domains

The shape of the fabric reaches up into how systems are designed.

A leaf switch (or the rack's power)Every server in the rack, at the same momentSpread replicas across racks. This is what "rack-aware" placement in Kafka, HDFS, Cassandra and Kubernetes means

Because every path is the same length, a service doesn't need to care which rack its dependencies are in for latency, only for failure. And because the fabric is plain IP routing, tenants' private networks are carried on top of it as overlays (Chapter 29), whose encapsulation picks a UDP source port from a hash of the inner flow, exactly so that ECMP can spread tenants' flows over the spines.

Google's Jupiter: a decade of building networks from small switches

In 2015 Google published an account of how its data-centre networks had been built since 2004. The starting point was a problem money couldn't solve. The largest switches anyone sold were too small for the clusters Google wanted, and the cost of the big ones grew far faster than their capacity. A single cluster needed a network that no vendor made. So its engineers did what Clos had described fifty years earlier for telephone exchanges. They took the small, cheap switching chips sold to every equipment maker, the kind inside an ordinary top-of-rack switch, and wired large numbers of them into a multi-stage fabric with many equal paths. The first generation carried 10,000 servers at 1 Gbit/s. The fifth, named Jupiter, connected more than 100,000 servers and could carry over a petabit per second across its middle, a hundred times the capacity of the first, from switches of essentially the same kind. The switches were the easy part, and the hard part was control. Thousands of identical boxes can't each be configured by hand or left to run a routing protocol designed for a few dozen routers, so Google ran the fabric from central software that knew the whole topology (Chapter 39). Facebook, Microsoft and Amazon published similar designs in the same years. Since then the large data-centre network has been a matter of many small parts and the software that coordinates them, with no single remarkable machine in it.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Assuming ECMP balances load. It balances flows, blindly. A few huge flows can collide while other links are idle.
  • One long-lived connection for bulk transfer. It is one flow on one path. Several parallel connections spread over the fabric.
  • All replicas in one rack. One leaf switch or power feed takes them all. Spread across racks and zones.
  • Reading a traceroute as "the" path. Each probe may hash differently. Tools that hold the flow constant show one consistent path.
  • Too few spines. With two, a single failure halves the fabric's capacity.
  • Stretching layer 2 across the fabric. Loops, storms and blocked links come with it. Route to the leaf and use overlays where layer 2 is needed.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is a leaf–spine network, and why do data centres use it?

How a strong answer sounds
It is a two-layer network, a folded Clos topology. Leaf switches sit at the top of each rack and connect the servers. Spine switches connect the leaves, and every leaf has a link to every spine, with no leaf-to-leaf or spine-to-spine links. So any two servers in different racks are always three hops apart, leaf, spine, leaf, with as many equal-cost paths as there are spines. Data centres use it because most of their traffic is east–west, between servers, and the older three-tier tree forced that traffic up through a pair of large aggregation switches that were both a bottleneck and a large failure domain, with half the links disabled by spanning tree. Leaf–spine uses every link through layer-3 routing with equal-cost multipath, gives uniform latency, scales out by adding identical spines for capacity or leaves for ports, and degrades gracefully: losing one of eight spines loses an eighth of the capacity. It is built from small commodity switches and not a few very large ones.

Deeper: A large data transfer between two racks is getting far less throughput than the fabric's capacity suggests, while the network overall is lightly loaded. What might be happening?

How a strong answer sounds
ECMP assigns traffic to paths per flow, by hashing the 5-tuple, and takes no account of flow size or current load. A single TCP connection is one flow, so it uses exactly one uplink, one spine and one downlink, and is limited to that link's capacity, shared with whatever else hashed there. If two or three large flows hash to the same uplink, they congest it, with drops and TCP backing off, while parallel links sit nearly idle. That matches "low overall utilisation, poor throughput for this transfer". I'd check per-interface utilisation and drop counters on the source leaf's uplinks for one hot link, and retransmissions on the sender. Mitigations: split the transfer over many parallel connections with different source ports, so that they hash across the paths; make sure the hash uses ports and isn't polarised by using the same function at every tier; and for elephant-heavy workloads use flowlet switching or a scheduler that places large flows deliberately. I'd also check the plain explanations, such as a degraded link, a 10-gigabit server NIC or an oversubscribed leaf.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Data-centre traffic is mostly east–west, which a three-tier tree handles badly and expensively.
  • In a leaf–spine fabric every leaf connects to every spine. Any two racks are three switch hops apart, by as many paths as there are spines.
  • The fabric scales out with small identical switches: add spines for capacity, leaves for ports, another stage for size.
  • ECMP hashes each flow's 5-tuple onto one of the equal paths. It keeps a flow's packets in order and can't see load, so large flows can collide.
  • Oversubscription is server-facing capacity over spine-facing capacity. About 3:1 is usual and 1:1 is non-blocking.
  • A leaf failure takes a rack and a spine failure takes a share of capacity, which is why replicas are spread across racks.

Check your understanding

In a two-tier leaf–spine fabric, how many switches does a packet cross between servers in two different racks?

Every leaf reaches every other through any one spine, so the path length is always the same.

Why does ECMP choose a path per flow and not per packet?

Per-packet spraying balances better and wrecks TCP. Hashing the 5-tuple keeps each connection on one path.

A leaf has 48 servers on 25 Gbit/s links and four 100 Gbit/s uplinks. What is its oversubscription ratio?

1,200 Gbit/s of server-facing capacity over 400 Gbit/s of uplink.

One of four spine switches fails. What happens?

The failed next hop is withdrawn and its flows are rehashed over the remaining spines.

Two 60 Gbit/s flows hash to the same 100 Gbit/s uplink while three other uplinks are almost idle. What does ECMP do about it?

The congested link drops packets and TCP backs off. Fixing it takes more, smaller flows or a smarter placement.
