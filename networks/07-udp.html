UDP

UDP adds almost nothing to IP: two port numbers, a length and a checksum. No connection, no handshake, no acknowledgements, no retransmission, no ordering, no flow or congestion control. That sounds like a list of defects until you look at who chooses it: DNS, voice and video calls, online games, metrics pipelines, and HTTP/3. For all of them, something TCP does would get in the way, usually waiting. This chapter covers what UDP gives you, what it leaves to you, and how to tell when "unreliable" is the right choice.

Eight bytes of header

The whole UDP header (RFC 768, 1980, three pages long) is four 16-bit fields:

Source port16 bitsDestination port16 bits Length16 bits: header + dataChecksum16 bits Dataone application message, up to 65,507 bytes (keep it under ~1,200)

The ports pick the socket, as with TCP. The length covers header and data. The checksum covers the header, the data and a "pseudo-header" of the IP addresses, so a datagram delivered to the wrong host is detected; it's optional in IPv4 (zero means "not computed") and mandatory in IPv6. That's all. Compare TCP's 20 to 60 bytes of header and the state machine behind them (Chapter 8).

What datagrams do and don't promise

  • Boundaries are kept. One sendto() becomes one datagram, and one recvfrom() returns exactly one datagram, whole. Unlike TCP, no framing is needed.
  • Delivery isn't promised. A datagram can be lost (queue overflow, a bad checksum, a firewall) and nobody is told.
  • Order isn't promised. Datagrams on different paths can arrive out of order.
  • Duplicates are possible, rarely, if something below retransmits.
  • No connection state. The server doesn't know a client exists until a datagram arrives, and one socket can talk to thousands of peers with sendto/recvfrom.
  • No congestion control. UDP sends as fast as the application asks. A careless UDP sender can flood a link and starve TCP flows that politely back off (Chapter 13).

So a UDP application takes on the jobs it needs. Typical additions are a sequence number in each message (to detect loss and reorder), timeouts and retries for request–response, and rate limiting. RTP, used for voice and video, adds a 12-byte header with a sequence number and a timestamp on top of UDP. QUIC goes all the way and reimplements reliability, streams and congestion control on top of UDP (Chapter 18).

# A metrics client in the statsd style: fire and forget, one counter per datagram
import socket

sock = socket.socket(socket.AF_INET, socket.SOCK_DGRAM)
def incr(name: str, n: int = 1) -> None:
    sock.sendto(f"{name}:{n}|c".encode("ascii"), ("127.0.0.1", 8125))   # never blocks on the network

incr("api.requests")        # if the agent is down, the metric is lost and the request isn't slowed

$ ss -ulnp                                   # UDP sockets: no LISTEN/ESTAB states, just "UNCONN"

The metrics example shows the design choice clearly. Losing one counter increment occasionally costs nothing; making every API request wait for a TCP connection to a metrics agent, or fail when the agent restarts, costs a lot.

When late is the same as lost

The strongest reason to choose UDP is real-time data. A voice call sends a 20 ms audio frame fifty times a second, and the receiver plays each one at a fixed moment, a little behind real time to absorb jitter (the playout or jitter buffer delay, typically 40–100 ms). A frame that arrives after its play time is useless: the moment has passed. Over TCP, a single lost segment makes the receiver wait at least a round trip for the retransmission, and because TCP delivers in order, every frame behind it waits too. One loss turns into a burst of late audio, which sounds worse than the brief gap UDP would have had. The panel below shows this.

Request–response over UDP: DNS

DNS is the most common UDP conversation: a query fits in one datagram, so does the answer, and a connection handshake would double the cost of each lookup. The client handles loss itself, with a timeout and a retry, possibly to another server.

🧠
Kubernetes and the mysterious 5-second DNS lookups

In 2018 many teams running Kubernetes saw a small fraction of requests take just over 5 seconds, and the extra time was always in DNS. The cause, written up by Weaveworks and XING engineers, was a race in Linux connection tracking: glibc sends the A and AAAA queries in parallel from the same UDP socket, the two packets are NATed to the cluster DNS service at the same moment, and conntrack occasionally drops one of them while inserting the duplicate entry. UDP has no acknowledgement, so the resolver only notices when its 5-second timeout expires, and the user sees a 5-second stall. The fixes were all workarounds around UDP's silence: options single-request-reopen or use-vc (TCP) in resolv.conf, a node-local DNS cache (NodeLocal DNSCache) that avoids the NAT step, and later kernel fixes. The lesson is general: with UDP, every loss costs a full timeout, so the timeout is your tail latency.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • No timeout on a UDP receive. A lost reply hangs the program forever.
  • Large datagrams. Anything over the path MTU is fragmented, and one lost fragment loses the whole datagram. Stay under ~1,200 bytes.
  • No congestion control on a high-rate UDP sender. It will fill queues and hurt everyone else on the link.
  • Forgetting NAT timeouts. NAT and firewalls drop idle UDP mappings after 30 seconds to a few minutes; long-lived UDP flows need keep-alives (Chapter 9).
  • Using UDP "because it's faster" for bulk data. Without reliability and congestion control you'll end up rebuilding TCP, badly.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: When would you choose UDP over TCP?

How a strong answer sounds
When TCP's guarantees cost more than they're worth. Real-time media and games: late data is useless, and TCP's retransmission and in-order delivery turn one loss into a stall for everything behind it. Small request–response protocols like DNS: one datagram each way, no handshake, retry on timeout. Fire-and-forget telemetry like statsd, where losing a sample is fine and blocking the app isn't. And protocols that build their own transport on top, like QUIC, which needs to evolve faster than kernels and to avoid TCP's head-of-line blocking. The price is that the application has to handle loss, ordering and congestion itself, so for bulk transfers and ordinary APIs I'd use TCP.

Deeper: You're designing telemetry from 100,000 IoT devices over UDP. What do you have to add yourself?

How a strong answer sounds
Decide first how much loss is acceptable. Then: a sequence number per device in each message, so the server can measure loss and discard duplicates; a device ID and authentication (a MAC or DTLS), since UDP source addresses are trivially spoofed; small messages under ~1,200 bytes to avoid fragmentation; for data that must arrive, application-level acknowledgements and retries with backoff and jitter so devices don't retry in lockstep after an outage; rate limits on the device side as congestion control; and periodic keep-alives if devices sit behind NAT and must receive commands. At that point I'd also look at existing protocols (CoAP, MQTT-SN, or QUIC) rather than inventing one.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • UDP is IP plus ports, a length and a checksum: 8 bytes, no connection.
  • Datagram boundaries are kept; delivery, order and uniqueness are not.
  • Applications add what they need: timeouts and retries, sequence numbers, rate limits, or a whole transport (QUIC).
  • Real-time traffic prefers UDP because late is the same as lost, and TCP turns loss into delay for everything behind it.
  • With UDP, every loss costs a full timeout, so the timeout setting is your tail latency.

Check your understanding

How big is the UDP header?

Source port, destination port, length and checksum, 16 bits each. UDP has no options.

A sender calls sendto() three times with 100-byte messages. What can the receiver's recvfrom() calls return?

Boundaries are preserved, so each recvfrom returns one whole datagram, but any of them can be lost or reordered.

Why do voice and video calls use UDP rather than TCP?

A short gap from a lost UDP frame is concealed by the codec; a TCP stall makes a burst of frames miss their play time.

Who is responsible for congestion control in a UDP-based protocol?

UDP has none. Well-behaved UDP protocols implement it themselves; badly behaved ones flood links.

A service's p99 latency has a cluster of requests at about 5.0 seconds. What's a likely UDP-related cause?

A lost UDP datagram is only noticed when the timeout fires. glibc's default resolver timeout is 5 seconds, so lost DNS queries show up as exactly that.
