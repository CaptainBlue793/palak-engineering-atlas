DDoS & Network Attacks

A denial-of-service attack doesn't break into anything. It sends a service more work than it can do, so that the real users can't get through. "Distributed" means the traffic comes from many machines at once, which makes it both larger and harder to block. The attacks differ in what they exhaust: the capacity of a link, a table in a kernel or a firewall, or the processors of the application itself. Each one works by finding a place where a little effort by the attacker costs the victim a great deal. This chapter covers the main kinds, and the small number of defences that work against them.

The last row decides the defence, and the first column has the hardest constraint. If the link to your data centre carries 10 Gbit/s and 50 Gbit/s is sent to it, the link is full before any of your equipment sees a packet. A firewall on your side of that link can discard every attack packet perfectly and your site is still unreachable, because the legitimate packets were lost upstream in the crowd. A volumetric attack can only be dealt with by someone with more capacity than the attacker, at a point before the bottleneck.

Volumetric floods

The plainest attack is a flood of packets from a botnet, thousands or millions of compromised machines told to send as much as they can. For years these were home computers. Now they are more often cameras, routers and video recorders with default passwords, which is the subject of Chapter 45. Each device contributes only its own uplink, a few megabits a second, so the size of the flood is the size of the botnet. In 2025 the largest floods measured passed 20 Tbit/s, lasting less than a minute each.

An attacker without a large botnet can borrow other people's servers instead.

Amplification

Two properties of UDP make this possible. It has no handshake, so a server answers the first packet it receives. And nothing checks the source address in that packet (Chapter 7). An attacker sends a request to a public server with the source address forged to be the victim's. The server sends its answer to the victim. That is reflection, and it hides the attacker. If the answer is also much larger than the question, the server has multiplied the attacker's traffic. That is amplification.

The multiplier depends on the protocol, and on finding a request whose answer is as large as possible:

The multipliers are the largest reported for each protocol; real attacks average less, and the total is also limited by how many open servers exist. When more arrives than the link can carry, the link drops packets without regard to whose they are, so the share of legitimate traffic that survives is roughly the link's capacity divided by the traffic offered to it.

Things to notice. Sent directly, 25 Mbit/s is 2.5% of a 1 Gbit/s link and nobody notices. Reflected off DNS resolvers it becomes 1.35 Gbit/s, which fills that link. Off NTP servers it is 13.9 Gbit/s, and off memcached servers about 1.3 Tbit/s, which is more than a hundred times a 10 Gbit/s link and leaves under 1% of legitimate packets getting through. Buying a bigger link doesn't keep up with a multiplier of fifty thousand. Blocking forged source addresses at the attacker's own network stops every reflected attack at once, because the forged requests never leave. And a scrubbing network can remove reflected traffic easily, since it all comes from one well-known source port. For a volumetric attack the difficulty is having the capacity to receive it, and recognising it is the easy part.

🧠
GitHub, February 2018: 1.35 Tbit/s, and back within ten minutes

At 17:21 UTC on 28 February 2018, traffic to GitHub rose from its normal level to 1.35 terabits per second, 126.9 million packets a second, the largest attack then recorded. It used no botnet. It was reflected off memcached servers, a cache meant to run inside a private network, tens of thousands of which were reachable from the internet with UDP enabled by default. An attacker could store a large value in one, then send a 15-byte request for it with GitHub's address as the source, and the server would send hundreds of kilobytes to GitHub. The traffic came from more than a thousand different networks. GitHub's own links couldn't have carried it, and the engineers didn't try. Their monitoring detected the flood and, within minutes, they changed their BGP announcements so that traffic for GitHub's addresses was routed to a DDoS-mitigation provider instead. That provider's network had the capacity to receive 1.35 Tbit/s, discarded everything with a source port of 11211, and passed the remainder on. GitHub was unreachable for five minutes and intermittently reachable for four more. Within a week an attack of 1.7 Tbit/s by the same method hit another company. Within a month most of those memcached servers had been firewalled or updated to turn UDP off, and the method stopped working. The defence was a plan that existed before the attack: somebody else's capacity, and a tested way to divert traffic to it.

The proper fix for all reflection is for every network to drop outgoing packets whose source address isn't one of its own. This is called source address validation, or BCP 38, and has been recommended since 2000. Most networks do it. It only takes a few that don't, since an attacker needs just one network to send forged packets from, and a network that skips the filter suffers nothing itself.

SYN floods

A SYN flood fills a table instead of a link. Chapter 8 covered the mechanism: each SYN makes the server remember a half-open connection while it waits for the final ACK, the attacker never sends that ACK, and the queue fills so that real clients are turned away. SYN cookies defend the server by encoding the state in the sequence number, so nothing is remembered until a client proves it exists.

At the scale of a network there is more to add. The server is not the only device keeping a table. A SYN flood of a few million packets a second fills the connection-tracking table of a stateful firewall or load balancer in front of it long before the link is full (Chapter 35), and then that device drops new connections for everything behind it. The devices bought to protect a service are often the first to fail. So floods must be handled at the edge by something stateless: a filter that answers SYNs with cookies itself and only passes on connections that complete, or an anycast network that spreads the flood across many sites.

Layer-7 attacks

The third kind sends complete, valid requests. They pass every filter that works on packets, because as packets there is nothing wrong with them. What they use up is the application, and the imbalance is in the work: a request costs the sender one line of text and may cost the server a database query, a rendered page or a password hash.

  • HTTP floods. Many clients request the most expensive pages: search, login, anything that can't be served from a cache. A few thousand requests a second is a trickle as bandwidth, and enough to stop a site whose search page takes 200 ms of processor time.
  • Slow attacks. A tool such as Slowloris opens many connections and sends each request a few bytes at a time, never finishing. A server that gives each connection a thread or process (Chapter 15) runs out of them while almost no traffic flows.
  • Protocol abuse. In 2023 attackers found that an HTTP/2 client can open a stream and cancel it immediately, over and over, staying under the limit on concurrent streams while the server starts work on each one (Chapter 16). This "Rapid Reset" attack produced a peak of 398 million requests a second against Google, from a botnet of about 20,000 machines.
  • Handshake floods. A TLS handshake costs the server a private-key operation, which is far more work than the client does to ask for it (Chapter 17).

These are hard to stop because the defender must tell a real user from a program that behaves much like one, and a limit per address fails against a botnet of real home addresses.

A rate limit is usually a token bucket: each client has a bucket that is refilled at a steady rate and holds a fixed maximum, each request takes a token, and a request that finds the bucket empty is refused with status 429. The refill rate sets the sustained limit and the bucket size sets how large a burst is tolerated.

# nginx: 10 requests a second per client address, bursts of 20, on the expensive endpoints only
limit_req_zone $binary_remote_addr zone=perip:10m rate=10r/s;
location /search { limit_req zone=perip burst=20 nodelay; limit_req_status 429; }

Two rules hold across all of these. Reject as early and as cheaply as possible, since a request dropped at the edge costs nothing and one refused by the application has already cost a connection and a thread. And hide the origin: if the real address of your servers can be found, an attacker sends the flood there directly and goes round the protection.

Common mistakes
  • Planning to filter a volumetric attack on your own equipment. The link is full before your equipment is reached.
  • Putting a stateful device at the very edge. Its connection table is the easiest thing to exhaust.
  • Leaving the origin's address discoverable in old DNS records, mail headers or certificates, so the attack goes round the CDN.
  • Running a UDP service open to the internet without need. An open resolver, NTP server or cache is a weapon for use against others.
  • Rate limiting only by IP address. It blocks an office behind one address and misses a botnet of ten thousand.
  • Meeting the mitigation provider for the first time during the attack. Diversion has to be set up and tested beforehand.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is a DNS amplification attack?

How a strong answer sounds
It is a reflected volumetric attack. The attacker sends DNS queries over UDP to many open resolvers, with the source IP forged to be the victim's. Because UDP is connectionless there is no handshake to verify the source, so each resolver sends its response to the victim. The attacker chooses queries that produce very large responses, for example a record set of several kilobytes for a 60-byte query, so the traffic is amplified by up to around fifty times: a few megabits from the attacker become gigabits at the victim. The victim sees traffic from thousands of legitimate resolvers and never the attacker's address. It depends on two things: networks that let packets with forged source addresses out, and servers that answer anyone. The fixes are correspondingly source address validation at network edges, BCP 38, and not running open resolvers, or rate limiting responses. For the victim, the traffic is easy to identify, UDP from source port 53 that it never asked for, but it has to be filtered upstream by a network with enough capacity, because by the time it reaches the victim's own link the link is already full.

Deeper: Your public API is being attacked. How do you work out what kind of attack it is, and what do you do about each kind?

How a strong answer sounds
I'd classify it by which resource is saturated. If inbound bits per second at the edge are far above normal and links are full, it is volumetric, typically UDP reflection; nothing on my side of the link can help, so I'd divert to a scrubbing provider or rely on an anycast CDN in front, and ask upstream to filter, with black-holing the target address as a last resort to protect everything else. If bandwidth is moderate but packets per second are very high, with SYN counters rising, SYN cookies being sent, or a firewall's or load balancer's connection table full, it is a protocol attack: make sure SYN cookies are on, and get the filtering done at a stateless edge in front of the stateful devices. If network metrics look nearly normal but request rate, CPU, thread pools or database load are saturated, it is layer 7: I'd look at what the requests have in common, the paths, user agents, TLS fingerprints, networks of origin, and apply rate limits and challenges at the edge on those attributes, cache what can be cached, and protect expensive endpoints with stricter limits or authentication. In every case I'd check that the origin can't be reached directly, bypassing the edge, and I'd shed load deliberately, returning cheap 429s or 503s, so the service degrades instead of collapsing.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Denial of service exhausts a resource: link capacity (volumetric), connection state (protocol), or application work (layer 7).
  • A full link can only be relieved upstream of it, by a network with more capacity than the attack.
  • Reflection forges the victim's address on UDP requests, and amplification picks requests with huge answers, up to tens of thousands of times larger.
  • Source address validation at every network would end reflection, and open UDP services are what it uses.
  • SYN floods fill tables, and stateful firewalls and load balancers often fail before the servers behind them.
  • Layer-7 attacks send valid requests that are cheap to make and costly to serve, and are met with rate limits, challenges and caching at the edge.

Check your understanding

A site's 10 Gbit/s link is receiving 80 Gbit/s of UDP junk. Its firewall drops every junk packet correctly. Why is the site still unreachable?

Filtering has to happen before the bottleneck, by someone with more capacity than the attack.

Why do amplification attacks use UDP services and not TCP ones?

A handshake proves the source address can receive packets before any large response is sent.

In the 2018 attack on GitHub, what made memcached servers such effective reflectors?

An amplification factor of up to about 51,000, from servers that were never meant to face the internet.

During a SYN flood, a stateful firewall in front of healthy servers starts dropping new connections. Why?

State is the resource under attack, and any device that keeps some can run out of it.

A flood of 3,000 requests a second to a search page takes a site down, though bandwidth use is barely above normal. What kind of attack is it, and what helps?

Each request is cheap to send and costly to serve, so the application runs out before the network does.
