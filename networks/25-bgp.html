BGP: How the Internet Routes

There is no map of the internet, and nobody is in charge of it. It is some 75,000 separately owned networks, belonging to carriers, companies, universities and cloud providers, each of which knows only its own neighbours. A packet still finds its way from a phone in Lagos to a server in Oregon, across half a dozen of them. What makes that possible is the Border Gateway Protocol, by which each network tells its neighbours which addresses it can reach, and they tell theirs. BGP is slow, has no central authority, and runs largely on trust. Each of those properties has, at some point, taken a household name off the internet for hours. This chapter covers how BGP decides, and how a typing mistake in one network becomes an outage in another.

Autonomous systems

An autonomous system (AS) is a network run by one organisation with one routing policy, identified by a number. Your internet provider is one, and so are Google (AS15169), Cloudflare (AS13335) and any large company that connects to more than one provider. Inside an AS, routers find paths with an interior protocol and the operator controls everything. Between ASes there is only BGP, and every connection is a business relationship of one of two kinds:

Customer and provider (transit)Peers
MoneyThe customer pays the providerUsually nobody pays
The provider or peer offersA way to reach the whole internetA way to reach each other's own networks and customers, and nothing more
Typical pairA company and its ISP; a regional ISP and a global carrierTwo ISPs of similar size; a content network and an access network, often meeting at an exchange point
WhyThe customer needs reachabilityBoth save on the transit they would otherwise pay to exchange that traffic

At the top are a dozen or so carriers that pay nobody. They peer with each other and sell transit to everyone below.

Announcements and withdrawals

Two neighbouring routers open a TCP connection on port 179 and keep it open for months. Over it, each tells the other which prefixes it can deliver packets to, and by what route:

UPDATE from AS300 to AS100:
   prefix   203.0.113.0/24
   AS path  300 64500                # "to reach it, go through me (300), then 64500, which originates it"
   next hop 198.51.100.1

# AS100 passes it on to its own neighbours, with itself added at the front:
   AS path  100 300 64500

An announcement is a promise:"send me packets for these addresses and I will get them there". The AS path grows by one at each hop, which serves two purposes. It measures distance, in networks crossed. And it prevents loops: a router that sees its own AS number in a path throws the route away. When a route stops working, a withdrawal is sent, and the neighbours look for another. BGP sends changes, not the whole table, and the full table today is about a million IPv4 prefixes.

router> show ip bgp 203.0.113.0/24

    198.51.100.1 from 198.51.100.1, localpref 200, valid, external, best
  200 500 64500                                # learned from a peer: a longer path, and a less preferred neighbour
    192.0.2.9 from 192.0.2.9, localpref 100, valid, external

Path selection

When a router has several routes to the same prefix, it chooses one. The surprise for newcomers is that the shortest path is only the second consideration. The first is policy, which in practice means money:

RuleWhat it saysWhy
1. Prefer by neighbour typeA route learned from a customer beats one from a peer, which beats one from a provider (set with "local preference")Traffic sent to a customer earns money; to a peer is free; to a provider costs money
2. Then the shortest AS pathFewer networks to crossA rough stand-in for distance
3. Then tie-breakersHints from the neighbour, the nearest exit, the lowest router IDTo make the choice deterministic
ExportingRoutes learned from customers are announced to everyone. Routes learned from peers and providers are announced only to customersNobody carries traffic between two parties who aren't paying them

One thing sits above all of it. A router's forwarding decision is made by longest-prefix match (Chapter 5), before any of these rules are consulted. BGP's preferences decide between routes to the same prefix. A route to a more specific prefix isn't competing: it simply wins for the addresses it covers, whoever announced it and however long its path.

Route leaks and hijacks

BGP was designed in 1989 for a few dozen networks whose operators knew each other. A router believes what its neighbour announces. Nothing in the protocol checks that the network claiming a prefix has any right to it. Two kinds of failure follow, and both happen every year:

  • A hijack: an AS announces a prefix that belongs to someone else. Networks that prefer the false route send their traffic to the wrong place, where it is dropped, inspected or answered by an impostor.
  • A route leak: an AS passes on routes it shouldn't, breaking the export rule above. The classic case is a customer with two providers that re-announces one provider's routes to the other. Suddenly a small network is advertising itself as the way to a large part of the internet, and traffic pours into links that can't carry it.
🧠
February 2008: Pakistan takes YouTube off the internet

On 24 February 2008 the government of Pakistan ordered the country's providers to block YouTube. Pakistan Telecom did it in a common way: inside its own network it created a route for 208.65.153.0/24, part of YouTube's address space, pointing at nothing, so that its customers' packets to YouTube would be discarded. Then, by mistake, it announced that route to its upstream provider, a large carrier in Hong Kong, which accepted it without checking and passed it to the rest of the world. YouTube itself was announcing 208.65.152.0/22, a block four times as large that contained those addresses. Pakistan Telecom's /24 was more specific, so no comparison of paths or preferences took place: within about two minutes, routers everywhere were sending YouTube's traffic to Pakistan, where it was thrown away. YouTube was unreachable worldwide for about two hours. Its engineers' first response used the same rule against the problem: they announced the range as two /25s, more specific still, and pulled part of the traffic back while the carrier in Hong Kong was reached and removed the bad route. Nobody attacked anybody. One network made an internal change visible outside, its provider didn't filter what a customer may announce, and the protocol did the rest.

Things to notice. With only the real announcement, all six networks reach AS64500, and the AS path gets longer with distance. When the intruder announces the same /24, the internet splits in two. Provider C hears the false route from its own customer and prefers it, Carrier 2 hears it from its customer C and prefers it to the true route from its peer, and D follows its provider. Half the networks are hijacked, and the true route is still there in the other half. That is preference by neighbour type deciding, not path length. Now let the intruder announce two /25s. Every network in the diagram sends its traffic to the intruder, including Provider A, whose own customer is the rightful owner, because a more specific prefix isn't compared with a less specific one. Turn on validation at the two carriers and the false routes stop spreading: only the intruder's own provider is still fooled. With validation everywhere, the false announcements go nowhere.

RPKI

What the panel's second control models is RPKI, the Resource Public Key Infrastructure. The holder of an address block publishes a signed statement, a route origin authorisation: "prefix 203.0.113.0/24, up to length /24, may be originated by AS64500". The signatures chain up to the regional registries that hand out addresses, in the manner of the certificate chains in Chapter 17. Routers fetch the validated list and sort every announcement into one of three classes:

ClassMeansRouters that validate
ValidAn authorisation covers this prefix, this length and this origin ASAccept
InvalidAn authorisation exists for these addresses, and the origin AS is wrong or the prefix is more specific than it allowsReject
Not foundThe owner hasn't published oneAccept, as before RPKI existed

After many years of slow adoption, most of the largest carriers now drop invalid routes, and over half of all announced prefixes are covered by an authorisation. It has turned many would-be global incidents into local ones. Know its limits, though:

  • It checks the origin, not the path. An attacker can announce a route whose path ends with the rightful AS number, with itself in the middle. That is valid as far as RPKI is concerned.
  • It doesn't stop leaks. A leaked route has the correct origin. It is the path that is wrong.
  • It protects only those who publish. A prefix with no authorisation is "not found", and accepted.
  • It works only where it is enforced. A network that doesn't validate still believes everything.

The older defences remain necessary: providers filtering what each customer is allowed to announce, limits on how many prefixes a neighbour may send, and monitoring your own prefixes from the outside so that you hear about a hijack from an alert and not from your users.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Assuming BGP picks the shortest path. Policy comes first: a longer path through a customer beats a shorter one through a provider.
  • Forgetting that more specific wins. No preference setting helps against a longer prefix. It is also the standard tool for pulling traffic back.
  • Accepting everything a customer announces. Every large leak and hijack passed through a provider that didn't filter.
  • Not publishing route origin authorisations for your prefixes. Without them, validating networks can't protect you.
  • Expecting changes to be instant. Announcements and withdrawals take seconds to minutes to settle across the world, and routes can flap on the way.
  • Running the tools you need for recovery over the network that BGP controls. Chapter 44 is about exactly that.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is BGP and how does it choose a route?

How a strong answer sounds
BGP is the protocol that connects the internet's autonomous systems, the independently run networks such as ISPs, carriers and large companies. Neighbouring routers hold long-lived TCP sessions and exchange announcements: a prefix together with the AS path to reach it. Each AS adds its own number to the path when it passes a route on, which gives a distance measure and prevents loops, since a router rejects any route containing its own AS. When a router has several routes to the same prefix, it applies policy first, usually through local preference: routes from customers are preferred over routes from peers, and those over routes from providers, because of who pays whom. Only then does it prefer the shortest AS path, followed by tie-breakers. Export follows the same economics: customer routes are announced to everyone, but peer and provider routes only to customers. Separately, forwarding uses longest-prefix match, so a more specific prefix always takes precedence regardless of those preferences.

Deeper: Monitoring shows that another network has started announcing a /24 from inside your /22. What is happening to your traffic, and what do you do?

How a strong answer sounds
That is a prefix hijack, malicious or accidental. Because their /24 is more specific than my /22, every router that accepts it will send traffic for those 256 addresses to them, regardless of AS-path length or preference; my announcement is still in the table but loses on longest-prefix match. Users of services in that /24 will see timeouts, or worse, a different party answering. Immediately, I'd announce the affected range myself at equal or greater specificity, the /24 itself and if necessary two /25s, though many networks filter prefixes longer than /24, so the /24 competes on path selection and only partly recovers. At the same time I'd contact the offending AS and, more usefully, its upstream providers through their network operations centres, asking them to filter the announcement. I'd check whether we have a route origin authorisation published; if we do, networks that validate are already rejecting the bogus route as invalid, which limits the damage, and if we don't, I'd create one now with a sensible maximum length. Afterwards: keep authorisations in place for all prefixes, keep external BGP monitoring, and consider announcing critical ranges as /24s permanently so that there is nothing more specific to steal.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • The internet is tens of thousands of autonomous systems. BGP is how each tells its neighbours which prefixes it can reach.
  • An announcement carries a prefix and an AS path, which grows at each hop and prevents loops.
  • Route choice is policy first (customer over peer over provider), then the shortest AS path. Peer and provider routes are passed on only to customers.
  • Forwarding uses longest-prefix match, so a more specific announcement wins everywhere it is accepted.
  • BGP believes its neighbours. A wrong announcement, by mistake or malice, becomes a hijack or a leak unless someone filters it.
  • RPKI lets networks reject announcements with the wrong origin or an over-specific prefix. It doesn't validate the path, and it works only where enforced.

Check your understanding

A router receives a route whose AS path contains its own AS number. What does it do?

The AS path is BGP's loop prevention. A route that has already passed through you is one you gave out yourself.

A network hears a route to the same prefix from a customer, with an AS path of four, and from a peer, with a path of two. Which does it normally use?

Sending traffic to a customer earns money. Local preference encodes that, and it outranks AS-path length.

You announce 198.51.100.0/22. Another network announces 198.51.100.0/24 with a much longer AS path. Where do packets for 198.51.100.7 go, in networks that accept both?

Path attributes only decide between routes to the same prefix. A /24 inside a /22 is a different, better-matching route.

A customer of two providers re-announces to provider B all the routes it learned from provider A. What is this called, and why is it harmful?

The origins are all correct, which is why origin validation doesn't catch it. The fault is in passing provider routes to a provider.

Which announcement does RPKI origin validation not stop?

RPKI checks only the origin and the prefix length. A path with the right AS at its end looks valid.
