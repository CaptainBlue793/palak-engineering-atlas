Networking for Multiplayer Games

Ten players in ten cities are in the same room of the same game, and each presses a key and sees their character move at once. That can't be literally true, since the server that decides what happened is tens of milliseconds away from all of them, and each of them is further still from the others. A fast multiplayer game works by showing each player a slightly different version of events: your own actions before the server has confirmed them, and everyone else's a little after they happened. The network code keeps those versions close enough together that nobody notices. This case study explains how, and why it is built on UDP.

The arrangement

Nearly all competitive games use an authoritative server. The server runs the real simulation and its version of events is the one that counts. Clients send it their inputs, such as "moving forward, turning left, fire pressed", and never their results, so a modified client can't simply announce that it hit everyone. The server applies the inputs, advances the world, and sends each client the state of whatever that player can see.

Client to serverServer to client
CarriesThe player's inputs, numberedA snapshot: positions, velocities, health and events
How oftenEvery client frame or every tickEvery tick, or every second or third
SizeTens of bytesA hundred to a thousand bytes, sent as differences from a snapshot the client has confirmed
If one is lostThe next packet repeats the last few inputsThe next snapshot replaces it

Why not TCP

The last row is the reason. A snapshot describes the world at one moment, and 16 ms later there is a newer one. If a snapshot is lost, the right thing to do is nothing, since its replacement is already on the way. TCP does the opposite on every point that matters here:

What TCP doesEffect on a game
Retransmits lost dataSpends time delivering a position that is already out of date
Delivers strictly in orderNewer snapshots that have arrived are held in the kernel until the old one is resent: a freeze of 200 ms or more for one lost packet
Reduces its rate after lossThe game has a fixed rate to send. It can't usefully send the world more slowly
Batches small writesInputs wait to be combined with others (Chapter 33)

With UDP each datagram stands alone. A lost one costs one tick of information, which the client covers by continuing the last known motion for a moment. Games that can wait, such as card games, turn-based strategy and most of what happens in menus, use TCP or HTTP and are right to.

Tick rate and snapshots

The server doesn't run continuously. It advances the world in fixed steps called ticks. In each tick it reads the inputs that have arrived, moves everything forward by one step of time, and sends snapshots.

import socket, time

TICK = 1 / 64                                         # 15.6 ms
sock = socket.socket(socket.AF_INET, socket.SOCK_DGRAM)
sock.bind(("0.0.0.0", 27015)); sock.setblocking(False)

tick = 0
while True:
    started = time.perf_counter()
    for data, addr in drain(sock):                     # every input that arrived since the last tick
        players[addr].apply(decode_input(data))
    world.step(TICK)                                   # advance the simulation by one tick
    tick += 1
    for addr, p in players.items():                   # each client gets the changes since the snapshot it last confirmed
        sock.sendto(encode_snapshot(world, tick, since=p.last_acked_tick), addr)
    time.sleep(max(0, TICK - (time.perf_counter() - started)))

A higher rate means fresher information and more work: twice the ticks is twice the processor time and twice the packets. A battle royale with a hundred players on a large map can't afford what a ten-player shooter can.

Snapshots arrive at intervals, and the screen redraws more often than that and needs smooth motion. So the client draws every other player slightly in the past. It keeps the last few snapshots and shows a moment about two snapshot intervals ago, sliding each character between two positions it actually knows. This is interpolation, and it is the same bargain as a jitter buffer (Chapter 42): a small fixed delay bought in exchange for smoothness, and for the ability to lose a snapshot without a visible jump.

Client-side prediction

If the client waited for the server before moving your own character, every key press would take a round trip to show. At 60 ms that feels like wading through mud. So the client doesn't wait. It runs the same movement code as the server and applies your input at once, predicting what the server will decide.

Nearly always the server agrees, because both ran the same code on the same inputs. They disagree when something happened that the client couldn't know: another player was in the way, or an explosion pushed you. Then the client must move your character to where the server says it is. If the difference is small the client blends it in over a few frames. If it is large the character snaps back, which players call rubber-banding. Only your own character is predicted. Other players' inputs can't be known in advance, so they are interpolated.

Lag compensation

Prediction and interpolation between them create a problem for shooting. You see your own position in the present and everyone else in the past, later by the network delay and by the interpolation delay. You aim at an opponent's head and press fire. By the time the server receives it, the server's copy of that opponent has moved on, and judged against the server's present your shot was at empty air.

The server therefore keeps a short history of every player's position. When your shot arrives, it works out what you were looking at, by subtracting your latency and your interpolation delay from the current time, moves everyone back to where they were at that moment, and tests the shot there. This is lag compensation. What you saw is what counts.

The cost falls on the target. They ran behind a wall, and on their own screen they were safe. A fraction of a second later they die, because on the shooter's screen, which is what the server judged, they were still in the open. Somebody's view has to be overruled, and games choose to favour the shooter because a hit that visibly lands and doesn't count feels worse. Servers limit how far back they will rewind, commonly to 200 ms or so, so that a player with a very slow connection can't shoot people in a past that everyone else has long left.

Ping is the round trip to the server. The delay before you see an opponent's move is their half of their ping, one tick for the server to process it, your half of your ping, and two ticks of interpolation. Real games add the time to draw a frame and the display's own delay.

Things to notice. At 60 ms each and 64 ticks, you see the opponent 107 ms late. They are 1.2 m out of cover and your screen shows them 0.56 m out. That gap is called peeker's advantage: the player who moves into view sees a stationary defender exactly where they are, and has about a tenth of a second before the defender's screen shows anyone at all. At 20 ticks a second the delay is 210 ms and the opponent has been in the open for a fifth of a second before you see them. At 128 ticks it falls to 83 ms. The tick rate matters as much as the ping does, because three ticks are added to every figure. When you fire, the server rewinds by your ping and your interpolation delay, 91 ms in the first case, and that is how long after reaching cover your opponent can still be hit. With prediction switched off nothing changes for the opponent, and your own movement lags by a full round trip and a tick, which is why every fast game predicts.

🧠
Valorant: spending on servers and a network to remove milliseconds

When Riot Games designed the tactical shooter Valorant, released in 2020, its engineers treated the delay in the panel above as a design target and published their reasoning. In a game where one shot decides a round, peeker's advantage sets how the game is played: if a player coming round a corner has a large head start, holding a position is pointless and every round turns into a rush. They broke the delay into its parts, the attacker's latency, the server's tick, the defender's latency, the interpolation buffer and the time to draw a frame, and went after each one. They ran every match at 128 ticks a second, which required reworking the server code until one tick took a fraction of the time it first had, so that a single machine could host enough matches to be affordable. They put servers in many more cities than was usual, with the stated aim that at least 70% of players would have a ping of 35 ms or less. And they extended Riot Direct, the company's own network, built earlier for League of Legends, which connects to internet providers directly so that players' packets take a short route on fibre Riot controls and avoid whatever route BGP would have picked across several carriers (Chapter 25). No design removes the delay entirely, and their published figures still showed a head start for the peeker. What they could show was that each millisecond in it had been found and a decision made about it.

Reliable-over-UDP

Some messages can't be lost. Positions can, since a newer one follows. "The round has ended", "you bought armour" and a line of chat must arrive, and UDP promises nothing. Games therefore build the reliability they need into their own protocol, and apply it only to the messages that need it.

# At the start of every packet, in either direction
seq        16 bits    this packet's number
ack        16 bits    the newest packet I have received from you
ack_bits   32 bits    one bit for each of the 32 packets before that one: received or not

Every packet reports on the last 33 packets from the other side, so each acknowledgement is repeated in dozens of packets, and losing some of those costs nothing. No packets are sent purely to acknowledge. The sender remembers which reliable messages went in which packet. If a packet is still unacknowledged after a short while, the reliable messages that were in it are put into the next outgoing packet, alongside the fresh state. The packet itself is never resent, since the rest of its contents are stale.

Kind of messageDeliveryExamples
Newest onlyUnreliable. Anything older than the last one received is discardedPositions, aim direction, animation state
Must arrive, in any orderResent until acknowledgedA hit, a death, picking up an item
Must arrive in orderResent until acknowledged, and held until its predecessors have arrivedChat, the steps of a purchase, loading a level
Player inputsUnreliable, with the last few repeated in every packetMovement and fire commands

In effect this is the useful part of TCP, rebuilt so that a lost chat message can't hold up a position update. Games also keep packets under about 1,200 bytes so that they are never fragmented, send something every few seconds to keep NAT mappings alive (Chapter 9), and encrypt and authenticate every packet. Few studios write this from nothing now. Libraries such as ENet and Valve's GameNetworkingSockets provide it, and QUIC's unreliable datagrams offer the same split inside a standard protocol (Chapter 18).

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Using TCP for fast state "to be safe". One lost packet freezes every player's view for a retransmission timeout.
  • Trusting the client. If clients report positions or hits, a modified client reports whatever it likes. Send inputs, and decide on the server.
  • Making everything reliable. Resending a position that is 200 ms old wastes bandwidth and delays the new one.
  • Sending the whole world every tick. Send differences from a snapshot the client has confirmed, and only what that player can perceive.
  • Unlimited lag compensation. Without a cap, a player with half a second of latency hits people who took cover long ago.
  • Testing only on a local network. Prediction errors and jitter appear at 80 ms with 2% loss. Test with those added artificially.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Why do real-time multiplayer games use UDP and not TCP?

How a strong answer sounds
Because game state is only valuable while it is fresh. The server sends a snapshot of the world many times a second, and each one supersedes the last. If a packet is lost, the best response is to ignore it and use the next one, which arrives a few milliseconds later. TCP can't do that: it guarantees in-order delivery, so when a segment is lost it holds back all the later data that has already arrived until the missing piece is retransmitted, which takes at least a round trip and often a 200 ms timeout. The player sees a freeze, and then old data they no longer need. TCP's congestion control also throttles the sender after loss, when a game has a fixed rate it needs to maintain. UDP gives independent datagrams with no ordering and no retransmission, so a lost update costs one tick, which the client hides by interpolating or extrapolating. For the messages that must arrive, chat or a purchase, the game implements its own acknowledgement and resend on top of UDP, for those messages only. And slower games, turn-based or card games, do just use TCP.

Deeper: A player complains: "I was already behind the wall and I still got shot." Explain what happened and whether it is a bug.

How a strong answer sounds
It is almost certainly lag compensation working as designed. Each client shows its own player in the present, through client-side prediction, and other players in the past, delayed by network latency plus an interpolation buffer of a couple of snapshot intervals. So when the shooter fired, their screen showed the victim where the victim had been perhaps 100 to 150 ms earlier, still in the open. The shot takes half the shooter's ping to reach the server. The server knows the shooter's latency and interpolation delay, so it rewinds the victim's position to what the shooter saw at the moment of firing, tests the shot against that, and registers a hit. Meanwhile the victim's own client had already moved them behind the wall, and the news of their death arrives a moment later. Both players saw something consistent; they saw different moments. The game has to pick whose view is authoritative, and favouring the shooter means that what you aim at is what you hit, which is the better of the two experiences. It isn't a bug unless the rewind is excessive: I'd check the shooter's ping, the cap on lag compensation, typically around 200 ms, the tick rate and the interpolation settings. Lowering the effect means lowering the delays: higher tick rate, servers closer to players, and a tighter cap, at the cost of making the game worse for players with high latency.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • An authoritative server receives inputs and sends snapshots. Clients never report results.
  • State goes over UDP because a newer snapshot replaces a lost one, and TCP would hold everything back to resend stale data.
  • The server advances in ticks. Clients draw other players about two snapshots in the past and interpolate between known positions.
  • Client-side prediction applies your own inputs at once, and replays them on top of the server's state when it arrives.
  • Lag compensation rewinds the world to what the shooter saw, so a target can be hit shortly after reaching cover.
  • Reliability is added per message with sequence numbers and acknowledgements carried in every packet.
