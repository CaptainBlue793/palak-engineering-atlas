Service Mesh & mTLS

Split an application into fifty services and every one of them needs the same things from the network: encryption, a way to know who is calling, load balancing that works per request, timeouts, retries, and a record of every call for when something is slow. You can write that into each service, in each language, and keep fifty implementations in step. Or you can take it out of the services altogether and put a small proxy next to every one of them, all configured from one place. The second arrangement is a service mesh. It rests on ideas you already have: the reverse proxy, TLS with certificates on both sides, and layer-7 load balancing. This chapter covers how a mesh is put together, what it gives you, and what it costs, including the way a well-meant retry policy can take a system down.

Sidecars and Envoy

In a sidecar mesh, every pod gets one extra container: a proxy, most often Envoy. It shares the pod's network namespace with the application (Chapter 31). When the pod starts, a few packet-redirection rules are installed inside that namespace, so that every connection the application opens, and every connection that arrives for it, is diverted through the proxy. The application isn't changed or even told. It makes a plain HTTP call to http://orders/ and believes it is talking to the orders service.

The proxies are the data plane. They do nothing by themselves. A control plane (Istio's istiod, or Linkerd's equivalent) watches the cluster and continuously pushes each proxy what it needs to know: which services exist and where their pods are, what the routing and retry rules are, and its certificate.

🧠
Lyft, 2015: "the network should be transparent"

Around 2015 Lyft was moving from one large application to many small services, and finding what most companies find at that stage. Calls between services failed in ways nobody could explain. Each team's client library did timeouts and retries a little differently, or not at all. When a request was slow there was no way to see which of a dozen hops was to blame, and engineers came to distrust the whole idea of microservices because the network between them was opaque. Matt Klein and his colleagues decided to stop fixing libraries. They wrote a proxy, Envoy, to run beside every service instance and carry all of its traffic, in and out. Every call in the company then passed through the same piece of software, which meant it was load-balanced, timed out, retried and, above all, measured in the same way, in whatever language the service was written. Klein's stated aim was that "the network should be transparent to applications", and that when problems occur it should be easy to find the source. Lyft open-sourced Envoy in 2016. It became the data plane of Istio and most other meshes, and the proxy inside many cloud load balancers and ingress controllers. The mesh grew out of the observation that once every call goes through an identical proxy, the proxy is the right place to put everything that calls have in common.

mTLS and workload identity

In ordinary TLS only the server proves who it is (Chapter 17). In mutual TLS the client presents a certificate too, and each side verifies the other. Inside a cluster that answers a question that IP addresses can't. A pod's address is temporary and says nothing about what is running there, so "allow 10.244.2.7" is not a meaningful rule. A certificate can carry a name.

In a mesh, each workload's identity comes from its Kubernetes service account and is written in a standard form:

spiffe://cluster.local/ns/shop/sa/orders          # "the orders service account, in the shop namespace"

$ istioctl proxy-config secret orders-b2 -o json | jq -r '.dynamicActiveSecrets[0].secret.tlsCertificate.certificateChain.inlineBytes' \
    | base64 -d | openssl x509 -noout -subject -ext subjectAltName -dates
X509v3 Subject Alternative Name: critical
    URI:spiffe://cluster.local/ns/shop/sa/orders
notBefore=Oct  6 09:14:07 2026 GMT
notAfter=Oct  7 09:16:07 2026 GMT                  # valid for one day, and replaced automatically long before that

The control plane is a private certificate authority. It issues each proxy a certificate for its identity, valid for hours, and renews it without anyone being involved, which removes the expiry outages of Chapter 17 by making renewal constant. With identities in place, three things become configuration:

# 1. Encrypt everything, and refuse plain text
kind: PeerAuthentication
spec: { mtls: { mode: STRICT } }

# 2. Say who may call whom, by identity
kind: AuthorizationPolicy
metadata: { name: orders-callers, namespace: shop }
spec:
  selector: { matchLabels: { app: orders } }
  rules:
  - from: [{ source: { principals: ["cluster.local/ns/shop/sa/web"] } }]
    to:   [{ operation: { methods: ["GET", "POST"], paths: ["/items/*"] } }]

Retries and timeouts in the mesh

Because the proxy sees every request as a request, it can do per-request things on the application's behalf: balance each call separately, which fixes the long-connection problem of gRPC (Chapter 22), stop sending to a pod that keeps returning errors, send 5% of traffic to a new version, enforce a timeout, and retry a failed call on another pod.

Retries are the feature most likely to hurt you. One retry turns a brief fault into a success, and that is worth having. But retries multiply through layers. If the edge retries three times, and the service it calls retries three times, and the service below that does too, a single user request can become 4 × 4 × 4 attempts at the bottom. And the moment at which that happens is the moment the bottom service is struggling.

Things to notice. With inventory down, three retries at every layer turn one user request into 64 requests at inventory, and the user gets an error anyway, later than they would have. If the service was failing because it was overloaded, it has just been handed sixty-four times the load, and it can't recover while that continues. Retrying only at the edge sends 4. A retry budget at every layer keeps it under 2, because once most calls are failing the proxies stop retrying. Now set inventory to fail half its requests, the case retries are meant for. With three retries at every layer nearly every user request succeeds, at twice the load on inventory. Retrying only at the edge gets 94% for slightly less load, and a 20% budget gets 86%. The cautious policies give up some success in the easy case, and that is the price of not multiplying the load by 64 in the hard one. Retries are good for small, random failures and bad for large ones, and a budget is what tells the two apart automatically.

Three more rules go with retries. Retry only requests that are safe to repeat (Chapter 15). Give every call a deadline and pass the remaining time down the chain (Chapter 22), so that a retry isn't started for a caller who has already given up. And make timeouts shorter at each hop going down, so that the layer above is still waiting when the answer comes.

Sidecarless meshes

A proxy in every pod has real costs. Each one uses memory and CPU, multiplied by thousands of pods. Upgrading the proxy means restarting every pod. And the proxy shares the pod's life, which produces awkward moments at start-up and shutdown when the application is running and its network isn't. The newer designs move the proxy out of the pod.

Sidecar meshSidecarless ("ambient") mesh
ProxiesOne per podOne small layer-4 proxy per node, plus optional shared layer-7 proxies per service or namespace
mTLS and identityIn the sidecarIn the node proxy, still with a separate identity per workload
Layer-7 featuresAlways on, for every podOnly where you ask for them, at a shared proxy
OverheadMemory and CPU in every podMuch lower. Paid per node
UpgradesRestart every podRestart the node proxies. Applications are untouched
IsolationA proxy fault affects one podA node proxy fault affects every pod on that node

Some network plugins take another route to the same end and do the layer-4 part, including encryption, inside the kernel. Whichever form it takes, a mesh is a lot of moving parts in the path of every request. It earns its place when you have many services in several languages and real requirements for encryption and identity between them. For five services written in one language, a good client library and TLS are simpler, and simpler is easier to debug.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Retries at every layer. They multiply. Retry at one layer, cap them with a budget, and never retry non-idempotent calls.
  • Retries in the application and in the mesh. Two layers of retry in one hop, usually discovered during an outage.
  • Timeouts that don't nest. If orders waits 5 seconds for inventory and web waits 2 seconds for orders, the last 3 seconds of work are for nobody.
  • Leaving mTLS in permissive mode for ever. Permissive accepts plain text, so anything can still call anything unencrypted.
  • Treating the mesh as free. It adds latency, resources and a new control plane that must be upgraded and can fail.
  • Adopting a mesh to fix a problem you can't name. Start from the requirement: encryption, identity, or traffic control.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is a service mesh, and what does mTLS add?

How a strong answer sounds
A service mesh moves the networking concerns that every service shares out of application code and into infrastructure. In the classic design a proxy, usually Envoy, runs as a sidecar container in every pod, and redirection rules in the pod send all inbound and outbound traffic through it. A control plane configures all the proxies centrally with service discovery, routing rules and certificates. The proxies then provide per-request load balancing, timeouts and retries, traffic shifting for canaries, uniform metrics and tracing, and security, without changes to the services and regardless of language. Mutual TLS is the security part: every proxy-to-proxy connection is TLS in which both sides present certificates. The certificate encodes the workload's identity, derived from its service account, and is issued and rotated automatically by the control plane. That gives encryption in transit inside the cluster and, more importantly, authenticated identity, so that authorisation rules can say which services may call which, by name, instead of relying on IP addresses that change with every pod.

Deeper: During an incident, a backend that was merely slow became completely unavailable, and stayed down after the original trigger was gone. Retries are configured throughout. What happened, and what would you change?

How a strong answer sounds
That's a retry storm, a form of cascading failure. The backend slowed down, calls to it began to time out, and each caller retried. Retries are extra load on a service whose problem is too much load, so it slowed further and more calls timed out. If several layers retry independently, the multiplication compounds: three retries at each of three layers is up to 64 attempts per original request. The backend never gets the breathing space to recover, so the outage outlasts its cause; the system has settled into a state where it is fully busy serving requests that will all be abandoned. To fix it I'd bound retries: retry at one layer only, preferably the one closest to the failure, with exponential backoff and jitter; add retry budgets so that retries can't exceed, say, ten or twenty percent of normal traffic; and add circuit breaking or outlier ejection so that callers fail fast when a backend is clearly unhealthy. I'd propagate deadlines so that work is dropped once the caller has given up, make timeouts decrease down the call chain, and have the backend shed load early, rejecting quickly when its queue is long. And I'd restrict retries to idempotent operations.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • A service mesh puts a proxy beside every service and configures them all from a control plane, so that networking behaviour is uniform and outside application code.
  • A sidecar takes over a pod's traffic by redirection inside the pod's network namespace. The application is unchanged.
  • Mutual TLS has both sides present certificates. Workloads get short-lived certificates carrying an identity from their service account.
  • With identities, access rules name services, methods and paths, which addresses and ports can't express.
  • Retries multiply across layers, to 64 times with three retries at three layers. Retry once, in one place, within a budget.
  • Sidecarless meshes move the proxy to the node to cut cost. Any mesh adds parts to every request's path, so adopt one for a stated need.

Check your understanding

How does a sidecar proxy come to handle a service's traffic when the service's code hasn't been changed?

The proxy shares the pod's network namespace, so redirection there is invisible to the application.

What does mutual TLS add to ordinary TLS?

Ordinary TLS authenticates the server only. With both ends authenticated, the server can decide by caller.

Why do meshes identify workloads by certificate and not by IP address?

An identity tied to the service account stays the same across restarts, and is proved cryptographically.

A request passes through three layers, and each layer retries a failed call up to three times. The service at the bottom is down. How many requests can it receive for one user request?

Four attempts at each of three layers multiply: 4 × 4 × 4.

What is the main change in a sidecarless ("ambient") mesh?

The functions stay. Where the proxy runs changes, cutting per-pod cost and making upgrades independent of applications.
