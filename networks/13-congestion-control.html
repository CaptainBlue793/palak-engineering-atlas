Congestion Control: Slow Start, CUBIC & BBR

A TCP sender doesn't know how fast the path to the other end is. Nobody tells it. The capacity might be 2 Mbit/s over a weak phone signal or 10 Gbit/s between two racks, it is shared with an unknown number of other connections, and it changes from second to second. Send too slowly and the transfer wastes a link that was idle. Send too fast, and routers' queues fill and overflow, and if every sender does that the network spends its time carrying packets that are about to be dropped. Congestion control is how each sender, alone, works out a rate that is fast and still fair. It is the reason the internet functions under load, and the reason a new connection is slow for its first few round trips.

Why congestion control

🧠
October 1986: the internet stops working

In 1986 the link between Lawrence Berkeley Laboratory and the University of California at Berkeley, two sites 400 metres apart, was rated at 32,000 bits per second. That October its useful throughput fell to about 40 bits per second, a factor of a thousand, and similar collapses were appearing across the early internet. Nothing was broken. The TCP of the time had flow control but no notion of the network's capacity, so every sender transmitted as much as the receiver's window allowed. When a link was overloaded, routers dropped packets. Senders timed out and sent them again, on top of the new data they were still sending. The more congested the network became, the more traffic was offered to it, and most of what the links carried was retransmissions of packets that would be dropped again. This is congestion collapse, and it is stable: the network stays in it for as long as the load is there. Van Jacobson, at LBL, worked out the fix with Michael Karels and published it in 1988 as "Congestion Avoidance and Control". It changed no router and no packet format. It was a handful of lines in the sender: start slowly, treat a lost packet as a sign that the network is full, back off sharply when one is lost, and probe upwards gently otherwise. Every TCP since has been built on those rules, and no collapse of that kind has happened again.

The mechanism is one more variable in the sender, the congestion window (cwnd): the sender's own estimate of how much data the path can hold in flight. It combines with Chapter 12 in the obvious way:

data in flight  ≤  min(congestion window, receive window)

The receive window is told to the sender. The congestion window has to be discovered, and each algorithm in this chapter is a different way of discovering it.

Slow start and congestion avoidance

Slow start is why the first moments of a connection matter so much for web performance. Whatever the bandwidth, a new connection may send only 10 segments in its first round trip:

It is the main reason that fitting the critical part of a page into the first 14 KB is worth the effort, that servers close to users matter, and that a connection which has already warmed up is worth keeping (Chapter 38).

AIMD and the sawtooth

In congestion avoidance the rule is additive increase, multiplicative decrease: add one segment per round trip while all is well, and halve on a loss. Plotted over time, cwnd climbs in a straight line, drops, and climbs again: a sawtooth. The asymmetry is deliberate. If two connections share a link and both follow it, the one with the larger window loses more at each halving, and the two converge on equal shares without ever communicating.

It has a weakness that shapes everything after it. The algorithm assumes every loss means congestion. On a wireless link, packets are also lost to interference, with no queue anywhere near full. For a sender obeying AIMD, the achievable rate is roughly:

throughput  ≈  (segment size / RTT) × 1.22 / √(loss rate)

# 1,460-byte segments, 100 ms round trip:
#   loss 0.01%  →  about 14 Mbit/s        loss 1%  →  about 1.4 Mbit/s, whatever the link's bandwidth

Two things follow from the formula. A small rate of random loss caps throughput far below the link's capacity. And throughput falls as RTT grows, so over the same bottleneck a nearby connection takes more than a distant one.

CUBIC

Adding one segment per round trip is painfully slow on a fast, long path. A connection whose window was halved from 10,000 segments needs 5,000 round trips to get back, which at 100 ms is over eight minutes. CUBIC, the default in Linux since 2006 and now in Windows and macOS as well, changes the growth rule. After a loss it reduces the window by 30%, not 50%, and remembers the size at which the loss happened. Then it grows as a cubic function of the time since that loss: quickly at first, flattening as it approaches the remembered size, where trouble is likely, and then accelerating again if nothing goes wrong. Growth depends on elapsed time and not on the number of ACKs, so a long round trip no longer slows it down.

BBR

CUBIC still fills the bottleneck's buffer until a packet is dropped, and still mistakes random loss for congestion. BBR, published by Google in 2016, takes a different approach. It doesn't wait for loss. It builds a model of the path from two measurements, the highest delivery rate seen recently (the bottleneck's bandwidth) and the lowest round-trip time seen recently (the path's delay with empty queues), and then it paces packets out at that rate, keeping about one bandwidth-delay product in flight. Every few round trips it sends 25% faster for a moment to see whether more bandwidth has appeared, then drains the queue it made. Every ten seconds it briefly sends almost nothing, so that it can measure the true minimum delay.

A model in whole round trips, with one flow and fixed random numbers so that runs are repeatable. Packets in flight beyond what the link carries wait in the queue and add delay; beyond the queue's size they are dropped. BBR here is the first version, simplified.

Things to notice. With CUBIC and a 100-packet queue, the window overshoots in slow start, then settles into a pattern between 140 and 200 packets. It never falls below what the link can carry, so 99% of the link is used, but the queue is never empty either, and packets wait in it for 81 ms on average. Make the queue deep and losses become rare, while the average wait grows to over 400 ms, four times the path's own delay: this is bufferbloat. Make it shallow and the window dips below the link's capacity after each loss, which costs a little throughput with CUBIC and more with Reno, at 87%, because Reno halves. Now add 1% random loss. Reno and CUBIC fall to about 15% of the link, because they read each random loss as congestion. BBR stays at 95% with a queue of a few milliseconds. Its line shows what it is doing: brief steps up and down to test for more bandwidth, and a dip every ten seconds when it re-measures the delay.

Queues, delay and what routers can do

A loss-based sender finds the capacity by filling the queue until it overflows. The bigger the queue, the longer packets wait in it before that happens. Home routers and mobile networks were long built with very deep queues, in the belief that dropping packets is always bad, and the result was connections with good throughput and seconds of delay whenever someone uploaded a file. Two things have improved it. Routers can manage their queues actively, dropping or marking packets early when delay starts to build, with algorithms such as CoDel. And with ECN, a router can set a bit in the packet instead of dropping it, the receiver echoes the bit back, and the sender slows down without anything having been lost.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Expecting a fast link to make a short transfer fast. The first few round trips are limited by slow start, not by bandwidth.
  • Opening a fresh connection for each request. Each one starts again from 10 segments. A reused connection keeps its window.
  • Treating all loss as congestion when diagnosing. On wireless paths, random loss can hold a loss-based sender far below capacity.
  • Looking for cwnd in a packet capture. It isn't in any header. Read it on the sender with ss -ti.
  • Opening many parallel connections to "go faster". It works by taking more than a fair share, and it multiplies the slow-start bursts.
  • Switching to BBR without measuring. It helps on lossy and long paths, and can be worse for neighbours on shallow buffers.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How does TCP decide how fast to send?

How a strong answer sounds
The sender keeps a congestion window, its estimate of how much data the network path can have in flight, and never has more unacknowledged data outstanding than the smaller of that and the receiver's advertised window. It discovers the right size by probing. A new connection starts with about 10 segments and doubles the window every round trip, which is slow start. When it detects a loss or reaches a threshold, it switches to congestion avoidance, growing by about one segment per round trip. On a loss signalled by duplicate ACKs it cuts the window, by half in classic Reno and by 30% in CUBIC, and resumes growing; on a timeout it drops to one segment and slow-starts again. That gives the sawtooth. CUBIC, the usual default, grows along a cubic curve in time so it recovers quickly on fast long-distance paths. BBR works differently: it measures the bottleneck's delivery rate and the minimum RTT and paces at that rate instead of waiting for loss.

Deeper: Users on mobile networks get poor download speeds from your servers although the radio link has plenty of bandwidth. What could congestion control have to do with it, and what would you try?

How a strong answer sounds
Loss-based algorithms such as CUBIC assume every lost packet means a full queue. Mobile and Wi-Fi links lose packets to interference and handovers with no congestion at all, and each such loss cuts the window. The steady-state rate of a loss-based sender is proportional to one over the square root of the loss rate, and inversely proportional to RTT, so one percent random loss on a 100 ms path caps a flow at a megabit or two regardless of the bandwidth. Mobile paths also have variable delay and deep buffers, which add latency. I'd first confirm it on the servers: retransmission rates, ss -ti showing small congestion windows and delivery rates well below the link, split by network type. Then I'd test BBR on a portion of traffic, since it estimates bandwidth from delivery rate and isn't driven by loss, and compare throughput, retransmissions and latency. I'd also look at whether clients use HTTP/3, because QUIC recovers from loss better and its congestion control can be updated without waiting for operating systems, and make sure responses are served from close to the users, since a shorter RTT helps every algorithm.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Without congestion control, overload causes retransmissions that add to the overload: congestion collapse.
  • The sender keeps a congestion window, its own estimate of the path's capacity, and obeys the smaller of it and the receive window.
  • Slow start doubles the window each round trip from 10 segments, so short transfers are limited by round trips, not bandwidth.
  • Additive increase and multiplicative decrease give a sawtooth and fair shares, and treat every loss as congestion.
  • CUBIC, the common default, grows on a cubic curve in time and backs off by 30%. BBR models bandwidth and delay and paces to them.
  • Loss-based senders fill the bottleneck's queue: deep queues mean delay, shallow ones cost throughput.

Check your understanding

Who sets a connection's congestion window, and how?

It lives only in the sender's kernel. The field in the header is the receive window, a different limit.

A connection starts with a congestion window of 10 segments and sees no loss. What is the window after three more round trips of slow start?

It doubles each round trip: 10, 20, 40, 80.

A Reno sender detects a loss through three duplicate ACKs. Another detects one through a timeout. How do their reactions differ?

Duplicate ACKs show the path is still delivering. A timeout suggests it may not be, so the response is harsher.

A CUBIC flow over Wi-Fi with 1% random loss gets a small fraction of the link's bandwidth. Why?

Loss-based control can't tell interference from a full queue. Throughput falls with the square root of the loss rate.

A router has a very deep queue, and a loss-based flow is uploading through it. What do other users of that link notice first?

Bufferbloat. The sender only backs off when the queue overflows, and a deep queue takes a long time to overflow.
