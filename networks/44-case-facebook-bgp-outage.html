Facebook's 2021 BGP Outage

On 4 October 2021 Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp and Messenger vanished from the internet for about six hours. Their servers were running the whole time and nothing had been attacked. An engineer had run a maintenance command, the company's own DNS servers responded by telling the world to stop sending them traffic, and the engineers who could have reversed the change found that the tools, the remote access and, by several accounts, the door badges they needed all depended on the network that was down. This case study follows the failure through its four stages. Each one is a mechanism from earlier in the course, working as it was designed to.

The network, in three parts

PartWhat it isWhat it does
Data centresA small number of very large sitesHold the data and run the applications
Edge sitesMany small sites in cities round the worldConnect to other networks, announce Facebook's address prefixes to them with BGP, end users' connections, and run the authoritative DNS servers
The backboneFacebook's own fibre and routers, tens of thousands of kilometres of itCarries everything between the edge sites and the data centres

A request from a phone reaches the nearest edge site over the public internet, and travels from there to a data centre over the backbone. The backbone is the only connection between the two. It is the part an outsider never sees and the part everything else depends on.

The command

Backbone links need maintenance: fibre is repaired, routers are upgraded, capacity is added. To do the work safely, engineers take part of the backbone out of service and let traffic move to the rest. On the afternoon of 4 October, during routine work of this kind, a command was issued that was meant to assess how much backbone capacity was available. Through an error in what it did, it took down every backbone connection at once, and disconnected all of Facebook's data centres from each other and from the edge.

Facebook had a safeguard for this. Commands of this kind passed through a tool that audited them and refused the dangerous ones. That tool had a bug, and it let the command through. The mistake itself was small and of a familiar kind. What turned it into a six-hour outage was everything it set off.

Routes withdrawn

Facebook's authoritative DNS servers sit at the edge sites and are reached by anycast: each site announces the same small prefixes, and a resolver's query goes to the nearest one (Chapter 26). Starting at about 15:40 UTC, other networks saw a burst of BGP messages from Facebook's network, AS32934, withdrawing those prefixes. Within minutes no router outside Facebook had a route to them.

# Before: the name servers for facebook.com, all inside Facebook's own address space
$ dig +short NS facebook.com
a.ns.facebook.com.      # 129.134.30.12
b.ns.facebook.com.      # 129.134.31.12
c.ns.facebook.com.      # 185.89.218.12
d.ns.facebook.com.      # 185.89.219.12

# During: a public resolver gets no answer from any of them, and says so
$ dig @1.1.1.1 facebook.com
;; ->>HEADER<<- opcode: QUERY, status: SERVFAIL, id: 31322
;; ANSWER: 0

The status is worth reading closely. It is SERVFAIL, "I couldn't get an answer", and not NXDOMAIN, "that name doesn't exist". The domain was still registered and the delegation from .com was intact. The resolvers knew which servers to ask and had no path to any of them. Once the records already cached had expired, after minutes, the names stopped resolving anywhere in the world.

DNS servers pull themselves

Nobody had withdrawn the DNS prefixes by hand. The DNS servers had done it, following a rule that is sound in ordinary conditions. An edge site that can't reach the data centres is broken: it can accept a user's connection and has no way to serve it. So each site checks continually whether it can reach the data centres, and if it can't, it stops announcing its prefixes, so that traffic flows to a site that works.

# The rule at every edge DNS site, simplified
def health_check():
    if can_reach_data_centres():
        announce(DNS_PREFIXES)      # BGP: "queries for facebook.com may come here"
    else:
        withdraw(DNS_PREFIXES)      # "this site is unhealthy, use another one"

The rule rests on an assumption that nobody had needed to state: that when one site is unhealthy, others are not. With the backbone gone, every site failed the check in the same minute, and every site withdrew. The DNS servers were all running and would have answered correctly, and no query could reach them.

Locked out

Reversing the change would have taken minutes for someone able to log in to the backbone routers. Nobody could. Remote access to the data-centre networks ran over the network that was down. The internal tools for diagnosing and repairing the network couldn't find their servers, because internal names were served by the same DNS. Chat, email and the systems used to coordinate incidents were Facebook's own products and services. Engineers reached each other by personal phones and other companies' tools.

The remaining way was to send people to the routers. That was slow by design. The buildings are built to be hard to enter, and the hardware inside is built to be hard to modify even by someone standing in front of it, both of which are right for security and wrong for this afternoon. Press reports at the time said some staff could not get through doors whose badge readers depended on the network. It took hours to get the right people inside with the right authority to act.

Restoring the backbone did not end the incident either. With no traffic, the data centres had cut their power use by tens of megawatts, and their caches were empty. Letting billions of users back at once risked electrical faults and a stampede on every backend (Chapter 43). Traffic was let back gradually, a procedure the company had rehearsed in drills, and services returned between about 21:00 and 22:00 UTC.

If the DNS sites had kept announcing, names would have resolved and the service would still have been down, since the edge can't reach the data centres either way. The withdrawal changed how the outage looked, from errors to total disappearance, and added a storm of retries, and it was not the cause. The other choice changes the length. The command caused the outage, and the dependency of the repair tools on the thing being repaired made it last six hours.

    What the rest of the internet felt

    The outage did not stay inside Facebook. Billions of devices had its apps installed, and when an app can't resolve a name it tries again, promptly and repeatedly. People did the same by hand, reloading pages. A resolver that gets no answer from an authoritative server keeps the failure only briefly, or not at all, so each retry became a new attempt to reach servers that could not be reached, with a wait for a timeout each time. Cloudflare, which runs the public resolver 1.1.1.1, reported that queries for Facebook's names rose to about thirty times their normal volume. Resolvers at internet providers all over the world carried the same extra load, and some slowed down for every name they served, so that for a while unrelated websites were harder to reach because of traffic meant for Facebook. Other social networks and messaging services, meanwhile, received a sudden wave of new users and some struggled with that. The failure also reached anything that used "Log in with Facebook", and any business that ran its customer service on WhatsApp. One organisation's outage becomes load on shared infrastructure, and clients that retry without backing off make that load larger (Chapter 32).

    Lessons

    What happenedThe general lesson
    One command reached every backbone routerLimit how much one action can affect. Apply changes to one part, verify, and then continue
    The audit tool had a bugA safeguard is software and fails like software. Test it with the dangerous input, and don't let it be the only check
    Every site failed the same health check togetherA health check that removes capacity needs a floor: if everything looks unhealthy, the check is probably wrong, so stay up
    All the name servers were inside the network that failedKeep some of the things that make you reachable, such as DNS, on infrastructure that doesn't share your failure
    Repair tools and access depended on the production networkThe way in for repairs must share nothing with what is being repaired, and must be exercised regularly
    Coming back was dangerous tooRehearse recovery from a total stop. Caches are empty and every client returns at once

    The third lesson deserves a second look, because the same rule exists in most systems. A load balancer that removes backends failing their health checks will, with a faulty check, remove all of them. Many load balancers therefore have a setting that ignores health checks when too many backends fail at once and sends traffic to all of them anyway, on the reasoning that a server that is probably broken is still better than no server (Chapter 46).

    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Calling it "a DNS outage" or "a BGP hijack". DNS and BGP did what they were told. The cause was a configuration change to a private backbone.
    • Hosting every authoritative name server on your own network. When the network fails, your names fail with it, and so does your status page.
    • Automation that withdraws capacity with no lower limit. It turns one false signal into a complete outage.
    • Keeping the runbook, the chat and the credentials behind the system they are for. You find out during the incident.
    • Assuming physical access is a quick fallback. It takes hours, and needs people with authority on site.
    • Clients that retry immediately and for ever. They turn someone's outage into everyone's load.

    Interview drill

    Explain it simply: Facebook's servers were running the whole time. Why couldn't anyone reach them?

    How a strong answer sounds
    Because nothing on the internet could find them. A maintenance command accidentally took down Facebook's backbone, the private network connecting its edge sites to its data centres. Facebook's authoritative DNS servers run at those edge sites and are announced to the internet with BGP using anycast, and each site is designed to withdraw its BGP announcement if it can't reach the data centres, on the reasonable principle that an unhealthy site should stop attracting traffic. With the backbone down, every site failed that check at once and withdrew, so the prefixes containing all of Facebook's name servers disappeared from the global routing table. Resolvers around the world could no longer reach any name server for facebook.com and returned SERVFAIL, so the names stopped resolving. Even with a cached address, connections would have failed, since the edge couldn't reach the data centres. So it was a routing failure that took DNS with it, and the servers themselves were healthy.

    Deeper: The fix was to undo one change. Why did it take six hours, and what would you design differently?

    How a strong answer sounds
    Because the means of making the fix depended on the thing that was broken. Remote access to the routers ran over the backbone that was down; the internal tools for diagnosis and configuration found their servers through the internal DNS that had gone with it; even communication tools were the company's own. So the only path was physical access to the data centres, which is deliberately slow: high physical security, hardware hardened against local modification, and reportedly access systems that themselves needed the network. Then recovery had to be staged, to avoid power surges and overloading cold caches. Designing differently, I'd want an out-of-band management network, with its own connectivity, addressing, DNS and authentication, sharing nothing with production, and I'd test it regularly by actually using it. I'd limit the blast radius of changes: staged rollout across the backbone with automatic verification and rollback, so no single command can touch everything. I'd treat the audit tool as fallible, and add a second independent check for global-scope commands. I'd give the DNS health check a floor, so that sites don't all withdraw when they all fail together, and I'd host some authoritative DNS outside my own network. And I'd keep incident communication and runbooks on independent systems. The common thread is to find circular dependencies before an incident does.

    🧠 Key takeaways

    • A command meant to assess backbone capacity took the whole backbone down, and the tool meant to stop such commands had a bug.
    • Edge DNS sites are designed to withdraw their BGP routes when they can't reach the data centres. All of them did, together.
    • With the name servers unreachable, resolvers worldwide answered SERVFAIL, and the names stopped resolving.
    • The outage lasted hours because remote access and repair tools ran on the network that had failed.
    • Automation that removes unhealthy capacity needs a floor, for the case where everything looks unhealthy at once.
    • The path used for recovery must share nothing with production, and must be tested by using it.
