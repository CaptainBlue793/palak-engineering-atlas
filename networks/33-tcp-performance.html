TCP Performance: Nagle, Delayed ACK & HOL

Two machines in the same rack are half a millisecond apart. A request between them takes 41 milliseconds, every time, and nothing is overloaded. That figure, 40 ms more than it should be, is one of the most recognisable in systems work. It comes from two reasonable optimisations inside TCP, each added in the 1980s to save packets, which wait for each other when an application sends its data in a certain way. This chapter is about that trap and its relatives: the places where TCP is correct and unexpectedly slow because of small packets, buffers of the wrong size, or one thing queued behind another. None of them appears as an error anywhere. They show up as latency, and you can only recognise them if you know they exist.

Nagle's algorithm

In the early 1980s people typed into remote terminals over slow links, and each keystroke was sent as its own packet: one byte of data inside 40 bytes of headers. On a busy link those tiny packets caused congestion by themselves. In 1984 John Nagle proposed a rule that every TCP still has, switched on by default:

# Nagle's algorithm, when the application writes some data
if there is a full segment's worth to send:           send it now
elif nothing sent earlier is still unacknowledged:    send it now      # the connection is idle: no reason to wait
else:                                                 hold it          # until an ACK arrives, or enough accumulates to fill a segment

It lets the first small write go at once and gathers any that follow into one packet, released when the first is acknowledged. For a stream of keystrokes it is ideal: the number of packets adapts by itself to the speed of the link. The assumption hidden in it is that acknowledgements come back promptly.

Delayed acknowledgements

The receiver has an optimisation of its own, from the same period. Acknowledging every segment the instant it arrives doubles the number of packets on the path, and many of those ACKs are wasted, because the application is about to send a reply that could carry the acknowledgement for free. So a receiver delays an ACK briefly:

The receiver sends its ACK whenWhy
It has data of its own to sendThe ACK rides along in that segment at no cost
A second full segment arrivesOne ACK then covers both
A timer expires: 40 ms on Linux, up to 200 ms elsewhereThe sender mustn't be left waiting for ever
Something is out of orderDuplicate ACKs must go at once, for fast retransmit (Chapter 11)

The 40 ms stall

Put the two together with an application that sends one request as two small writes, a header and then a body, and waits for the reply. This pattern is called write–write–read.

    The server needs 5 ms to produce the response once it has the whole request. The delayed-ACK timer is 40 ms, as on Linux. Arrows pointing right are from the client, and left from the server.

    Things to notice. With the defaults and a 1 ms round trip, the response takes 47 ms where 6 ms would do: 40 of those milliseconds are the two machines waiting for each other. Any one of three changes removes the stall. Set TCP_NODELAY on the client, and both writes leave at once. Make the application send the header and body in a single write, and there is nothing for Nagle to hold. Turn off delayed ACKs on the server, and the ACK comes straight back, though the body still waits one round trip for it, which is cheap at 1 ms and costs a full extra 100 ms on a long path. That last case shows what Nagle's algorithm costs when everything else is ideal: a small write behind unacknowledged data waits one round trip. On a 100 ms path the same defaults give 245 ms against a possible 105: the delayed ACK accounts for 40 of the difference, and the round trip that the body waits for its ACK accounts for the other 100. In proportion, though, the trap is at its worst on fast networks, where it multiplies the latency instead of adding to it.

    TCP_NODELAY

    sock.setsockopt(socket.IPPROTO_TCP, socket.TCP_NODELAY, 1)      # turn Nagle's algorithm off for this socket

# The bug, and the cheaper fix: one write, not two
sock.sendall(header); sock.sendall(body)       # write-write-read: stalls with Nagle on
sock.sendall(header + body)                     # one segment: nothing to stall

    With TCP_NODELAY every write is sent immediately. Nearly every request-and-response system sets it: database drivers, Redis, RPC libraries, web servers, and Go's standard library turns it on for every connection. The responsibility then moves to the application. If it makes a hundred tiny writes, it now sends a hundred tiny packets, so it must gather its output itself, in a buffer or with one vectored write, and hand TCP whole messages.

    🧠
    "It's always TCP_NODELAY. Every damn time."

    That is the title of a 2024 essay by Marc Brooker, a senior engineer at Amazon Web Services, and it describes something anyone who has run distributed systems for long enough will recognise. A service is slower than it should be by tens of milliseconds, with no load and no errors. Days go into examining garbage collection, disks, locks and queues. Then someone looks at a packet capture, sees a small segment sitting for 40 ms, and sets one socket option. Brooker's argument is that the world Nagle's algorithm was designed for has gone. In 1984 the problem was single keystrokes on links measured in kilobits. Today's applications send whole messages and want them delivered now, a data-centre round trip is shorter than the delayed-ACK timer by a factor of a hundred, and a default that silently trades 40 ms for a handful of bytes is the wrong way round. John Nagle himself has written, in comments on discussions of the subject, that the real fault lies with delayed ACKs and their fixed timer, which was added independently at about the same time and defeats his algorithm's assumption that acknowledgements return promptly. Both defaults are still in every operating system, because changing them would alter the behaviour of decades of software. So the knowledge travels by word of mouth: when a latency has a floor of 40 ms, or 200 ms on some systems, and a write–write–read pattern is anywhere near, check for this first.

    Buffer sizing and the BDP

    The opposite problem to small packets is not being able to keep enough data in flight. To use a path fully, a sender must have one bandwidth-delay product of data outstanding (P8): the amount that fits "in the pipe". The receiver's buffer must be large enough to advertise that as its window (Chapter 12), and the sender's buffer large enough to hold it for possible retransmission.

    BDP  =  bandwidth × round-trip time

1 Gbit/s  × 1 ms    =  125 KB          # in a data centre: any default is plenty
1 Gbit/s  × 80 ms   =  10 MB           # across an ocean: more than Linux's default ceiling of 6 MB
10 Gbit/s × 80 ms   =  100 MB

$ sysctl net.ipv4.tcp_rmem net.ipv4.tcp_wmem
net.ipv4.tcp_rmem = 4096  131072  6291456          # receive: minimum, initial, and the most autotuning may use
net.ipv4.tcp_wmem = 4096  16384   4194304          # send

    With a 6 MB ceiling and an 80 ms round trip, one connection can't exceed about 600 Mbit/s whatever the link's speed, and raising the third number is the fix for long, fast transfers. Bigger isn't simply better, though. Data sitting in a large send buffer has already been committed in order, so anything more urgent written afterwards waits behind it. For interactive traffic you want the buffer in the kernel short, and the queue in the application, where it can still be reordered or dropped.

    Head-of-line blocking, layer by layer

    "One thing stuck behind another" has appeared in several chapters. It is the same problem each time, and it moves down the stack as each layer fixes its own version:

    The remedy has the same shape at every layer: stop forcing unrelated things into one ordered queue. Where an order is truly required, keep the queue short.

    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Sending one message in several small writes. Header then body, length then payload. Build the message and write it once.
    • Setting TCP_NODELAY and then writing byte by byte. Every write is now a packet. Buffer in the application.
    • Hunting a 40 ms or 200 ms latency floor in the application. Those two numbers are delayed-ACK timers. Capture packets first.
    • Leaving default buffer limits on long, fast paths. Throughput is capped at buffer size over round-trip time.
    • Making every buffer huge. Queued data can't be reprioritised or cancelled, so latency rises and urgent messages wait.
    • Testing only on localhost. With no delay and no loss, none of these effects appears.

    Interview drill

    Explain it simply: What is Nagle's algorithm, and why do so many systems disable it?

    How a strong answer sounds
    Nagle's algorithm reduces the number of small packets a TCP sender emits. If the application writes less than a full segment while earlier data is still unacknowledged, the kernel holds the new data until the outstanding data is acknowledged or enough accumulates to fill a segment; if nothing is outstanding it sends immediately. It was designed for interactive terminals on slow links, where each keystroke would otherwise be a 41-byte packet. Systems disable it with TCP_NODELAY because it interacts badly with delayed acknowledgements on the receiver. A receiver holds its ACK for up to 40 ms on Linux, hoping to piggyback it on a response. If the sender does write–write–read, the second small write waits for an ACK that the receiver is deliberately delaying, and the request stalls for the length of the delayed-ACK timer. For request-and-response protocols that latency matters far more than a few saved packets, so RPC libraries, databases and web servers set TCP_NODELAY and take responsibility for batching writes themselves.

    Deeper: A service calls another in the same data centre. Median latency is 41 ms, though the callee's own timing shows 1 ms per request. What do you suspect, and how do you confirm it?

    How a strong answer sounds
    A constant extra 40 ms on a sub-millisecond path is the signature of Nagle's algorithm meeting delayed ACKs. I'd suspect that the client writes each request in two parts, typically headers and then body, or a length prefix and then the payload, on a socket without TCP_NODELAY. The first part goes out; the second is held by Nagle because the first is unacknowledged; the server can't respond because the request is incomplete, and it delays its ACK for 40 ms; when the ACK finally arrives the client sends the rest. The callee measures only from the moment it has the full request, hence its 1 ms. To confirm, I'd capture on either host and look at the timing: a small segment, a gap of about 40 ms, a bare ACK, and then the second small segment immediately after it. ss -ti on the server would show the delayed-ACK timer at 40. I'd also check for setsockopt with TCP_NODELAY in a trace of the client. The fix is to set TCP_NODELAY on the client and, better, to send each request in a single write, so that it doesn't depend on the option. I'd verify with the same capture that the gap is gone.

    🧠 Key takeaways

    • Nagle's algorithm holds a small write while earlier data is unacknowledged. Delayed ACKs hold an acknowledgement for up to 40 ms hoping to combine it with something.
    • Together, with a request sent as two small writes, each waits for the other: a stall of one delayed-ACK timer.
    • TCP_NODELAY removes Nagle, and so does writing each message in one call. With it on, batching is the application's job.
    • A latency floor of 40 ms, or 200 ms, on a fast network is a delayed-ACK timer until shown otherwise.
    • Throughput on a long path needs buffers of at least bandwidth times round-trip time. Oversized buffers add latency and hide queues.
    • Head-of-line blocking recurs at every layer where unrelated things share one ordered queue.

    Check your understanding

    With Nagle's algorithm on, an application writes 100 bytes to an idle connection, and immediately writes 100 more. What is sent, and when?

    Nothing was outstanding for the first write. The second is small and finds unacknowledged data, so it is held.

    Why does a receiver delay its acknowledgements?

    Most ACKs can be combined with something. The timer is the fallback when nothing turns up to combine with.

    A client sends each request as a 50-byte header write followed by a 200-byte body write, with default socket options on both sides. What does that cost?

    Write–write–read: Nagle holds the body, and delayed ACK holds the acknowledgement that would release it.

    After setting TCP_NODELAY, what must the application take care of?

    The kernel no longer coalesces small writes. Buffer in user space and write whole messages.

    A transfer runs over a 1 Gbit/s path with an 80 ms round trip. Roughly how much data must be in flight to use the path fully?

    The bandwidth-delay product: 125 MB per second times 0.08 seconds. Buffers and windows must allow at least that.
