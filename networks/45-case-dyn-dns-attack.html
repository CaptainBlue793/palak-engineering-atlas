The 2016 Dyn DNS Attack

On the morning of 21 October 2016, people on the east coast of the United States found that Twitter, Reddit, GitHub, Spotify, Netflix, PayPal and dozens of other sites wouldn't load. None of those companies had been attacked, and all of their servers were working. The attack was on Dyn, a company most of their users had never heard of, which answered DNS queries on their behalf. The traffic came from security cameras and video recorders in ordinary homes and shops, taken over by malware that had done nothing cleverer than try the passwords printed in their manuals. The case shows how much can depend on one provider of one service, and what it takes to survive losing it.

  • Explain how Mirai built a botnet of several hundred thousand devices
  • Describe the attack on Dyn and why it was hard to filter
  • Explain why healthy sites became unreachable, and why some lasted longer than others
  • Compare the ways of making DNS survive the loss of a provider

Mirai and IoT botnets

By 2016 there were tens of millions of small devices on the internet that were computers in everything but name: IP cameras, digital video recorders, home routers. Many ran Linux, had a remote login service enabled, and shipped with a username and password that were the same on every unit and often couldn't be changed. Nobody updated them. Mirai was malware written to collect them.

# A few of the sixty or so credentials in Mirai's list
root      xc3511        # a family of video recorders
root      vizxv         # a brand of IP camera
admin     admin
root      888888
root      default
support   support
root      juantech

The malware lived only in memory, so switching a device off and on removed it. A cleaned device still had the same password and was found again by the scanning, usually within minutes. Researchers who later measured the botnet counted about 600,000 infected devices at its peak. Its authors were three young men in the United States who had begun by attacking the servers of a rival Minecraft hosting business. In September 2016 they used it for two of the largest floods then recorded, about 620 Gbit/s against a security journalist's website and about 1 Tbit/s against a French hosting company. At the end of that month one of them published the source code, apparently to make it harder to prove who was responsible. After that, anyone could run a Mirai botnet.

The attack on Dyn

Dyn sold managed DNS. A company would delegate its domain to Dyn's name servers, and Dyn answered the world's queries for that domain from about twenty sites round the globe, reached by anycast (Chapter 26). Three weeks after the code was published, someone pointed a Mirai botnet at those name servers.

Time, UTCEvent
11:10The first wave begins, against Dyn's sites serving the eastern United States. Queries from that region start to time out
About 13:20Dyn's engineers, with their upstream providers, have filtered and re-routed enough for service to recover

Later that dayA third wave is attempted and absorbed without visible effect

Dyn's later analysis put the number of attacking devices at up to 100,000, most of them Mirai bots. The traffic was aimed at port 53, as floods of TCP and UDP packets, and included a method suited to DNS providers in particular:

What was sentWhy it is hard to stop
Floods of packets to port 53A DNS provider can't close port 53. The junk has to be separated from real queries on the same port
Queries for random names, such as x7f3k9q.customer.comEach bot asks its own ISP's resolver. No resolver has the answer cached, so every one is passed on to Dyn, and it arrives from a legitimate resolver
Retries by real resolversThese weren't part of the attack. Resolvers that got no answer asked again, and Dyn measured 10 to 20 times its normal volume of legitimate traffic on top of the flood

The second row is why blocking the attackers' addresses wasn't enough. The queries reached Dyn from the resolvers of large internet providers, the same resolvers that carried every real customer's query, and blocking them would have completed the attack. The third row is the retry storm of Chapter 44 again: once a service begins to fail, its own legitimate clients add to the load.

Why sites went down

A domain's delegation is a list of name servers. For many well-known sites in 2016, every server in that list belonged to Dyn.

$ dig +short NS twitter.com          # October 2016
ns1.p34.dynect.net.
ns2.p34.dynect.net.
ns3.p34.dynect.net.
ns4.p34.dynect.net.                  # four servers, one company, one attack

The sites didn't all vanish at once, and the reason is caching. A resolver that had looked a name up recently kept the answer for the record's TTL and went on handing it out. Only when the TTL ran out did it need Dyn again. A site whose address records had a TTL of 30 seconds, chosen so that traffic could be moved quickly between data centres, was gone almost immediately. One with a TTL of an hour faded out over the hour. Users whose resolvers were served by Dyn sites that weren't under attack, in the first wave mostly those outside the eastern United States, noticed little.

The model assumes each resolver last refreshed the record at a random moment within one TTL before the attack, so cached copies run out evenly over one TTL. It treats Dyn as completely unreachable. On the day some queries did get through, and some regions were hardly affected.

Things to notice. With a five-minute TTL and Dyn alone, half the users have lost the site after two and a half minutes and all of them after five, and it stays lost for the remaining two hours. An hour's TTL only stretches the decline over the first hour. A TTL of a day keeps over 90% of users working throughout, at the price that any change to the record takes up to a day to reach everyone, which rules it out for a site that moves traffic by DNS (Chapter 27). A second provider keeps the name resolving for everyone whatever the TTL, since a resolver that gets no reply from one name server asks the next in the list. And if resolvers are allowed to go on serving an answer they can't refresh, the outage is invisible to anyone who has visited the site before.

🧠
The target was a games network

For years it wasn't known who had attacked Dyn or why. At the time people guessed at hostile governments and at rehearsals for something larger. The answer came in December 2020, when a person who had been a juvenile at the time of the attack pleaded guilty in the United States to taking part in it. According to the Department of Justice, the aim had been to take the PlayStation Network offline. Sony's gaming service was a customer of Dyn. The attackers aimed at the name servers that answered for it, and those same servers answered for Twitter, Netflix, GitHub, Reddit, PayPal and thousands of others. Everything else that failed that day was collateral damage in an attempt to stop people playing games. Two things follow, and neither is about Mirai in particular. Attackers of this kind have small motives and large tools: the original authors wanted an advantage in the business of hosting Minecraft servers, and they produced software able to send a terabit per second. And shared infrastructure spreads the consequences of an attack on any one customer to all of them. A company that had never offended anyone was taken offline because it bought DNS from the same supplier as a games company. When choosing a provider, the other customers and the attacks they attract are part of the risk.

Secondary DNS and resilience

Many companies changed their DNS in the weeks that followed. The delegation for a domain can list name servers from more than one provider, and resolvers will use whichever answer.

$ dig +short NS github.com           # some months later
ns1.p16.dynect.net.
ns2.p16.dynect.net.
ns-1283.awsdns-32.org.
ns-421.awsdns-52.com.                # two providers, on separate networks
ApproachHow the records stay the sameGoodDifficult
One providerNothing to keep in stepSimple. Every feature of the provider is availableThe provider's bad day is yours
Primary and secondaryThe secondary copies the zone from the primary by zone transfer, whenever it changesStandard, and automatic once set upOnly plain records transfer. Changes can't be made while the primary is down
Two primariesYour own tooling writes every change to both providers through their APIsEither can be changed if the other is downYou must keep them identical, and can only use features both have
Longer TTLsNot applicableFree. Buys time in proportion to the TTLSlows every deliberate change by the same amount
Serve-stale in resolversNot applicableHides short authoritative outages completelyNot yours to configure: it is up to whoever runs each resolver

The difficulty in the middle rows is real, and it is why many sites still use one provider. Managed DNS products compete on features that go beyond plain records: answers that depend on where the user is, records that change automatically when a health check fails, load balancing by weight. None of these is standardised, so none can be copied to a second provider by a zone transfer, and a site that relies on them has to rebuild the same behaviour twice or do without it during a failover. The delegation at the registry matters as well. The NS records there have TTLs of a day or two, so a second provider has to be in the list before the attack. Adding one during an outage takes a day or two to reach resolvers.

Two other things changed after 2016. Resolver software gained the option of serving expired records when the authoritative servers can't be reached, and it was standardised in 2020. And governments began to regulate the devices: California from 2020 and the United Kingdom from 2024 prohibit selling connected devices with a universal default password.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Counting name servers and calling it redundancy. Four servers at one provider fail together. Count the independent providers and networks.
  • Very short TTLs everywhere "for flexibility". They also set how long you survive an authoritative outage: seconds.
  • Planning to add a second provider when needed. The delegation takes a day or two to change, so it can't be done during the outage.
  • Two providers whose zones have drifted apart. Resolvers pick either one, and some users get old records.
  • Depending on one provider's proprietary routing features without a plan for running without them.
  • Forgetting the dependencies of your dependencies. Your payment processor, login provider and CDN each have DNS too.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: In 2016 an attack on Dyn took Twitter offline, though Twitter's servers were never touched. How?

How a strong answer sounds
Dyn was Twitter's authoritative DNS provider: the NS records for twitter.com pointed only at Dyn's name servers. A Mirai botnet, made of cameras and video recorders compromised through default passwords, flooded those name servers with traffic on port 53, including queries for random subdomains relayed through ordinary ISP resolvers, which made it hard to filter. Dyn's servers couldn't answer legitimate queries. Recursive resolvers that still had twitter.com cached kept serving it until the TTL expired, then tried to refresh it, got no response from any of the listed name servers, and returned SERVFAIL. With no address, browsers and apps couldn't connect, even though Twitter's own servers were up and reachable by IP. It affected every site that used Dyn as its only DNS provider, and it was regional, because Dyn used anycast and the first wave targeted the sites serving the US east coast. The lesson is that DNS is a dependency of everything, and redundancy within one provider isn't independence.

Deeper: How would you make a service's DNS survive the loss of its DNS provider, and what are the trade-offs?

How a strong answer sounds
The main measure is to delegate the domain to name servers from two independent providers, on different networks and ideally different anycast footprints, so resolvers fail over between them by themselves. The work is keeping the two zones identical. One option is primary–secondary with zone transfers: simple and standard, but only plain records transfer, and if the primary is the one that is down you can't make changes. The other is to treat both as primaries and push every change to both from your own tooling, infrastructure as code calling both APIs, with a check that compares what the two actually serve. The trade-off is features: geo-routing, health-checked failover, weighted records and apex aliases are proprietary, so I'd either restrict myself to what both support, implement the same policy in each, or move that logic out of DNS into anycast or a global load balancer. DNSSEC with two signers needs extra care. The second lever is TTLs: longer TTLs on records that rarely change, especially NS records, buy survival time, at the cost of slower changes, so I'd pick them per record instead of using 60 seconds everywhere. I'd also remember that the delegation itself has a long TTL, so the second provider must be in place beforehand; I'd test by actually disabling one provider; and I'd audit third-party dependencies, because my login provider's DNS can take me down as well.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Mirai built its botnet by logging in to cameras and recorders with factory-default passwords. It needed no exploit.
  • The attack flooded a DNS provider's name servers, partly through legitimate resolvers, which made it hard to filter.
  • Sites that listed only Dyn's name servers became unreachable although their own servers were healthy.
  • Cached records kept each site alive for one TTL and no longer, so short TTLs failed first.
  • Name servers from two independent providers keep a domain resolving, at the cost of keeping two zones identical.
  • Retries by legitimate clients and resolvers multiplied the load, as they do in most outages.
