Packet-Level Debugging

When two teams each say the fault is on the other side, a packet capture settles it. Logs record what a program believed happened. A capture records what was sent and received, with a timestamp on every packet, and a handful of patterns in it account for most network problems: a SYN that is never answered, a segment sent twice, a window of zero, a reset, a long silence. The earlier chapters explained each mechanism. This one is about method: where to capture, how to read the output, and how to tell from the numbers on the lines which machine is responsible.

  • Follow a fixed order of questions when a connection fails or is slow
  • Read a line of tcpdump output field by field
  • Recognise retransmissions, and tell loss from reordering and from a lost ACK
  • Work out which device sent a reset
  • Measure round-trip time and server time from a capture

A debugging checklist

A connection goes through the same stages every time, and it fails at one of them. Asking the questions in order stops you from studying retransmissions on a connection whose real problem was a slow DNS lookup.

# Headers only, one conversation, written to a file to study later
$ sudo tcpdump -i eth0 -n -s 96 -w api.pcap 'host 203.0.113.50 and port 443'

# An intermittent fault: keep the last ten 100 MB files, and stop when it has happened
$ sudo tcpdump -i eth0 -n -s 96 -C 100 -W 10 -w ring.pcap 'port 5432'

# Reading it back: only the packets that open or abort connections
$ tcpdump -n -r api.pcap 'tcp[tcpflags] & (tcp-syn|tcp-rst) != 0'

One warning before trusting the lines. A capture on a host is taken above the network card, and the card does part of TCP's work (Chapter 34). Outgoing segments can appear tens of kilobytes long, because the card splits them afterwards, and with "incorrect" checksums, because the card fills those in afterwards too. Neither is a fault. They show that the capture was taken before the packet was finished.

Spotting retransmissions

A retransmission is a segment whose sequence range has been sent before. What caused it shows in what came just before it, and in how long the sender waited (Chapter 11).

What the capture showsWhat happenedWhat it suggests
Three or more duplicate ACKs, then the missing range, tens of milliseconds laterFast retransmit of one lost segmentOccasional loss. Normal on the internet at well under 1%
Silence for 200 ms or more, then a range sent againA retransmission timeoutThe last segments of a burst were lost, or several in a row. This is the costly kind
At the receiver, the same range arrives twiceThe data wasn't lost. The ACK was, or the sender's timer was too shortLoss or delay in the reverse direction
Duplicate ACKs, then the "missing" range arrives without being sent againReorderingPackets taking different paths. Harmless unless frequent
A SYN repeated at 1, 2 and 4 secondsThe SYN or its SYN-ACK is being droppedA firewall, a full accept queue, or a full conntrack table (Chapter 35)

Where the capture was taken changes what it can show. At the sender you see both copies of a lost segment and can't tell where it was lost. At the receiver you see the gap and then the repair. With both, you can match packets by sequence number and say exactly which ones left one end and never reached the other.

Resets and who sent them

A reset ends a connection at once, and "connection reset by peer" in a log doesn't say who the peer really was. When it arrives narrows it down:

The reset comesLikely senderLikely reason
In answer to the SYNThe destination host, or a firewall with a REJECT ruleNothing is listening on that port
Just after the handshake or the first requestThe application or a proxy in front of itIt crashed, refused the client, or has no healthy backend to pass it to
When a client reuses an idle keep-alive connectionThe serverIt had already closed the connection, and the request crossed its FIN on the way (Chapter 15)
At the first packet after a long silenceA NAT gateway, firewall or load balancerIts idle timeout expired and it forgot the connection
In the middle of a transferThe receiving host's kernelThe application closed the socket with unread data in it, or exited

Three checks tell you whether the reset came from the other end or from something in between:

  • TTL. Every packet from the real peer crosses the same number of routers, so they all arrive with the same TTL. A reset with a different TTL was sent from a different distance. tcpdump -v shows it.
  • Timing. A reply that arrives sooner than one round trip can't have come from the far end.
  • The other capture. If the server's own capture shows no reset leaving, the server didn't send one. This is the only conclusive check.

Measuring RTT from a capture

The handshake is the cleanest measurement, since no application is involved and neither side delays its reply. Timing a data segment against its ACK works too, with one caution: a receiver may hold an ACK back for up to 40 ms (Chapter 33), which looks like a slow network and is not one.

# Round-trip time of every handshake in the file, as seen from the client
$ tshark -r api.pcap -Y 'tcp.flags.syn == 1 && tcp.flags.ack == 1' -T fields -e ip.src -e tcp.analysis.ack_rtt
203.0.113.50    0.031204
203.0.113.50    0.030877
203.0.113.50    1.043150          # this SYN was sent twice: the first one was lost

Delays that are suspiciously regular are usually a timer, and each timer has a known value:

Extra delayThe timer behind itChapter
40 msA delayed ACK, usually meeting Nagle's algorithm33
200 ms and a little moreLinux's minimum retransmission timeout11
1 s, 3 s, 7 sA SYN retransmitted once, twice, three times8
5 sThe DNS resolver's timeout before it asks again10

The five-second DNS lookups in Kubernetes

From about 2017, teams running Kubernetes kept reporting the same odd fault: a small share of requests between services took five seconds longer than the rest. The extra time was always five seconds, never four or six. Application logs showed only that some calls were slow, and CPU, memory and network graphs were all normal. A delay that exact is a timer, and five seconds is the default time a Linux resolver waits before retrying a DNS query. Engineers at several companies captured packets on the affected nodes and saw the same thing. The C library sends two queries for each name, one for the IPv4 address and one for the IPv6, from the same UDP socket and within microseconds of each other. In the capture taken inside the pod, both queries left. In the capture taken on the node, after the address translation that directs them to a DNS server (Chapter 31), sometimes only one did. The resolver waited five seconds for the missing answer and asked again. The cause was a race in the kernel's connection tracking: two packets of a new flow, handled on two processors at the same moment, each tried to create the conntrack entry, and the one that lost the race was dropped. A counter, insert_failed, recorded each one. The workarounds were a resolver option that sends the two queries separately, and a DNS cache on every node so that lookups skip the translation; kernel fixes followed. The fault lay between two capture points, and it was found by comparing the two captures.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Capturing at one end only. You can see that a packet didn't arrive, but not whether it was ever sent.
  • Capturing everything. Without a filter and a snap length the file is huge and the capture itself drops packets. Check the "dropped by kernel" count when it stops.
  • Reading offload effects as faults: oversized segments and bad checksums on the sending host.
  • Blaming the network for every retransmission. A fraction of a percent is normal. Look at how many, and whether they line up with the slow requests.
  • Assuming a reset came from the far end. Check its TTL and timing.
  • Ignoring exact delays. 200 ms, 1 s and 5 s point to particular timers.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: In a packet capture, how do you recognise a retransmission, and what does it tell you?

How a strong answer sounds
A retransmission is a TCP segment whose sequence number range has already appeared earlier in the same direction. Wireshark flags them with tcp.analysis.retransmission, and in tcpdump you see the same seq range twice. The context tells you the kind. If it follows three or more duplicate ACKs, usually with SACK blocks showing later data arrived, and comes within a round trip or so, it is a fast retransmit of a single lost segment, which is cheap. If it follows a silent gap of at least 200 ms it is a retransmission timeout, which stalls the connection and collapses the congestion window, and usually means tail loss or a burst of loss. A repeated SYN at 1, 2, 4 seconds means the handshake is being dropped. It also matters where I captured: at the sender I see both copies and can't tell where the loss was; at the receiver, if the same data arrives twice, the data wasn't lost at all, the ACK was. A low rate, well under 1%, is normal; I'd care when retransmissions correlate with the slow requests.

Deeper: About one API call in a hundred is slow. You can run tcpdump on both the client and the server. How do you find the cause?

How a strong answer sounds
First I'd characterise the slowness, because the distribution is a clue: if the slow calls cluster at a fixed extra delay, 200 ms, 1 s, 3 s or 5 s, it is a timer, a retransmission timeout, a SYN retry or a DNS retry, and I know what to look for. Then I'd capture on both ends with a ring buffer and a tight filter, headers only, and log request IDs with timestamps on the client so I can find the slow calls in the capture. For a slow call I'd walk the stages. Was there a DNS lookup, and how long did it take? Was a new connection opened, and did the SYN need retransmitting? How long did TLS take? Then the request: when did its last byte leave the client, when was it acknowledged, and when did the first byte of the response arrive? If the ACK is prompt and the response late, the time is in the server application, and the server-side capture will confirm the request arrived on time. If the request itself was retransmitted, or the response arrived at the client long after it left the server, it is the path, and comparing the two captures by sequence number shows which packets were lost and in which direction. I'd also look for zero windows and for resets. One slow call rarely proves anything, so I'd check that the pattern holds across several.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Work through a connection in order: DNS, handshake, TLS, request, response, transfer, close.
  • Most diagnoses come from three things on tcpdump's lines: gaps in the timestamps, repeated sequence numbers, and flags.
  • Capture at both ends when you can. The difference between the captures is what the network did.
  • A retransmission after duplicate ACKs is cheap, one after a silence is a timeout, and data arriving twice means the ACK was lost.
  • A reset's timing and TTL show whether the far end sent it or a device on the way.
  • The handshake gives the round-trip time, and the wait for a response, less one round trip, is the server's own time.
