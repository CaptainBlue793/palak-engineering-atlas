TCP Reliability: ACKs & Retransmission

TCP promises that every byte arrives, over a network that promises nothing. The mechanism is simple to state: the sender keeps a copy of everything it has sent until the receiver acknowledges it, and sends it again if no acknowledgement comes. All the difficulty is in one question, which is how the sender knows a segment is lost and not just slow. Wait too long and a single lost packet stalls the application for seconds. Decide too quickly and the sender floods the network with copies of data that was about to arrive. This chapter covers the two ways TCP detects loss, the timer and the duplicate acknowledgement, and why the last packet of a response is the most expensive one to lose.

Cumulative ACKs and duplicate ACKs

From Chapter 8: an acknowledgement number means "I have everything before this byte; send this one next". It is cumulative. The receiver can't acknowledge byte 5,000 while byte 3,000 is missing, however much has arrived after the gap. So when a segment is lost and later ones keep arriving, the receiver has only one thing to say, and it says it again for every segment that arrives: the same acknowledgement number as before. Those repeats are duplicate ACKs.

A duplicate ACK carries two pieces of information. Something is missing at that position, and something after it got through, so the path is still working. The sender has two ways to act on loss, and they differ enormously in speed.

The retransmission timeout and RTT estimation

The timer must be longer than a round trip, or every segment would be resent before its acknowledgement could arrive. But round-trip times vary from path to path by a factor of a thousand, and on one path from moment to moment. So TCP measures. Each acknowledgement gives a sample of the RTT, and the sender keeps a smoothed average and a measure of how much the samples vary:

# RFC 6298, run on every RTT sample r (in seconds)
def on_rtt_sample(r: float) -> float:
    global srtt, rttvar
    if srtt is None:                                  # the first sample
        srtt, rttvar = r, r / 2
    else:
        rttvar = 0.75 * rttvar + 0.25 * abs(srtt - r)   # how jumpy is the path?
        srtt = 0.875 * srtt + 0.125 * r               # smoothed RTT: 1/8 of each new sample
    return max(RTO_MIN, srtt + 4 * rttvar)            # RTO: the average plus a safety margin

The margin of four times the variation is what stops a jittery path from causing needless retransmissions. Three more rules complete it. Before any sample exists, the timeout is 1 second, which is why a lost SYN costs a full second. Linux applies a 200 ms minimum to the margin, so a connection with an 80 ms round trip has a timeout of about 280 ms, and none is ever below 200. And each time the timer fires for the same segment the timeout doubles: 1, 2, 4, 8 seconds and so on. If the network is so congested that retransmissions are being lost, sending them faster would make it worse.

Fast retransmit

Waiting 200 ms or more for one lost packet on a path with a 20 ms round trip is a long stall. Duplicate ACKs give a much earlier signal, and fast retransmit acts on it: after the third duplicate ACK for the same position, the sender resends that segment immediately. The threshold is three, not one, because packets are sometimes merely reordered on the way, and a segment that arrives one or two places late also produces a duplicate or two.

Notice the condition hidden in that rule. Three duplicate ACKs need three segments to arrive after the lost one. If the lost segment is among the last three of a response, there aren't enough, and the sender is back to waiting for its timer.

Selective acknowledgements

A cumulative acknowledgement reports only the first gap. If two segments are lost from one burst, the sender learns of the second only after it has repaired the first, a round trip later, and so on for a third. SACK fixes that. Both sides agree to it in the handshake (the sackOK option in Chapter 8's capture), and from then on a duplicate ACK also lists the ranges of bytes that have arrived beyond the gap:

IP 93.184.216.34.443 > 10.0.0.5.51000: Flags [.], ack 2001, win 501, options [nop,nop,TS val 911 ecr 815,nop,nop,sack 2 {3001:6001}{7001:10001}], length 0
#   "I need byte 2001 next. I already hold 3001–6000 and 7001–10000."   So 2001–3000 and 6001–7000 are missing: resend both now.

      Why the tail matters most

      🧠
      Google measures where the time goes

      Around 2012, engineers at Google examined the TCP behaviour of billions of web responses leaving their servers. Most transfers saw no loss at all. About one in ten did, and those took roughly five times longer to complete than the rest. The reason wasn't the amount of loss, which was small. It was how the loss was repaired. Web responses are short: a few segments, often fewer than ten, and then the sender falls silent. When the lost packet is at or near the end of such a burst, there is nothing behind it to trigger duplicate ACKs, and well over half of all the losses they saw were being repaired by the retransmission timer, which was typically many times the round-trip time. A user on a 50 ms path waited hundreds of milliseconds for one packet. Their fix was small. If the last segments of a burst haven't been acknowledged after about two round trips, the sender transmits one more packet, a tail loss probe, which is either the missing data itself or enough to draw the duplicate or selective ACK that starts fast recovery. A later change, RACK, detects loss by time, treating a segment as lost if one sent after it has been acknowledged and enough time has passed, instead of by counting three duplicates. Both are in Linux and on by default, and both are now internet standards.

      For anyone building services, the consequence is that tail latency on a lossy network is set by timeouts, not by bandwidth. A request that fits in two packets and loses one of them can't be rescued by duplicate ACKs. That is one reason mobile clients on poor links see occasional requests that take a second longer than the rest, and one of the reasons connection reuse matters: an established connection has a measured RTT and a short timeout, where a new one starts with the 1-second default.

      ⚠️
      Common mistakes
      • Thinking TCP acknowledges each packet separately. ACKs are cumulative: one can cover many segments, and one lost ACK usually doesn't matter because the next covers it.
      • Reading duplicate ACKs in a capture as a fault at the receiver. They are the receiver reporting a gap, which is its job.
      • Assuming a loss costs one round trip. At the tail of a burst, or on a new connection, it costs a timeout: 200 ms to a second or more.
      • Setting application timeouts below TCP's own. A 500 ms request timeout with a retry on a new connection can pile a second handshake on top of a recoverable loss.
      • Disabling SACK or timestamps as a "tuning" step. Both make loss recovery faster and more accurate. Old advice to switch them off is obsolete.
      • Blaming bandwidth for stalls. Check the retransmission and timeout counters first.
      🎤
      Interview drill

      Explain it simply: How does TCP detect and recover from a lost packet?

      How a strong answer sounds
      The sender keeps every segment until it is acknowledged, and acknowledgements are cumulative: they name the next byte the receiver expects. There are two detection mechanisms. The first is a retransmission timer on the oldest unacknowledged segment. Its length, the RTO, is computed from measured round-trip times as the smoothed RTT plus four times the variation, with a floor of 200 ms on Linux, and it doubles on each consecutive expiry. The second is faster. When a segment is lost but later ones arrive, the receiver keeps sending the same acknowledgement number, so the sender sees duplicate ACKs. After three of them it retransmits the missing segment immediately, without waiting for the timer; that is fast retransmit. With selective acknowledgements the receiver also reports which later ranges it holds, so the sender can resend every gap at once instead of one per round trip. The timer remains as the fallback when there aren't enough later segments to generate duplicates.

      Deeper: A service on a mobile network has a median latency of 80 ms, but about 1% of requests take over a second. Packet loss is around 1%. Connect the two.

      How a strong answer sounds
      Small requests and responses are a handful of segments. If a segment in the middle of a long transfer is lost, the segments behind it generate duplicate ACKs and it is repaired in about a round trip. But when the lost segment is the last one, or the only one, nothing follows it, so no duplicate ACKs come back, and the sender has to wait for its retransmission timer. On an established connection that is at least 200 ms; if it's the SYN or the first data on a new connection, the timeout is the initial one, a full second, which matches the "over a second" tail. So roughly the loss rate's worth of requests pay a timeout. I'd confirm it from the servers' TCP counters, comparing timeouts with fast retransmits, and by capturing a slow request and looking for the gap before a retransmission. To improve it: reuse connections so there is a measured RTT and a short timeout, make sure tail loss probes and SACK are enabled, consider QUIC for mobile clients, and add hedged or retried requests at the application level with care, only for idempotent calls.

      🧠 Key takeaways

      • TCP resends what isn't acknowledged. Acknowledgements are cumulative, so a gap makes the receiver repeat the same number: duplicate ACKs.
      • The retransmission timeout is the smoothed RTT plus four times its variation, at least 200 ms on Linux, and it doubles each time it fires.
      • Three duplicate ACKs trigger fast retransmit, repairing a loss in about one round trip.
      • SACK tells the sender every range that has arrived, so several losses are repaired together.
      • Fast retransmit needs later segments. A loss at the tail of a burst waits for the timer, which is why short transfers suffer most.
      • Tail latency on a lossy path is made of timeouts. Count them before blaming bandwidth.

      Check your understanding

      Segments 1 to 6 are sent and segment 3 is lost. What does the receiver send when segments 4, 5 and 6 arrive?

      Acknowledgements are cumulative. With a gap at 3, every later arrival produces the same ACK again: a duplicate.

      Why does fast retransmit wait for three duplicate ACKs and not act on the first?

      A segment that is merely a little late shouldn't be resent. Three duplicates make loss the likely explanation.

      A connection's smoothed RTT is 80 ms with very little variation. Roughly what is its retransmission timeout on Linux?

      The margin is four times the variation, with a 200 ms floor. One second is only the starting value before any measurement.

      The final segment of a short HTTP response is lost. Why does the client wait so much longer than one round trip for it?

      Fast retransmit depends on later arrivals. At the tail there are none, which is what tail loss probes were added for.

      Two segments from the same burst are lost. What does SACK change?

      Without SACK the second gap is revealed only by the ACK that follows the first repair, one round trip later.
