Latency, Bandwidth & Throughput

"We upgraded to a 1 Gbit/s line and the app is no faster." That sentence is common because bandwidth and latency are different things that people call "speed". Bandwidth is how many bits per second a link can carry; latency is how long one bit takes to get there. Most web traffic is many small exchanges, each waiting a full round trip, so it's bound by latency, and no amount of bandwidth shortens the speed of light. This chapter gives you the four delays, the round trip as the unit of cost, and the bandwidth-delay product that explains why one TCP connection can't fill a fast, long link.

Four kinds of delay

Every packet's one-way trip is the sum of four delays, added up at every hop:

DelayWhat it isFormula / sizeExampleHow to reduce it
PropagationTime for the signal to travel the distancedistance ÷ ~200,000 km/s in fibre: 5 µs per kmLondon → New York, ~5,600 km: 28 ms one way, at bestMove closer: CDNs, edge regions. Nothing else works.
TransmissionTime to push all the packet's bits onto the linkpacket size × 8 ÷ bandwidth1,500 bytes at 100 Mbit/s: 120 µs; at 10 Gbit/s: 1.2 µsFaster links

Light in glass travels at about two-thirds of its speed in a vacuum, roughly 200,000 km per second. That's the useful number to remember: 1 ms per 200 km, one way. Real cables don't follow great circles, so measured round trips are typically 1.3–1.6× the theoretical minimum: London to New York is about 56 ms in theory and 70–75 ms in practice. Queuing is the delay that varies: an idle path and a congested one can differ by hundreds of milliseconds, a problem called bufferbloat when oversized buffers keep queues long.

Bandwidth is not latency

Think of a network path as a pipe. Bandwidth is its width: how many bits enter per second. Latency is its length: how long one bit takes to come out the other end. Doubling the bandwidth makes a big file arrive sooner; it doesn't make the first byte arrive any sooner.

Most application traffic is a conversation, not one big transfer, and each step of the conversation costs a full round-trip time (RTT): there and back. Before an HTTPS request can even be sent, a new connection pays one RTT for the TCP handshake (Chapter 8) and one for the TLS 1.3 handshake (Chapter 17); the request and response take a third. A 2 KB API response over a fresh connection from London to Virginia is therefore about 3 × 80 ms = 240 ms, of which transmitting the 2 KB takes well under a millisecond on any modern link. Mike Belshe's 2010 measurements for Google showed the same thing for page loads: beyond about 5 Mbit/s, more bandwidth barely helped, while every cut in RTT helped almost linearly.

Throughput and the bandwidth-delay product

Throughput is what you actually get: useful bytes delivered per second. For a big transfer over TCP, it's limited by the smallest of three things: the link's bandwidth, the receiver's window, and the sender's congestion window. The window limit is simple but surprising. TCP may only have one window of unacknowledged data in flight, and it takes an RTT for acknowledgements to come back, so:

throughput ≤ window ÷ RTT

Turn it around and you get the bandwidth-delay product (BDP): bandwidth × RTT, the number of bytes that must be in flight to keep the pipe full. A 1 Gbit/s path with an 80 ms RTT has a BDP of 109 × 0.08 ÷ 8 = 10 MB. The original TCP window field is 16 bits, a maximum of 64 KB, which on that path allows 65,535 × 8 ÷ 0.08 ≈ 6.5 Mbit/s: under 1% of the link. That's why window scaling exists (Chapter 12) and why "long fat networks" need large socket buffers.

def bdp_bytes(bandwidth_bps: float, rtt_s: float) -> float:
    return bandwidth_bps * rtt_s / 8

def max_throughput_bps(window_bytes: int, rtt_s: float) -> float:
    return window_bytes * 8 / rtt_s

>>> bdp_bytes(1e9, 0.080) / 1e6          # MB in flight to fill 1 Gbit/s at 80 ms
10.0
>>> max_throughput_bps(65535, 0.080) / 1e6  # Mbit/s with a 64 KB window
6.55

$ ping -c 3 -q us-east.example.com          # measure the RTT you're paying
rtt min/avg/max/mdev = 78.912/79.304/79.877/0.402 ms

The 10 Gbit/s link that copied at 400 Mbit/s

A team replicates nightly database backups from London to a region in Virginia over a dedicated 10 Gbit/s link. A single copy job peaks at about 400 Mbit/s, and adding bandwidth changes nothing. The RTT is 80 ms, so filling the link needs 10 Gbit/s × 0.08 s = 100 MB in flight, but the copy tool's socket buffers top out at 4 MB, and 4 MB ÷ 80 ms × 8 = 400 Mbit/s exactly. Nothing is broken: the window, not the link, is the bottleneck. The fixes all raise bytes in flight: larger socket buffers (net.ipv4.tcp_rmem/tcp_wmem maximums), a tool that uses many parallel TCP streams (cloud storage CLIs and rclone do), or both. The same arithmetic explains why a laptop on a fast connection downloads quickly from a nearby CDN and slowly from a server on another continent.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Calling bandwidth "speed". It's capacity. A request–response API is usually latency-bound.
  • Measuring only server time. 5 ms in the handler can be 250 ms for the user three round trips away.
  • Expecting one TCP stream to fill a long, fast link. Throughput ≤ window ÷ RTT; size buffers to the BDP or use parallel streams.
  • Ignoring queuing. A link at 95% utilisation can add more delay than the distance does.
  • Forgetting that latency compounds. Ten sequential calls between services at 2 ms each is 20 ms; across regions at 80 ms each it's 800 ms.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What's the difference between latency and bandwidth, and which matters more for a web page?

How a strong answer sounds
Bandwidth is capacity, bits per second; latency is delay, how long one bit takes to arrive, dominated over distance by the speed of light in fibre, about 5 µs per km. A web page is many small requests, each needing round trips: DNS, the TCP and TLS handshakes, then requests whose responses reveal more requests. Transmitting a 20 KB resource takes 1.6 ms at 100 Mbit/s, but each round trip to a server 80 ms away costs 80 ms whatever the bandwidth. So beyond a few Mbit/s, page load is latency-bound, and the big wins are fewer round trips (connection reuse, HTTP/2, TLS 1.3) and shorter ones (CDNs, edge servers).

Deeper: Why can't a single TCP connection fill a 10 Gbit/s transatlantic link, and what would you do about it?

How a strong answer sounds
TCP can only have a window's worth of unacknowledged data in flight, and acknowledgements take an RTT to return, so throughput is at most window ÷ RTT. At 10 Gbit/s and 80 ms RTT the bandwidth-delay product is 100 MB, so the window would have to be about 100 MB; default socket buffer maximums are a few MB, and congestion control must also grow the window that far without loss, and after any loss CUBIC takes a long time to recover at that scale. I'd raise the socket buffer limits to at least the BDP, check window scaling is on, consider BBR, which doesn't treat every loss as congestion, and use parallel streams for bulk transfer.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Delay = propagation (distance, ~5 µs/km in fibre) + transmission (size ÷ bandwidth) + queuing + processing.
  • Bandwidth is capacity; latency is delay. More bandwidth doesn't make the first byte arrive sooner.
  • Protocols cost round trips: TCP handshake, TLS handshake, then each request. Count them.
  • Small request–response traffic is latency-bound; big transfers are bandwidth- or window-bound.
  • BDP = bandwidth × RTT: the bytes in flight needed to fill a path. Throughput ≤ window ÷ RTT.

Check your understanding

What's the minimum one-way propagation delay over 2,000 km of fibre?

About 5 µs per km (200,000 km/s): 2,000 × 5 µs = 10 ms one way, 20 ms round trip, before any queuing or route detours.

How long does it take to transmit one 1,500-byte packet onto a 100 Mbit/s link?

1,500 × 8 = 12,000 bits ÷ 100,000,000 bits/s = 0.00012 s = 120 µs.

A connection's window is fixed at 64 KB and the RTT is 100 ms. What's the maximum throughput, whatever the link speed?

Throughput ≤ window ÷ RTT = 65,536 × 8 bits ÷ 0.1 s ≈ 5.2 Mbit/s. The link could be a thousand times faster and it wouldn't matter.

A page makes 30 small sequential requests to a server 100 ms away over a 50 Mbit/s connection. Which change helps most?

30 round trips × 100 ms = 3 s of waiting; transmitting a few KB each takes microseconds. Only fewer or shorter round trips move the total.

What is the bandwidth-delay product of a 1 Gbit/s path with a 40 ms RTT?

10⁹ bits/s × 0.04 s = 4 × 10⁷ bits = 5 × 10⁶ bytes. TCP needs about 5 MB in flight to keep this path full.
