DNS in Depth: DNSSEC, GeoDNS & Failover

Chapter 10 treated DNS as a lookup service: ask for a name, get an address. In production it does three more jobs. It is a traffic director, giving different answers to different users so that each reaches a nearby or healthy data centre. It is a failover switch, the thing you change when a region dies. And it is a target, because whoever can forge its answers can send your users anywhere. Each of those has its own mechanism, and each is limited by the same fact from Chapter 10: every answer is cached, by machines you don't control, for as long as its TTL says. This chapter covers signing DNS answers, steering traffic with them, failing over through them, and hiding the questions from the network.

  • Describe how an authoritative server gives different answers by location, latency or weight
  • Work out how long a DNS failover takes from the health check and the TTL
  • Distinguish DNSSEC from encrypted DNS, and say what each protects

DNSSEC: a chain of trust

A classic DNS answer is a UDP datagram with nothing to prove where it came from. A resolver accepts the first reply that matches its question's 16-bit ID and source port. In 2008 Dan Kaminsky showed how to win that guessing game reliably and plant a false record in a resolver's cache, so that everyone using the resolver would be sent to an attacker's server for a chosen name. Resolvers were hurriedly patched to randomise their source ports, which makes the guess much harder. It doesn't make the answer verifiable.

DNSSEC does. The owner of a zone signs its records, and a resolver checks the signatures. It adds four record types:

RecordHoldsLives in
RRSIGA signature over one set of records, with a validity periodThe zone, next to the records it signs
DNSKEYThe zone's public keysThe zone
DSA hash of the child zone's keyThe parent zone, next to the NS records that delegate to the child
NSEC / NSEC3A signed statement of which names exist, so that "no such name" can be proved tooThe zone

The DS record is what joins zones into a chain, in the same way that certificates chain to a root (Chapter 17):

$ dig +dnssec www.example.com
;; flags: qr rd ra ad; QUERY: 1, ANSWER: 2          # ad = "authenticated data": the resolver validated the chain
www.example.com.   300  IN  A      93.184.216.34
www.example.com.   300  IN  RRSIG  A 13 3 300 20261105000000 20261015000000 31406 example.com. oJB1W6WNGv+ldvQ3…

$ dig +short DS example.com @a.gtld-servers.net             # the link held by the parent
31406 13 2 3490A6806D47F17A34C29E2CE80E8A999FFBE4BE…

$ dig www.dnssec-failed.org | grep status                    # a zone signed wrongly on purpose, for testing
;; ->>HEADER<<- opcode: QUERY, status: SERVFAIL

Be clear about what this buys. DNSSEC proves that an answer is what the zone's owner published. It encrypts nothing, so anyone on the path can still read the question and the answer. And it adds a way to break your own domain: let a signature expire, publish a key that doesn't match the DS record, or remove the signatures while a DS record still says they should be there, and every validating resolver, which is roughly a third of the world's users, refuses to resolve the name.

🧠
Slack, September 2021: a rollback that the caches didn't follow

On 30 September 2021 Slack began signing slack.com with DNSSEC, the last step of a careful rollout that had already covered its other domains. The DS record went into the .com zone, and almost at once some users reported that they couldn't resolve Slack's names. The cause was a subtle fault in how their DNS provider answered for a wildcard record under DNSSEC, though that wasn't known yet, and the on-call engineers did what incident training says to do: undo the last change. They had the DS record removed from .com and switched signing off. That turned a small problem into a large one. Resolvers all over the world had already fetched the DS record, and it had a TTL of 24 hours. For as long as a resolver held it, the resolver believed slack.com was a signed zone. It now received answers with no signatures at all, which is precisely what a forgery looks like, and returned SERVFAIL. Slack was unreachable for a large share of users for most of a day, until the cached DS records expired, and the engineers had no means of making other people's resolvers forget them. Their own account of the incident is unusually clear on the point. In DNS, a rollback changes what you publish and doesn't touch what has been cached, and with DNSSEC the old cached state doesn't merely give a stale answer: it invalidates the new one.

GeoDNS and latency routing

An authoritative server is a program, and nothing obliges it to give everyone the same answer. A managed DNS service can choose its answer by looking at who is asking:

PolicyThe answer depends onUsed for
GeolocationThe country or region of the resolver's addressLegal and language requirements: users in Germany get the Frankfurt site
Latency-basedMeasured latency from the resolver's network to each regionSpeed: each user gets the region that is fastest for them
WeightedChance, in proportions you setShifting 5% of traffic to a new region or a new version
FailoverHealth checks on the primarySwitching to a standby when the primary stops answering
Multi-valueHealth checks; several addresses are returnedSimple spreading over a few servers
 
$ dig +short api.shop.example @ns1.dns-provider.example     # asked from Frankfurt
18.184.20.7                                                  # the eu-central region
$ dig +short api.shop.example @ns1.dns-provider.example     # the same question from Oregon
44.230.11.3                                                  # the us-west region

$ dig +short api.shop.example +subnet=203.0.113.0/24 @8.8.8.8   # a resolver passing on part of the client's address

The limits are the ones from Chapter 26. The server sees the resolver, not the user, unless the resolver forwards part of the client's address in the query. Location databases are sometimes wrong. And each answer is then cached for its TTL, so any steering decision is really a decision about the next few minutes of everyone behind that resolver.

Failover and the TTL trade-off

DNS failover is simple to describe. A health checker probes the primary region, and when it fails, the authoritative servers start answering with the standby's address. How long users are actually affected is the sum of two delays that people tend to underestimate: the time to notice, and the time for caches to forget.

Resolver caches are assumed to have fetched the record at evenly spread moments, so after the record changes they expire at a steady rate over one TTL. The query rate is for 100,000 resolver caches each refreshing once per TTL.

Things to notice. With a 5-minute TTL and the faster health check, nothing at all improves for the first 30 seconds, because the DNS provider doesn't yet know. After that users return steadily, half of them by 3 minutes, and the last at 5 and a half. Set the TTL to an hour and the outage for the unluckiest users is an hour long, whatever the health check does. Set it to 30 seconds and everyone is back within a minute, but the authoritative servers now answer ten times as many queries, and every user pays for a fresh lookup far more often. Now add clients that ignore the TTL: applications that resolve a name once at start-up, connection pools that never reconnect, resolvers that enforce a minimum. They stay on the dead region until someone restarts them, and no DNS setting reaches them.

That is why DNS is the slow layer of failover. It is the right tool for moving traffic between regions, where minutes are acceptable and nothing else can do the job. Within a region, a load balancer's health checks act in seconds (Chapter 24), and an anycast address moves with no TTL at all (Chapter 26).

DoH and DoT: hiding the question

One more weakness of classic DNS has nothing to do with forged answers. The query from your device to its resolver travels as plain text on UDP port 53. The café's Wi-Fi, your internet provider and anyone else on the path can read every name you look up, and can change the answers. Two protocols wrap that first hop in TLS:

Classic DNSDNS over TLS (DoT)DNS over HTTPS (DoH)
Carried inUDP (or TCP) port 53, plain textTLS on port 853Ordinary HTTPS requests on port 443
The network on the path seesEvery name, and can alter answersThat DNS is in use, and nothing of its contentHTTPS to a resolver's address, indistinguishable from web traffic
Easy for a network to blockn/aYes: block port 853No, short of blocking the resolver's addresses
Configured inThe operating system, usually from DHCPThe operating system (Android's "private DNS")Often the browser itself, bypassing the system's resolver
 
$ curl -s -H 'accept: application/dns-json' 'https://cloudflare-dns.com/dns-query?name=example.com&type=A'
{"Status":0,"AD":true,"Question":[{"name":"example.com","type":1}],
 "Answer":[{"name":"example.com","type":1,"TTL":287,"data":"93.184.216.34"}]}      # a DNS lookup that is just an HTTPS request

Encrypting the hop moves trust; it doesn't remove it. Your internet provider can no longer see your lookups, and the operator of the resolver you chose sees all of them. A company's network loses the ability to filter or log by DNS, and internal names that only the company's resolver knows stop resolving in a browser that sends its queries elsewhere. And the server name in the TLS handshake that follows is usually still visible (Chapter 17).

DNSSEC and encrypted DNS are often confused, and they solve different problems on different parts of the path:

DNSSECDoT / DoH
ProtectsThe data: an answer can be proved genuineThe conversation between a device and its resolver
AgainstForged or altered records, wherever they were introducedEavesdropping and tampering on the first hop
Hides what you look upNoYes, from the local network
Set up byThe owner of each zone, and resolvers that validateThe user's device or browser, and the resolver
If the resolver itself liesDetected, if the device validatesNot detected
⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Counting only the TTL in a failover plan. Add the detection time, and the clients that never look the name up again.
  • Leaving a long TTL on a record you may need to switch. Records used for failover want 30 to 60 seconds. NS and MX records can be long.
  • Rolling back DNSSEC by deleting things. Cached DS records outlive the change. Follow the documented order, and wait out each TTL.
  • Letting signatures or keys expire. An expired signature is an outage for every validating resolver. Automate signing and monitor it.
  • Thinking DNSSEC gives privacy, or that DoH gives authenticity. One signs the data and the other encrypts one hop.
  • One DNS provider for everything. If it is down, every name you own is gone, however healthy your servers are (Chapter 45).

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What does DNSSEC do, and what doesn't it do?

How a strong answer sounds
DNSSEC lets a resolver verify that a DNS answer is exactly what the zone's owner published. Each set of records in a signed zone has an RRSIG signature made with the zone's private key. The public key is published as a DNSKEY record, and the parent zone holds a DS record containing a hash of that key, signed by the parent's own key. That continues up to the root, whose key is built into validating resolvers, so there is a chain of trust from the root down to the record. If every signature checks out, the answer is authentic; if any fails or has expired, a validating resolver returns SERVFAIL. It also covers non-existence, through signed NSEC records. What it doesn't do: it provides no confidentiality, since queries and answers still travel in the clear; it doesn't protect the hop between a device and its resolver unless the device validates itself; and it doesn't protect against a compromised zone or registrar. Operationally it adds a new failure mode, because a signing or key-rollover mistake makes the domain unresolvable for everyone who validates.

Deeper: You fail over between two regions by changing a DNS record with a 60-second TTL. During a drill, some clients are still hitting the old region twenty minutes later. Why?

How a strong answer sounds
The TTL only bounds how long well-behaved resolvers cache the record, and several things sit outside that. First, detection: nothing moves until the health check has failed enough times to flip the record, so that time comes before any TTL starts to matter. Second, many clients don't re-resolve. A long-lived process may resolve the name once at start-up; HTTP clients and database drivers keep pooled connections open to the old address and never look the name up again while those connections work or hang; some runtimes cache DNS results much longer than the TTL by default; and some ISP resolvers enforce a minimum TTL of several minutes. Browsers add their own short cache. If the old region is degraded and not dead, existing connections stay up and those clients never get a reason to reconnect. So I'd find which clients they are from the old region's logs. The fixes are on the client side: bound connection lifetime so that pools re-resolve periodically, make sure runtimes honour TTLs, and on failover actively close connections in the old region so that clients are forced to reconnect. For a faster and more deterministic switch I'd move the decision out of DNS, to an anycast address or a global load balancer.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • DNSSEC signs record sets and chains each zone's key to its parent with a DS record, up to the root, so answers can be verified.
  • A validation failure is a SERVFAIL, not a fallback. Expired signatures and mismatched keys take a domain offline for validating users.
  • Authoritative servers can answer by location, latency, weight or health. They see the resolver, and their choice is cached for a TTL.
  • DNS failover takes the detection time plus up to one TTL, and never reaches clients that don't look the name up again.
  • Short TTLs make changes fast and cost more queries and more dependence on your DNS provider.
  • DoT and DoH encrypt the hop from device to resolver. That is privacy from the local network, and it is separate from DNSSEC.

Check your understanding

What does DNSSEC guarantee about an answer that validates?

Authenticity and integrity of the data. Not confidentiality, and nothing about the destination.

Which record, held in the parent zone, links a signed zone to the chain of trust?

The parent signs the DS record, and the child's DNSKEY must match the hash in it.

A zone's signatures expire because the signing job failed. What do users of a validating resolver see?

An invalid signature can't be told apart from an attack, so the resolver refuses to answer.

A failover record has a TTL of 300 seconds. The health check needs 30 seconds to declare the primary down. Roughly when have all well-behaved clients moved to the standby?

Detection first, then up to one full TTL for the last cache to expire.

A browser uses DNS over HTTPS to a public resolver. Who can still see which names the user looks up?

The hop to the resolver is encrypted, and the resolver itself reads every question. Trust has moved, not vanished.
