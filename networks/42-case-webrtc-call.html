A WebRTC Video Call

A video call asks two things of the network that a web page never does. The two machines have to send packets straight to each other, although both sit behind routers built to refuse unsolicited traffic and neither knows its own public address. And the data has to arrive within about a fifth of a second or it is useless, so waiting for a lost packet to be sent again is usually not an option. WebRTC is the set of protocols built into every browser to meet both demands. This case study follows one call from the click on "Join" to the picture appearing: how the two ends find each other, how they find a path, and how audio and video are carried over a network that loses and delays packets.

Signalling

Alice and Bob each open a page for the same meeting. Before either browser can send the other a packet, they have to exchange some facts: which codecs each supports, which addresses each might be reachable at, and a fingerprint of the key each will use. They can't send these to each other, since they have no path yet. So they send them through a server both can already reach. This exchange is signalling. WebRTC deliberately doesn't define it, and most applications use a WebSocket to their own server (Chapter 21).

// In Alice's browser. `signal` is the application's own WebSocket.

ICE candidates

Neither browser knows in advance which address the other can reach it at, so each collects every address that might work. These are candidates, and the procedure for gathering and testing them is ICE, Interactive Connectivity Establishment.

CandidateWhat it isHow it is foundWorks when
HostThe machine's own address and a UDP portRead from the network interfacesBoth are on the same network
Server reflexiveThe public address and port the NAT gave that socketAsked of a STUN server, which reports where the question came fromThe NATs allow hole punching (Chapter 9)
RelayAn address on a TURN server, which forwards to and from the browserAllocated by asking the TURN serverAlmost always, at a cost
 
# Alice's candidates, as they appear in signalling
a=candidate:1 1 udp 2122260223 192.168.1.23 54400 typ host
a=candidate:2 1 udp 1686052607 198.51.100.7 40211 typ srflx raddr 192.168.1.23 rport 54400
a=candidate:3 1 udp 41885695   203.0.113.80 61002 typ relay raddr 198.51.100.7 rport 40211

The large number is a priority. Host candidates are preferred to reflexive ones, and those to relays. Each browser pairs every candidate of its own with every one of the peer's, and sends a STUN request on each pair, in priority order, from both ends at once. Sending from both ends at once is what makes a hole in each NAT. The best pair that gets an answer in both directions is chosen, and the media uses it.

        "Ordinary" means a NAT that gives a socket the same public port whatever the destination, as most home routers do. A symmetric NAT gives a different port for each destination, as many mobile carriers and corporate gateways do. Some home routers filter more strictly than the model here and need the relay in the mixed case too.

        Things to notice. On the same network the host candidates connect and the call never leaves the building. Between two ordinary NATs the reflexive candidates work, by hole punching, and the media goes directly from one home to the other. With one symmetric NAT the direct path can still be found, because the first check that gets through reveals the port the symmetric side is really using. With two, neither side can learn the other's port in time, every direct check fails, and the call falls back to the relay. A firewall that blocks UDP stops even STUN, so that end has no reflexive candidate at all, and the only path is a relay reached over TCP on port 443, which looks like HTTPS. The call connects in every case. The relay exists to make sure of that.

        STUN and TURN

        The two servers have very different costs. A STUN server answers one small question per call and never sees the media. A TURN server carries the whole call: every audio and video packet in both directions goes to it and out again, for as long as the call lasts.

        STUNTURN
        DoesTells a browser its public address and portForwards media between the two browsers
        TrafficA few packets when the call startsAll of the call: 1 to 3 Mbit/s each way for video
        Cost to runNext to nothing. Public ones are freeBandwidth in proportion to use, so it needs credentials
        Effect on the callNoneA detour through the relay, which adds delay unless it is near the path
        Needed forEvery call between different networksThe calls where direct paths fail, commonly one in five to ten

        The relay doesn't weaken the encryption, because the keys are agreed between the two browsers and the relay forwards packets it can't read.

        🧠
        Discord: relaying every call on purpose

        Discord carries voice for millions of people at once and is built on WebRTC, and it does not let its users connect to each other directly at all. Every packet of audio goes through Discord's own media servers. In 2018 its engineers described the result: 2.6 million people in voice at the same moment, more than 850 voice servers in 13 regions, and 220 Gbit/s of traffic leaving them. They gave two reasons for paying for all of that bandwidth. The first is that a direct connection gives each participant the other's IP address, and in a community of gamers who are sometimes each other's opponents, an exposed address invites a DDoS attack on a home connection (Chapter 36). Through a server, users see only Discord's addresses. The second is group size. With direct connections every participant sends a copy of their audio to every other, so a channel of twenty people means nineteen uploads from each machine. With a server in the middle each client sends one stream, and the server forwards it to those who should hear it. This kind of server is a selective forwarding unit, and nearly every group-call product uses one. With every client connecting to a server that has a public address, the ICE procedure nearly always succeeds on the first try. Direct connection remains the cheapest design for a call between two people, and products built for groups or for privacy choose the relay.

        SRTP and jitter buffers

        Once a pair of candidates is chosen, the browsers run a DTLS handshake over it, which is TLS adapted for datagrams. Neither has a certificate signed by an authority. Each made a key pair for this call and sent the fingerprint of its certificate in the offer or answer, and now checks that the certificate presented in the handshake matches the fingerprint it was told. The call is therefore as trustworthy as the signalling channel that carried the fingerprints. The keys that come out of the handshake are used for SRTP, which encrypts the contents of every media packet.

        Each media packet is an RTP packet: a sequence number, a timestamp saying when the sound or frame was captured, and about 20 ms of audio or part of a video frame. The receiver sends back periodic reports of how many packets were lost and how irregularly they came, and the sender uses these to choose its bitrate, lowering the video quality when the path is struggling. What the receiver does about a missing packet depends on how long there is to wait:

        TechniqueHow it worksUsed when
        ConcealmentThe decoder fills the gap with a guess based on the sound just before itAlways, for audio. One lost 20 ms packet is usually inaudible
        Forward error correctionEach packet also carries a low-quality copy of the previous oneLoss is frequent. Costs extra bandwidth all the time
        Retransmission on requestThe receiver asks for the missing packet againThe round trip is short enough for the copy to arrive in time, mostly for video
        A new key frameThe receiver asks for a complete picture to restart the video fromToo much was lost for the decoder to continue

        Packets that do arrive don't arrive evenly. The sender emits one every 20 ms, and after crossing queues in several networks they come in at intervals of 5, 40, 12 and 31 ms. That variation is jitter. Played as they came, the audio would stutter. So the receiver holds packets briefly in a jitter buffer and plays them out at a steady 20 ms rhythm, slightly behind real time. A deeper buffer absorbs more irregularity and adds more delay. A packet that arrives after its turn to be played is thrown away, and counts as lost although the network delivered it.

        Stage, one wayTypical time
        Capture and encode25 ms, including one 20 ms frame of audio
        Network10 to 150 ms, by distance (Chapter 38)
        Jitter buffer20 to 100 ms, adjusted continuously to the jitter observed
        Decode and play15 ms
        Target, mouth to earUnder 150 ms feels natural. Beyond about 300 ms people start talking over each other

        This budget is why a call can't use TCP. A retransmission after a timeout takes at least 200 ms, which is more than the whole budget, and TCP would hold every later packet back while waiting (Chapter 16). The call does better to play a guess for 20 ms and carry on. It is also why a call relayed over TCP through a strict firewall, the last case in the panel, sounds worse than the others whenever a packet is lost.

        ⚠️
        Common mistakes
        • Shipping without a TURN server. It works in the office and on home broadband, and fails for the users on strict networks.
        • Offering TURN only over UDP. The networks that need a relay most are the ones that block UDP. Offer TLS on port 443 as well.
        • Thinking the signalling server carries the call. It carries the introduction. Its load and the media's are unrelated.
        • Sending signalling over an insecure channel. Whoever can alter the fingerprints can sit in the middle of the call.
        • A full mesh for a large group. Uploads grow with every participant. Past four or five people, use a forwarding server.
        • Measuring quality by bitrate alone. Delay and loss are what people notice. Read the receiver's reports.

        Interview drill

        Explain it simply: What are STUN and TURN servers for?

        How a strong answer sounds
        They solve the problem that two devices behind NAT can't simply connect to each other. A STUN server lets a client discover its public address: the client sends it a UDP packet, and the server replies with the source address and port it saw, which is the mapping the client's NAT created. The client shares that with the peer, and if both sides send to each other's public mapping at the same time, each NAT sees outgoing traffic and lets the other's packets in. That is hole punching, and it gives a direct peer-to-peer path. It fails when the NATs assign a different port per destination, symmetric NATs, or when a firewall blocks UDP. A TURN server is the fallback: both clients connect out to it and it relays all the media between them. That always works, since each client only makes an outbound connection, even over TCP or TLS on port 443, but the provider pays for the bandwidth and the path is longer. ICE is the procedure that gathers all of these options as candidates, tests them in priority order, and uses the best one that works, so the relay is only used when it has to be.

        Deeper: Users report that video calls work well from home, and connect but have poor audio from their office. How would you investigate?

        How a strong answer sounds
        "Connects, with poor quality, from one network" suggests the call is finding a path, but a bad one. I'd look at which candidate pair was selected, in the browser's WebRTC internals page or from the statistics API the application can collect. My first guess is that the office firewall blocks outbound UDP, so no server-reflexive candidate is gathered and all direct checks fail, and the call falls back to TURN over TCP or TLS on port 443. Media over TCP suffers from head-of-line blocking: one lost packet stalls everything behind it until it is retransmitted, which the jitter buffer experiences as bursts of late packets, heard as gaps and robotic audio. The detour through the relay adds delay as well, more if the nearest TURN server is far away. I'd confirm with the statistics: candidate type relay with protocol TCP, jitter and packets discarded as late, round-trip time. If that is the cause, the fixes are to ask the office to allow UDP to the media and TURN address ranges, which is the usual documented requirement for conferencing products; to make sure TURN servers are deployed close to users; and, if the office uses a proxy that inspects TLS, to check that it isn't interfering. If instead the selected pair is direct UDP and quality is still poor, I'd look at loss and jitter on the office network itself: congested Wi-Fi, or a link without quality-of-service marking for real-time traffic.

        🧠 Key takeaways

        • Signalling carries session descriptions and candidates through the application's own server. The media takes a different path.
        • ICE gathers host, server-reflexive and relay candidates, tests the pairs, and uses the best one that works.
        • STUN reveals a public mapping and costs almost nothing. TURN relays the whole call and costs bandwidth.
        • Direct paths fail between two symmetric NATs and where UDP is blocked. A relay over TLS on port 443 connects nearly everywhere.
        • DTLS agrees keys between the browsers, authenticated by fingerprints sent in signalling, and SRTP encrypts the media.
        • A jitter buffer trades delay for smoothness, and a packet that arrives too late is as good as lost.

        Check your understanding

        What does the signalling server carry during a WebRTC call?

        It introduces the two browsers. The media flows directly between them, or through a TURN relay.

        What is a server-reflexive candidate?

        The browser can't see its own NAT mapping, so it asks a server outside what address its packet came from.

        In which case does a call have to use a TURN relay?

        A relay is the fallback for when no direct pair of candidates passes its checks.

        An audio packet arrives 90 ms late, after the moment it was due to be played. What does the receiver do with it?

        For real-time media, late is the same as lost.

        Why not make the jitter buffer very deep, so that no packet is ever late?

        The buffer trades smoothness for delay, so it is kept as shallow as the measured jitter allows.
