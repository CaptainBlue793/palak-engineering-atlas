Internet Latency Budgets

A server that answers in 20 ms can still deliver a response that takes half a second to arrive. The rest of the time is spent on the network, and nearly all of it in round trips: one to find the address, one to open the connection, one or two to set up encryption, and one for the request itself. Each costs the full distance there and back, and the distance has a floor set by the speed of light in glass that no engineering removes. A latency budget is the sum of these parts for one request. Writing it out shows which parts can be removed, which can only be shortened by moving closer, and how little of the total is usually the server's own time.

  • Count the round trips before the first byte of a response, for each protocol and connection state
  • Estimate the least possible round-trip time between two places
  • Choose the technique that removes a given round trip
  • Measure each phase of a real request with curl -w
  • Write a latency budget for a request and say where to spend effort

DNS, TCP, TLS, request: the round trips

Before a client that has never spoken to a server can read the first byte of a response, four exchanges have to complete in order. None can start until the one before it has finished.

How many round trips are needed depends on the protocol and on what the client already has:

A DNS lookup that isn't cached on the client is counted as 30 ms: a round trip to a resolver close by that has the answer cached. A resolver that has to look the name up itself takes longer. The time to download the body of the response is left out.

Things to notice. Across the Atlantic with nothing cached, TLS 1.3 and a 50 ms server, the first byte arrives after 320 ms, and 270 ms of that is the network. Making the server twice as fast saves 25 ms. Reusing an open connection saves 190 ms. On the far side of the world the same cold request takes 920 ms, and an open connection brings it to 330 ms. In the same city the round trips nearly vanish and the 30 ms DNS lookup is the largest network cost, so caching the address matters most there. With a 300 ms server over a short distance the bar is nearly all grey, and none of this chapter's techniques will help: the work is in the application. The budget tells you which of these situations you are in before you start optimising.

Speed of light in fibre

A round trip can't be shorter than the distance allows. Light in glass travels at about 200,000 km/s, two-thirds of its speed in a vacuum, which is 5 µs per kilometre one way and so 1 ms of round-trip time for every 100 km (P8). Real paths are slower than that floor, because cables follow coastlines, railways and roads instead of straight lines, traffic detours through the cities where networks interconnect, and every router adds a little.

The last row is a reminder that the user's own access link often adds more than a thousand kilometres of fibre does. And since nothing makes a round trip to Sydney faster, the only two options are to make fewer round trips, or to answer from somewhere nearer: an edge location (Chapter 26) or another region.

🧠
Three hundred million dollars for a millisecond and a half

Prices on the futures exchange in Chicago and the stock exchanges in New Jersey move together, and a trading firm that learns of a price change in one city before its competitors can trade on it in the other. By the late 2000s the fastest firms were all limited by the same thing: the fibre between the two, which followed railway lines through Pennsylvania and took about 14.5 ms for the round trip. In 2010 a company called Spread Networks finished a new cable, laid in secret at a reported cost of $300 million, that ran as straight as its builders could make it, cutting through the Allegheny mountains instead of going round them. It was more than a hundred kilometres shorter and brought the round trip down to about 13 ms. Firms paid millions of dollars a year each for that 1.5 ms. Within two years the cable had been beaten by chains of microwave towers. Radio through air travels at very nearly the speed of light in a vacuum, half as fast again as light in glass, and towers can stand in a straighter line than any trench. The microwave round trip was under 9 ms, against a physical limit for the distance of about 7.9 ms. Nothing in this story involved a faster computer or a better protocol. The firms had already removed every round trip they could, and what was left was the length of the path and the speed of the signal along it. The same floor applies to an ordinary web request.

Between services the same saving comes from holding on to a client object. This is one of the most common avoidable costs in back-end code:

import requests, time

URL = "https://api.example.com/orders/42"      # 80 ms away, 50 ms of server time

t = time.perf_counter()
for _ in range(20):
    requests.get(URL)                          # a new TCP and TLS handshake every time
print(f"new connection each time: {time.perf_counter() - t:.2f} s")

t = time.perf_counter()
with requests.Session() as s:                # one pooled connection, kept alive
    for _ in range(20):
        s.get(URL)
print(f"one session:              {time.perf_counter() - t:.2f} s")

# new connection each time: 5.80 s      20 × (3 round trips + 50 ms)
# one session:              2.76 s      one handshake, then 19 × (1 round trip + 50 ms)

Measuring with curl -w

curl records a timestamp at the end of each phase and will print them. Each value is the time since the start, so the phases are the differences between them.

$ curl -so /dev/null -w '
  dns        %{time_namelookup}
  tcp        %{time_connect}
  tls        %{time_appconnect}
  first byte %{time_starttransfer}
  total      %{time_total}\n' https://api.example.com/orders/42

  tcp        0.110480
  tls        0.190903

  total      0.324610

The trick in the fourth row is the useful one. The TCP handshake has told you the round-trip time, so subtracting it from the wait gives the time the server spent, measured from outside without any access to the server. Run the command from where your users are, since the figures from a machine in the same data centre describe a path nobody uses. Browsers expose the same phases to JavaScript through the Navigation Timing and Resource Timing interfaces, so they can be collected from real users.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Optimising the server when the time is on the network. Write the budget first.
  • Opening a new connection for every request between services, by creating a client object inside the request handler.
  • Measuring from next to the server. A 1 ms round trip hides every cost in this chapter.
  • Looking only at the average. Cold connections and lost packets are in the slowest few percent, and those are what users remember.
  • Chains of redirects. http:// to https:// to www. is three full requests, each with its own handshakes.
  • Preconnecting to everything. Each unused connection costs the server and the client a handshake for nothing.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How many round trips does it take to get the first byte of an HTTPS response from a server you have never contacted?

How a strong answer sounds
After the DNS lookup, three with today's defaults: one for the TCP handshake, one for the TLS 1.3 handshake, and one for the HTTP request and the start of the response. With TLS 1.2 the handshake took two round trips, making four. With HTTP/3 the transport and TLS handshakes are combined in QUIC, so it is two. The DNS lookup adds at least one more round trip, though to a nearby resolver, not to the server. On a returning visit, TLS 1.3 session resumption with 0-RTT lets the request go with the first TLS flight, giving two over TCP or one over QUIC. And on a connection that is already open, it is one round trip regardless of protocol, which is why keep-alive and connection pooling matter so much. To turn that into time, multiply by the RTT: at 80 ms across the Atlantic a cold TLS 1.3 request costs about 240 ms of network time before the server does any work.

Deeper: Users in Australia say your API, hosted in Virginia, is slow. Server-side latency is 30 ms at the median. What do you do?

How a strong answer sounds
I'd start by measuring from Australia with curl -w, or from real-user timing data, to get the breakdown. The round trip from Sydney to Virginia is around 200 to 230 ms, so I'd expect to find that a cold request costs three round trips, about 650 ms, plus 30 ms of server time: the server is under 5% of it. So the first thing is to stop paying for handshakes: check that clients reuse connections, that keep-alive timeouts are long enough, that TLS 1.3 and session resumption are enabled, and consider HTTP/3. The second is to shorten the handshake round trips by terminating TCP and TLS at an edge location in Australia, through a CDN or a global load balancer, which keeps warm connections back to Virginia; then the user's handshakes cost a few milliseconds each and only the request itself crosses the Pacific. That gets a cold request from roughly 680 ms to about 250. To go below one long round trip, the data has to be nearer: cache responses at the edge where they are cacheable, and for the rest consider a read replica or a full deployment in an Asia-Pacific region, which brings in consistency trade-offs. I'd also look at the client's behaviour, how many sequential calls it makes per screen, because five sequential calls is five round trips whatever I do, and batching or parallelising them may matter more than anything on the server side.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Time to first byte is mostly round trips: DNS, TCP, TLS, then the request, each costing the full distance there and back.
  • A first visit costs 4 round trips with TLS 1.2, 3 with TLS 1.3 and 2 with HTTP/3. An open connection costs 1.
  • Fibre gives about 1 ms of round trip per 100 km at best, and real paths take half as long again or more.
  • Reusing connections is the largest and cheapest saving. Edges shorten the round trips that remain.
  • curl -w splits a request into its phases, and the wait less one round trip is the server's own time.
  • Write the budget before optimising, so that effort goes where the time is.
