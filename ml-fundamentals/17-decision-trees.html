Decision Trees

A decision tree predicts by asking a short sequence of yes-or-no questions about the features: is the customer's spend above £120? If so, have they logged in fewer than twice this week? Each answer leads to another question, until a leaf gives the prediction. The questions are not written by anyone; the algorithm chooses, at each step, the single question that best separates the training examples.

Trees handle mixed feature types, need no scaling, capture interactions and thresholds naturally, and can be printed as rules a person can read. Their weakness is that a tree grown until it is perfect on the training data memorises it. This chapter covers how splits are chosen, how depth and leaf size control the tree, pruning, and reading the result; the simulator grows a real tree on two features, split by split.

Choosing a split

At each node, the algorithm (CART, in scikit-learn) considers every feature and every threshold between consecutive values, and asks: if I split here, how much purer are the two children than the parent? Purity is measured by an impurity function of the class proportions in a node. The chosen split maximises the decrease in impurity, weighted by how many rows go each way. Then it repeats on each child, greedily, without ever reconsidering an earlier split.

   Gini     =  1 − Σ pₖ²             0 for a pure node, 0.5 for a 50/50 binary node
   Entropy  =  − Σ pₖ log₂ pₖ        0 for a pure node, 1 bit for 50/50

   decrease =  impurity(parent) − (n_left/n) · impurity(left) − (n_right/n) · impurity(right)

   e.g. parent 50 churn / 50 stay: Gini 0.5
        left 40 / 10, right 10 / 40: Gini 0.32 each   →   decrease 0.5 − 0.32 = 0.18

Gini and entropy almost always choose the same splits; Gini is slightly cheaper to compute and is scikit-learn's default. For regression trees the impurity is the variance of the target in the node, and each leaf predicts its mean.

  • Depth 1, then 2. One question on logins gives 79.8% on new customers. Depth 2 adds two splits and the accuracy does not move at all: read the rules, and both leaves on the left predict churn, both on the right predict stay. The splits made the leaves purer without changing a single prediction, the same quirk as the real tree in the listing below.
  • Depth 3. 86.6% validation accuracy, the best for this data: eight leaves form a rough staircase along the diagonal.
  • Depth 12, one row per leaf. 59 leaves, 99.7% training accuracy, 79.5% validation: tiny rectangles around noisy customers, and worse than depth 3.
  • Minimum 10 rows per leaf, then depth 12 again. Validation stays at 86.6% however deep you go: the leaf-size limit stops the tree from isolating noise. At 30 rows per leaf it cannot even form the staircase, and falls back to 79.8%.
  • Gini or entropy. On this data they build the same trees.

Controlling the tree

Grown without limits, a tree keeps splitting until every leaf is pure, which on noisy data means a leaf for nearly every mislabelled example: zero training error and a jagged, high-variance model (Chapter 11). Three controls stop that.

ControlEffectTypical values
max_depthCaps the number of questions on any path3–10 for a single tree
min_samples_leafNo leaf may hold fewer rows; stops splits that isolate a few points5–50, or a fraction of the data
ccp_alpha (cost-complexity pruning)Grows the full tree, then removes branches whose impurity gain does not justify their sizeChosen by cross-validation from cost_complexity_pruning_path
max_leaf_nodesGrows best-first up to a number of leavesAn alternative to depth
 
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier, export_text

tree = DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=2, random_state=0).fit(X_train, y_train)   # breast-cancer data
print(export_text(tree, feature_names=list(X_train.columns), decimals=2))
# |--- worst perimeter <= 106.10
# |   |--- worst concave points <= 0.13  →  class: 1 (benign)
# |   |--- worst concave points >  0.13  →  class: 0
# |--- worst perimeter >  106.10
# |   |--- worst concave points <= 0.14  →  class: 0
# |   |--- worst concave points >  0.14  →  class: 0   (a split that purifies without changing the answer)

# test accuracy by depth: 1 → 0.888, 3 → 0.916, unlimited → 0.902 with 100% training accuracy

Notice the last two leaves: both predict class 0. The split still lowered impurity, because one side became purer, so the greedy algorithm took it even though no prediction changed. Reading the printed tree is how you notice things like that, and it is one of the few models a domain expert can audit line by line.

Strengths and limits

StrengthsLimits
No scaling needed; monotone transforms change nothingBoundaries are axis-aligned: a diagonal becomes a staircase
Captures thresholds and interactions automaticallyHigh variance: a small change in the data can change the whole tree
Handles mixed feature types; some implementations handle missing valuesCannot extrapolate: predictions outside the training range are flat
Readable as rules when shallowGreedy: a good split that only pays off two levels later is never found
Fast to train and to predictA single tree is rarely the most accurate model

The right-hand column is why trees are rarely used alone. Averaging many trees trained on resampled data cancels much of their variance (random forests, Chapter 18); adding shallow trees one after another, each correcting the last, reduces bias (gradient boosting, Chapter 19). Both are the strongest general-purpose models for tabular data.

🚑
Use case: a tree on a laminated card

One of the best-known decision trees in medicine came from Leo Breiman, Jerome Friedman, Richard Olshen and Charles Stone's 1984 book Classification and Regression Trees, which introduced CART. Among its examples was a rule for identifying heart-attack patients at high risk of dying within 30 days, based on work at the University of California, San Diego: a tree of three questions, about minimum systolic blood pressure over the first 24 hours, age, and whether the heart rate was abnormally fast. Short enough to remember, it was the kind of model a clinician could apply at the bedside without a computer, and check against their own judgement. That combination, an accurate-enough rule that people can read, question and carry in their heads, is still the reason to fit a shallow tree today, even when a forest or boosted model will be the one deployed.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Growing a tree without limits and reporting its perfect training accuracy.
  • Interpreting a deep tree's structure as meaningful; refit on a bootstrap sample and it changes.
  • Reading impurity-based feature importances as truth; they favour features with many split points (Chapter 18).
  • Scaling features for a tree, which does nothing.
  • Expecting a tree to extrapolate a trend beyond the training range (Chapter 3's time-series example).
  • Tuning depth on the test set instead of with cross-validation.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How does a decision tree decide where to split?

How a strong answer sounds
At each node it tries every feature and every candidate threshold, and for each split computes how impure the two resulting groups are, using Gini impurity or entropy for classification and variance for regression. It picks the split with the largest decrease in impurity, weighted by the number of rows on each side, then repeats on each child. It is greedy: it never revisits an earlier split. Growth stops when a node is pure or a limit is reached, such as maximum depth or minimum rows per leaf, and each leaf predicts the majority class or the mean of its rows.

Deeper: A single decision tree has 100% training accuracy and 81% validation accuracy. How do you improve it, and when would you stop using a single tree altogether?

How a strong answer sounds
That is overfitting: the tree has grown until its leaves are pure, memorising noise. I would constrain it, choosing max_depth, min_samples_leaf or, often best, cost-complexity pruning with ccp_alpha, all by cross-validation, and watch the validation curve rather than training accuracy. That usually closes much of the gap. But a single tree's main weakness is variance: small changes in the data change its structure, and constraining it trades that for bias, so its accuracy ceiling is limited. If I need the best accuracy on tabular data, I would move to a random forest, which averages many decorrelated trees to cut variance, or gradient boosting, which builds shallow trees sequentially to cut bias, and keep a shallow single tree only if interpretability as explicit rules is the requirement, perhaps as a surrogate that explains the bigger model.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • A tree asks yes/no questions on single features; each split is chosen greedily to maximise the impurity decrease.
  • Gini and entropy measure node impurity and usually pick the same splits; regression trees use variance.
  • Unlimited depth means pure leaves, perfect training accuracy and overfitting; limit depth and leaf size, or prune.
  • Trees need no scaling and capture interactions, but their boundaries are axis-aligned staircases.
  • Single trees have high variance and cannot extrapolate; forests and boosting fix the first.
  • A shallow tree is readable as rules, which is sometimes the whole point.

Check your understanding

What is the Gini impurity of a node with 50% of each of two classes?

1 − (0.5² + 0.5²) = 0.5, the maximum for two classes. A pure node has Gini 0.

How does a decision tree choose each split?

CART evaluates every feature and candidate threshold and keeps the one with the largest weighted impurity decrease. Random splits belong to extremely randomised trees.

The true class boundary is a diagonal line. What does a decision tree draw?

Each split is a threshold on one feature, so the boundary is made of axis-aligned pieces; approximating a diagonal takes many of them.

Which change most directly reduces a deep tree's overfitting?

Requiring more rows per leaf stops splits that isolate a few noisy points. Scaling does nothing for trees, and the impurity measure rarely matters.

A tree is trained on house prices up to £800k. What does it predict for a house worth far more?

Leaves predict averages of training targets, so predictions never exceed what was seen in training: trees cannot extrapolate.
