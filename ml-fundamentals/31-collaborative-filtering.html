Recommender Basics: Collaborative Filtering

A streaming service has millions of members and tens of thousands of titles, and each member has rated or watched a few hundred at most. The question is which of the rest each member would enjoy. Collaborative filtering answers it from behaviour alone: people who agreed in the past tend to agree again, and items liked by the same people tend to be alike. No one has to describe a film's genre or a member's taste; both are inferred from the pattern of who liked what.

This chapter treats the data as a huge, mostly empty matrix of users by items, and covers the two main ways of filling it in: neighbourhood methods, which look for similar users or items, and matrix factorisation, which learns a small number of hidden taste dimensions. It also covers what changes when you only see clicks and views rather than ratings, and what to do for users and items with no history at all.

  • Predict ratings with item-based nearest neighbours

  • Handle implicit feedback, and evaluate with ranking metrics
  • Recognise and mitigate the cold-start problem

The user-item matrix and baselines

Put users on the rows and items on the columns; a cell holds a rating, or a count of plays, or nothing. Real matrices are more than 99% empty, and the empty cells are not random: people rate what they chose to watch. Before any collaborative method, fit the baseline: r̂ = μ + bᵤ + bᵢ, the global mean plus a user bias (some people rate everything high) plus an item bias (some films are liked by almost everyone). Fitted with regularisation, it is already a strong predictor, and the more sophisticated methods model what is left over.

Neighbourhood methods

User-based collaborative filtering predicts my rating of a film from the ratings of users whose past ratings resemble mine. Item-based collaborative filtering predicts it from my own ratings of films that resemble this one, where two films are similar if the same users rated them alike. Item-based is usually preferred in practice: there are fewer items than users, item similarities are more stable over time, and the recommendation comes with a natural explanation, "because you liked X". Similarities are computed on ratings after removing the baseline, and shrunk towards zero when few users rated both items.

Matrix factorisation

Matrix factorisation gives each user a vector pᵤ and each item a vector qᵢ of k hidden factors, and predicts r̂ᵤᵢ = μ + bᵤ + bᵢ + pᵤ·qᵢ. A factor might end up meaning "serious drama versus light comedy", with users scored on how much they like it and films on how much they have it, but nobody labels the factors; they are whatever best explains the observed ratings. Training minimises squared error on the observed cells only, plus an L2 penalty λ on all the parameters, which is essential because most users have few ratings. Two standard solvers are stochastic gradient descent and alternating least squares (ALS): fix the item vectors and solve a small ridge regression for each user, then fix the users and solve for each item, and repeat.

import numpy as np

def als_step(R_obs, fixed, lam):            # R_obs: list of (index, rating) per row; fixed: other side's factors
    k = fixed.shape[1]
    out = np.zeros((len(R_obs), k))
    for u, obs in enumerate(R_obs):
        idx, r = zip(*obs) if obs else ((), ())
        Q = fixed[list(idx)]                       # factors of the items this user rated
        out[u] = np.linalg.solve(Q.T @ Q + lam * np.eye(k), Q.T @ np.array(r))
    return out
# alternate: P = als_step(user_rows, Q, lam); Q = als_step(item_rows, P, lam); repeat until stable

Things to try.

  • Climb the methods. Predicting the global mean for everything gives a test RMSE of 1.014; user and film biases bring it to 0.908; item k-NN to 0.843; matrix factorisation with 3 factors and λ = 3 to 0.738. The best possible, the true model itself, is 0.515.
  • Look at item k-NN by user activity. Users with 21 to 40 ratings get 0.77; users with 3 to 5 get 0.97, barely better than the global mean, because they have few rated films to act as neighbours. That is cold start in miniature. (Each bar shows how many test ratings it covers; light users contribute few, so their numbers are noisier.)
  • Overfit the factorisation. Set k = 10 and λ = 0.01: training RMSE falls to 0.06 while test RMSE rises to 1.34, worse than the global mean. Raise λ to 3 and the same 10 factors give 0.760.
  • Over-regularise. With λ = 30, the factors shrink to almost nothing and test RMSE is 0.928, about what the biases alone achieve.
  • Vary k at λ = 3. 1 factor: 0.849; 2: 0.791; 3: 0.738; 5: 0.746. The data was generated from 3 hidden factors, and more do not help. Switch the matrix to Predictions to see every empty cell filled in.

Implicit feedback

Most systems have no ratings at all, only behaviour: plays, clicks, purchases, minutes watched. Such implicit feedback is plentiful but one-sided: a play suggests interest, but a missing play could mean dislike or simply never having seen the item. The standard treatment (Yifan Hu, Yehuda Koren and Chris Volinsky, 2008) sets a preference of 1 for any interaction and 0 otherwise, and weights each cell by a confidence that grows with the amount of interaction, so every cell, including the empty ones, enters the fit with a low weight for zeros. Evaluation changes too: what matters is the order of the recommended list, so use ranking metrics such as precision and recall at k, mean average precision or NDCG on held-out interactions, not RMSE.

Cold start

A new user has no row and a new item no column, so collaborative filtering has nothing to work with: the cold-start problem. Common remedies are to fall back to popularity or the baseline, ask new users for a few preferences during onboarding, use content features (genre, cast, text, the user's location or device) in a hybrid model, and explore deliberately by showing new items to some users to gather data. Popular items also tend to get recommended more and so collect more data, a feedback loop that narrows what users see unless it is countered.

Use case: Amazon's item-to-item recommendations

In 2003 Greg Linden, Brent Smith and Jeremy York described the recommender behind Amazon's "Customers who bought this item also bought" in IEEE Internet Computing. With tens of millions of customers and millions of catalogue items, user-based collaborative filtering was too slow: comparing each customer with every other at request time does not scale, and customer profiles change with every purchase. Their item-to-item approach moved the expensive work offline. For every item, they computed its similarity to the items that customers who bought it also bought, and stored the most similar ones in a table. At request time, recommending for a customer only meant looking up the similar items for the handful of things that customer had bought or rated, so the cost depended on the size of one customer's history, not on the number of customers or items. The recommendations were fast, easy to explain, and kept working for customers with only one or two purchases. The paper became one of the most cited in the field, and in 2017 the journal named it the paper from its first twenty years that had best withstood the test of time.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Treating missing ratings as zeros or dislikes in explicit-rating data.
  • Splitting randomly when the system will predict future interactions; split by time.
  • Optimising RMSE when users see a ranked list; evaluate the ranking.
  • Too little regularisation, so users with three ratings get extreme factor vectors.
  • Ignoring cold start and popularity bias until launch.
  • Trusting offline gains without an online test; recommendations change behaviour.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How does matrix factorisation make recommendations?

How a strong answer sounds
It treats the ratings as a big table with users as rows and items as columns, where most cells are empty. It learns a short list of numbers for every user and every item, say twenty each, such that multiplying a user's numbers by an item's numbers and adding them up reproduces the ratings we do know. Those numbers act as hidden tastes and hidden traits: one might capture how much a user likes action and how much action a film has. Once learned, the same calculation fills in every empty cell, and we recommend the items with the highest predicted scores. Regularisation keeps the numbers small for users or items with little data, so the predictions do not overreact to a few ratings.

Deeper: You are building recommendations for a video app with only view events and many new videos each day. How would you approach it?

How a strong answer sounds
With only views, this is implicit feedback, so I would model preference as watched or not, with confidence from watch time, and evaluate by ranking metrics, such as recall at k and NDCG, on views held out by time. For the core model I would start with popularity and recency baselines, then implicit ALS or a two-tower model trained on user and video interactions. Because new videos arrive constantly, a pure collaborative model would never recommend them, so I would add content features, such as text, audio or visual embeddings, category and creator, in a hybrid or two-tower model, and reserve some traffic for exploration so new videos collect feedback. I would also watch popularity bias and diversity, and confirm offline improvements with online A/B tests on watch time and retention, since recommendations change the data they are later trained on.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Collaborative filtering fills in a sparse user-item matrix from patterns of behaviour.
  • Start from a regularised baseline of global mean plus user and item biases.
  • Item-based neighbours are simple and explainable; matrix factorisation learns hidden taste factors.
  • Regularisation is essential: most users and items have little data.
  • Implicit feedback needs confidence weighting and ranking metrics.
  • Cold start needs content features, onboarding and exploration.

Check your understanding

In the baseline r̂ = μ + bᵤ + bᵢ, what does bᵢ capture?

Item bias is the tendency of an item to be rated high or low overall. The personal interaction is what factors or neighbours add on top.

Why is item-based collaborative filtering often preferred to user-based?

Items change less than user profiles, catalogues are usually smaller than user bases, and "because you liked X" is a natural explanation.

You increase the number of factors k a lot and set λ near zero. What happens to held-out error?

With many parameters per user and few ratings each, the model fits noise. Regularisation, or fewer factors, is needed.

With implicit feedback such as plays, what is the main problem with treating unplayed items as dislikes?

Absence is ambiguous. Confidence weighting treats zeros as weak evidence rather than firm dislikes.

Which is a standard remedy for recommending brand-new items?

A new item has no interactions, so collaborative signals are empty. Its content can place it near similar items, and exploration gathers data.
