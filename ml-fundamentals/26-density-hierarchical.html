Density & Hierarchical Clustering

k-means needs the number of clusters in advance, assumes round clusters, and forces every point into one of them, outliers included. Two other families drop those assumptions. Density-based methods define a cluster as a crowded region separated from others by emptier space, so clusters can take any shape and isolated points are labelled as noise. Hierarchical methods build a whole tree of nested clusters, from single points up to one group, and let you choose where to cut it.

This chapter covers DBSCAN and its successor HDBSCAN, agglomerative clustering with its four common linkage rules, and how to read the dendrogram it produces. The simulator runs both in the page on shapes that defeat k-means.

DBSCAN

DBSCAN (Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise; Martin Ester, Hans-Peter Kriegel, Jörg Sander and Xiaowei Xu, 1996) has two parameters: a radius eps and a count min_samples. A point with at least min_samples points within eps of it, itself included, is a core point. Core points within eps of each other belong to the same cluster, and clusters grow by chaining through core points. A non-core point within eps of a core point is a border point and joins that cluster; everything else is noise, labelled −1.

The number of clusters comes out of the data, clusters can be any shape a chain of dense neighbourhoods can trace, and outliers are not forced into a cluster. On two interleaved half-moons, DBSCAN recovered the shapes exactly (ARI 1.00) where k-means scored 0.48. A useful way to set eps is the k-distance plot: sort every point's distance to its min_samples-th nearest neighbour and look for the knee.

from sklearn.cluster import DBSCAN, HDBSCAN

labels = DBSCAN(eps=0.3, min_samples=5).fit_predict(X_scaled)   # -1 marks noise
labels = HDBSCAN(min_cluster_size=25).fit_predict(X_scaled)     # no eps to choose

One radius for every density: HDBSCAN

DBSCAN uses the same eps everywhere, so it struggles when clusters differ in density. Take two tight blobs close together and one spread-out blob further away. With eps = 0.2, the tight blobs separate, but 97.5% of the spread-out blob is called noise; with eps = 0.6, the spread-out blob is found, but the two tight blobs merge into one. No single value gets all three. (The adjusted Rand index was a misleading 0.967 at eps = 0.2, because it treats the noise label as just another cluster, and the noise happened to be mostly one blob. Count the noise points separately.)

HDBSCAN (Ricardo Campello, Davoud Moulavi and Jörg Sander, 2013) runs DBSCAN at every eps at once, builds the tree of clusters that appear and disappear as eps shrinks, and keeps the clusters that persist longest. Its main parameter is min_cluster_size, which is much easier to choose than a distance. On the blobs above it found all three with 3% of points as noise (ARI 0.95). It is in scikit-learn as HDBSCAN since version 1.3.

Agglomerative clustering

Start with every point as its own cluster. Repeatedly merge the two closest clusters until one remains. The sequence of merges forms a tree, the dendrogram, whose leaves are the points and whose branch heights are the distances at which clusters joined. Cutting the tree with a horizontal line gives a flat clustering; a cut through k vertical branches gives k clusters.

"Closest clusters" needs a definition, the linkage:

LinkageDistance between clustersTends to produceMoons ARI
SingleThe closest pair of pointsLong chains; follows any shape, but one bridge of points merges clusters1.00
CompleteThe farthest pair of pointsCompact clusters of similar diameter0.33
AverageThe mean of all pairwise distancesA compromise between single and complete0.76
WardThe increase in within-cluster variance from mergingRound, similar-sized clusters, like k-means0.64

The last column is scikit-learn's AgglomerativeClustering with two clusters on 600 standardised moon points. Ward is scikit-learn's default and is usually the best choice for blob-like data. Agglomerative clustering needs all pairwise distances, so memory grows with n² and time faster still; it suits thousands of points, not millions.

Things to try.

  • Start with DBSCAN on Moons + noise (eps 0.05, min_samples 5). Two clusters that follow the curves, 22 noise points, ARI 0.94. Drop eps to 0.03 and the moons break into 7 fragments with 43 noise points; raise it to 0.08 and everything chains into one cluster.
  • Look at the k-distance plot. Most points have their 5th neighbour within a short distance, then the curve shoots up for the scattered noise. eps just below that knee is where the clustering works.
  • Switch to Mixed density. With min_samples 12 and eps 0.04, the two tight blobs separate and ARI reads 1.00, yet all 120 points of the spread-out blob are noise. With min_samples 5 and eps 0.06, only 14% of it is noise, but the two tight blobs merge. No eps does both.
  • Try Rings with eps 0.1. DBSCAN separates the ring from the centre exactly (ARI 1.00 at min_samples 5); no centre-based method can.
  • Switch to Agglomerative on Moons and compare linkages at 2 clusters. Single linkage: ARI 1.00. Average 0.67, complete 0.44, Ward 0.41. Ward's dendrogram even shows its biggest gap at 2 clusters: a clean-looking cut can still be the wrong shape.
  • Agglomerative on Mixed density. Every linkage, cut into 3, splits the spread-out blob before it separates the two tight ones; Ward needs 4 clusters (ARI 0.86). One cut height has the same problem as one eps.

Reading a dendrogram

The vertical position where two branches join is the linkage distance at which they merged. A long vertical gap with no merges means the clusters below it are well separated: cutting anywhere in that gap gives the same clusters, which makes it a natural choice. The horizontal order of leaves carries little meaning; any branch can be flipped without changing the tree. Two leaves next to each other may have joined only at the top. Distance between two points in the dendrogram is the height of their lowest common merge, not their position along the axis.

from sklearn.cluster import AgglomerativeClustering
from scipy.cluster.hierarchy import linkage, dendrogram, fcluster

agg = AgglomerativeClustering(n_clusters=2, linkage="single").fit(X_scaled)   # moons: ARI 1.00; ward: 0.64

Z = linkage(X_scaled, method="ward")          # the full merge tree
dendrogram(Z, truncate_mode="lastp", p=30)       # plot the top 30 merges
labels = fcluster(Z, t=4, criterion="maxclust")   # cut into 4 clusters

Use case: the heatmaps that organised the genome

In 1998 Michael Eisen, Paul Spellman, Patrick Brown and David Botstein published a method for making sense of microarray experiments, which measure the activity of thousands of genes under many conditions at once. They clustered about 2,500 yeast genes hierarchically, using average linkage and a correlation-based similarity, so genes whose activity rose and fell together over the course of the cell cycle, sporulation or stress ended up on nearby branches. Then they drew the data as a coloured grid, one row per gene and one column per condition, with the rows in dendrogram order and the tree drawn alongside. Genes with related functions, such as those making the parts of the ribosome or those involved in DNA replication, formed visible coloured blocks, each a branch of the tree. No one had to say in advance how many groups there were; biologists could read the tree at whatever level suited their question. The software, Cluster and TreeView, was given away free, and the clustered heatmap became a standard figure in genomics papers; the same pairing of dendrogram and heatmap is now a single call, such as seaborn's clustermap.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Choosing eps without looking at the data's scale or the k-distance plot; scale the features first.
  • Expecting one eps to work for clusters of very different densities; use HDBSCAN.
  • Ignoring the noise label when reporting results or computing scores.
  • Using single linkage on noisy data, where a thin bridge of points chains clusters together.
  • Reading leaf order or horizontal distance in a dendrogram as similarity.
  • Running agglomerative clustering on hundreds of thousands of points without checking memory.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How is DBSCAN different from k-means?

How a strong answer sounds
k-means needs the number of clusters up front, describes each cluster by a centre, and puts every point in some cluster, which works for round blobs. DBSCAN instead looks for crowded regions: a point with enough neighbours within a set radius is a core point, and clusters are chains of core points close to each other. So DBSCAN finds the number of clusters by itself, can find clusters of any shape, like curves or rings, and labels points in sparse areas as noise rather than forcing them into a cluster. Its weakness is that one radius has to suit every cluster, so it struggles when clusters have very different densities.

Deeper: When would you use hierarchical clustering, and how would you choose the linkage?

How a strong answer sounds
I would use it when the data has meaningful structure at several levels, such as product categories inside departments or genes in pathways, when I want to explore without fixing the number of clusters, or when the dataset is small enough, thousands of points, for the quadratic memory cost. The linkage defines what a cluster is. Ward merges whatever increases within-cluster variance least, so it makes compact, similar-sized clusters and is a good default for blob-like data. Complete linkage also favours compact clusters. Single linkage follows chains, so it can recover elongated or curved shapes but is very sensitive to noise points bridging clusters. Average is in between. I would scale the features, try two or three linkages, look at the dendrograms for a clear gap to cut at, and check that the resulting clusters are stable and make sense to people who know the domain.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • DBSCAN builds clusters from chains of dense neighbourhoods and labels sparse points as noise.
  • Its eps applies everywhere, so clusters of different densities need HDBSCAN.
  • Agglomerative clustering merges the closest clusters step by step, giving a dendrogram you can cut.
  • The linkage defines "closest": single chains, complete and Ward make compact clusters, average sits between.
  • Read dendrograms by merge height, not leaf order.
  • Check noise counts separately: ARI treats noise as one more cluster.

Check your understanding

In DBSCAN, what makes a point a core point?

Core points have dense neighbourhoods. Border points are near a core point without being dense themselves; everything else is noise.

Your data has one very dense cluster and one sparse cluster. What is the likely problem with DBSCAN?

One radius must suit both densities. HDBSCAN considers all radii and keeps the most persistent clusters.

Which linkage is most likely to chain two separate clusters together through a thin line of points?

Single linkage uses the closest pair of points, so one bridge of nearby points is enough to merge clusters.

In a dendrogram, what tells you how similar two points are?

Leaf order is partly arbitrary; the merge height is the linkage distance at which they ended up in the same cluster.

Why can a high adjusted Rand index be misleading for DBSCAN output?

If most of one true group is labelled −1, ARI sees "noise" as a cluster matching that group. Report the noise share alongside it.
