The ML Fundamentals Interview Playbook

Machine learning interviews test the same few things again and again: whether you understand why models behave as they do, whether you choose metrics and thresholds that match the problem, whether you validate honestly, and whether you can turn a vague business question into a well-posed modelling task. They rarely reward reciting formulas; they reward reasoning out loud, naming trade-offs and spotting traps.

This chapter collects the question types, a framework for open-ended case questions, an approach to take-home tasks, and the rules of thumb from this course worth having at your fingertips. The practice tool scores your answers to common questions against the kind of rubric interviewers use: what an answer must contain, what makes it strong, and what sinks it.

  • Answer concept questions with definition, intuition, example and caveat
  • Handle metric and threshold questions by starting from costs and base rates
  • Structure a case question from objective to monitoring
  • Approach a take-home task so that the reviewer sees your judgement
  • Practise against a rubric rather than by re-reading

Concept questions

"Explain overfitting", "What is regularisation?", "How does a random forest work?" A strong answer has four parts, in this order: a one-sentence definition, the intuition in plain words, a concrete example or how you would see it in practice, and a caveat or trade-off. Interviewers listen for the last two; they separate people who have used an idea from people who have read about it. Keep the first answer to about a minute, then let the follow-up questions take you deeper. Each chapter's interview drill in this course follows that pattern, with a simple and a deeper version.

Metric and threshold questions

"Which metric would you use?" is almost never answered by a metric. It is answered by questions: what is the base rate, what does each kind of error cost, who acts on the prediction and how many can they handle, are probabilities used directly? Then the metric follows: precision and recall at a threshold set by costs or capacity for alert queues, PR-AUC for rare positives, calibrated probabilities and log loss when numbers drive decisions, MAE or quantile losses when over- and under-prediction cost differently. Expect the follow-up "and the threshold?", and answer it with costs, validation data and the formula from Chapter 23.

Take-home modelling tasks

A take-home task is a work sample, and reviewers read it the way they would read a colleague's analysis. They look for a clear framing of the problem and the metric; a quick look at the data that finds its quirks; a baseline before anything clever; a leak-free pipeline with honest validation; a short error analysis; and an honest list of limitations and next steps. Polish matters less than judgement: a well-reasoned logistic regression with a careful write-up usually beats an untuned deep model with no explanation. Respect the time box, and say what you would do with more time instead of doing all of it.

Practice: build an answer

Pick a question. Choose the points you would actually make in your answer, as if speaking, then read the rubric. Essentials are what an interviewer needs to hear; strong extras lift an answer from acceptable to good; red flags are statements that would worry an interviewer, because they are wrong or show a habit that causes real failures.

Use case: why interviewers use rubrics

In 1998 Frank Schmidt and John Hunter combined 85 years of research on how well different hiring methods predict job performance. Structured interviews, in which every candidate gets the same questions and answers are scored against predefined criteria, were among the best predictors, with a validity of about 0.51, against about 0.38 for unstructured conversations; work-sample tests scored about 0.54. Large technology companies took note. Google's people-analytics team, led by Laszlo Bock, reported that brainteasers such as estimating how many golf balls fit in an aircraft were a complete waste of time, and moved to structured questions scored with rubrics. For candidates the consequence is practical: interviewers are listening for specific elements, the essentials of a good answer, and for specific warning signs. An answer that is technically right but omits the base rate, the validation scheme or the trade-off may score below a shorter one that names them. Practise saying the essentials explicitly, which is what this chapter's tool is for.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Answering "which metric?" with a metric, before asking about base rates and costs.
  • Jumping to a model in a case question before defining the target and the prediction moment.
  • Reciting definitions without an example or a trade-off.
  • Claiming certainty about a comparison without mentioning noise or validation.
  • Over-engineering a take-home and leaving no time for the write-up.
  • Not asking clarifying questions, which interviewers often expect and reward.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How do you structure your answer to "How would you build a model to predict X?"

How a strong answer sounds
I start with the decision the prediction supports and how success is measured, then pin down the target precisely and the moment the prediction is made, because that decides which data I may use. Next I list data sources and check for leakage, set a simple baseline and pick a metric that reflects the cost of errors. Then I choose a strong default model, usually gradient-boosted trees for tabular data, and a validation split that mirrors production, such as by time. After that I would look at errors by segment, and finally cover deployment: serving, monitoring for drift, retraining, and an A/B test to confirm the business impact. I say my assumptions as I go so the interviewer can redirect me.

Deeper: The interviewer pushes back: "Your model's AUC is 0.92, why not ship it today?"

How a strong answer sounds
AUC 0.92 tells me the model ranks well on the validation data, which is necessary but not sufficient. Before shipping I would want to know: that the validation split mirrored production, by time and by entity, with no leaky features, so the 0.92 is honest; how it performs at the threshold we will actually use, in precision and recall or expected cost, since AUC averages over all thresholds; that probabilities are calibrated if anyone will use them as numbers; that no important segment is badly served; and that the gain over the current system is larger than the noise in the estimate. Then I would ship it carefully: shadow mode or a limited A/B test, with monitoring and a rollback path, because offline wins do not always become online wins.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Concept answers: definition, intuition, example, caveat.
  • Metric questions start from base rates, costs and who acts on the prediction.
  • Case questions: objective, target and prediction moment, data and leakage, baseline and metric, model and validation, errors, deployment.
  • Take-homes reward framing, baselines, honest validation and a clear write-up.
  • Practise against a rubric: say the essentials, add trade-offs, avoid confident half-truths.
