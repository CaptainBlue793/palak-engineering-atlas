Missing Data & Outliers

Real tables have holes. A form field was optional, a sensor dropped out, a join found no match, a value was only recorded after some later event. Most scikit-learn models refuse to run on NaN, so every project has to decide what to do with missing values, and the decision is not just mechanical. Why a value is missing often matters more than what you fill it with.

The same is true of outliers: some are recording errors to fix, some are the rare cases the model most needs. This chapter covers the kinds of missingness, the standard ways to impute, the missing indicator that often turns a hole into a feature, and how to treat extreme values without hiding real problems. The simulator hides incomes in three different ways and compares five strategies on the same loan-default model.

  • Distinguish values missing completely at random, at random, and not at random
  • Impute with a constant, the median, or a model, inside a pipeline
  • Add missing indicators, and explain when missingness is itself a signal
  • Decide whether an outlier is an error, a placeholder or a real extreme
  • Use clipping and robust methods instead of deleting rows

Why values go missing

MechanismMeaningExampleConsequence
Missing completely at random (MCAR)Missingness unrelated to anythingA sensor drops 2% of readings at randomSimple imputation is unbiased; dropping rows only loses data
Missing at random (MAR)Depends on other observed columnsYounger applicants skip the income field more oftenImputing from the other columns recovers most of it
Missing not at random (MNAR)Depends on the missing value itselfHigh earners leave income blankEvery imputation is biased; the fact of missingness carries information

You can rarely prove which mechanism applies, but you can investigate: compare the rows with and without a value on every other column, and on the target. If missing-income applicants default at a different rate, the missingness is informative, and throwing that information away is a mistake.

Imputation

from sklearn.impute import SimpleImputer, KNNImputer
from sklearn.experimental import enable_iterative_imputer   # noqa: needed before the next import
from sklearn.impute import IterativeImputer

SimpleImputer(strategy="median", add_indicator=True)      # median fill plus a 0/1 "was missing" column
SimpleImputer(strategy="most_frequent")                     # for categorical columns
KNNImputer(n_neighbors=5)                                  # average of the 5 most similar rows
IterativeImputer(random_state=0)                          # models each column from the others, in rounds
HistGradientBoostingClassifier()                           # handles NaN natively: learns which side missing values go

Three rules make imputation safe. Fit the imputer on training data only, inside a pipeline, because the median of the full dataset includes the validation rows. Add a missing indicator whenever missingness might mean something; it costs one column and lets the model learn "income not given" as a fact in itself. And remember what imputation does to a distribution: filling with the mean piles every missing row onto one value, shrinking the variance and weakening correlations, which matters for linear models more than for trees.

Things to try.

  • At random, mean. AUC 0.703, against 0.738 with no missing values. The blue spike at the mean is 31% of applicants sharing one income. Predicting income from age recovers a little (0.717); the indicator adds nothing, because missingness says nothing here.
  • Drop rows. 0.733, which looks better, but only on the 69% of applicants who gave an income. The model cannot score anyone else.
  • Young people skip it. Now the regression on age is the best fill (0.721 against 0.703 for the mean): missingness depends on a column the model can see.
  • High earners skip it, mean. Applicants without an income default at 10.3%, against 31.4% for the rest, but the mean fill makes them look like average earners: AUC 0.682, and the average income the model sees drops from £45.0k to £35.6k.
  • Same, median plus indicator. AUC 0.729, nearly the full-data 0.738. The fact that income was hidden carries most of what the income would have said.

Outliers: error, placeholder, or the point?

An outlier is a value far from the rest, and before treating it you need to know what it is. A recording error, such as a house with 4,000 bedrooms, should be fixed at the source or set to missing. A placeholder, such as 0, −1 or 9999 for "unknown", is missing data in disguise (Prerequisite 8). A real extreme, such as a genuinely huge order, a whale customer or a fraud, may be exactly what the model is for, and deleting it removes the signal.

Use case: the lab test that was never ordered

Hospital datasets are the classic place where missingness carries meaning. A lactate test is ordered when a clinician suspects sepsis; for most patients it is never taken, so the value is missing. Imputing the population median makes every untested patient look average, and throws away the fact that a doctor saw no reason to order the test, which is itself strong evidence that the patient was less sick. Researchers building early-warning models on electronic health records have repeatedly found that the pattern of which tests were ordered, and when, can be as predictive as the results. It is also a warning: a model that learns "test ordered means sick" learns the clinicians' existing suspicion, and may add little when it is needed most, before anyone suspects anything. A missing indicator captures the signal; understanding where it comes from tells you whether to trust it.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Imputing with statistics from the whole dataset, including validation and test rows.
  • Dropping every row with a missing value, which loses data and biases the sample when missingness is not random.
  • Mean-filling without an indicator when missingness is related to the target.
  • Treating placeholders like 0 or 9999 as real values.
  • Deleting outliers automatically, including the fraud cases the model exists to find.
  • Cleaning the training data by hand in ways production data will never get.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How would you handle missing values in a feature?

How a strong answer sounds
First I would find out why they are missing and whether that matters: compare rows with and without the value on the other features and on the target. If the value is missing at random and the rate is small, median imputation for numbers or the most frequent value for categories is usually fine. If missingness is related to the target, I add a missing indicator so the model can use that fact. If it depends on other columns, a model-based imputer, iterative or k-nearest-neighbour, can recover more. Gradient-boosted trees can also handle missing values natively. Whatever I choose, the imputer is fitted on the training data only, inside a pipeline, so the same rule applies in production; and I avoid dropping rows unless the missing rate is tiny and random.

Deeper: In a credit model, applicants who leave income blank default at half the average rate. What do you do?

How a strong answer sounds
That is missingness not at random, and it is informative, so the model should see it: impute a value and add a binary "income missing" indicator, or let a tree model handle the missing value natively so it can route those applicants separately. I would not drop those rows, which would bias the training sample towards applicants who disclose income, and I would not trust a plain mean fill, which would make them look like average earners. Then I would investigate why: perhaps high earners or self-employed applicants skip the field, or a particular channel does not ask for it, in which case the indicator is really encoding the channel. That matters for stability: if the application form changes and the field becomes mandatory, the indicator's meaning disappears, so I would monitor the missing rate in production and plan to retrain. I would also check fairness implications, since the indicator could proxy for a protected group.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Ask why values are missing: completely at random, at random given other columns, or because of the value itself.
  • Median or most-frequent imputation is a reasonable default; model-based imputation helps when missingness depends on other columns.
  • Add a missing indicator whenever missingness may relate to the target; it often turns a hole into a feature.
  • Dropping incomplete rows loses data and can bias the sample.
  • Outliers can be errors, placeholders or the cases that matter most; identify them before treating them.
  • Fit every imputer and clipper on training data, inside the pipeline.

Check your understanding

High earners are more likely to leave the income field blank. Which mechanism is that?

Missingness depends on the value that is missing, income itself, so no imputation from other columns can fully recover it.

What does a missing indicator add to a model?

It lets the model learn from the fact of missingness, which is valuable whenever missing rows behave differently.

What does mean imputation do to the distribution of a feature?

Every missing row gets the same value, creating a spike at the mean and reducing the spread and the correlations with other features.

Why must the imputer be fitted on the training data only?

The median or regression learned from all rows carries information about the held-out rows, a small but real leak. Pipelines prevent it.

A fraud dataset has a handful of transactions 100 times larger than the rest, all confirmed fraud. What should you do with them?

They are real and they are exactly what the model must learn. Transform or flag the amount if a model is sensitive to scale, but keep the rows.
