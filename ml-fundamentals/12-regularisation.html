Regularisation: Ridge, Lasso & Elastic Net

A linear model with many features and not much data will happily use every feature, including the ones that only fit noise, and give large, unstable weights to correlated ones. Regularisation adds a price for large weights to the loss. The model can still use a feature, but only if the improvement in fit is worth the price.

Two prices dominate. Ridge charges the sum of squared weights and shrinks every weight smoothly towards zero. Lasso charges the sum of absolute weights and does something qualitatively different: it sets some weights exactly to zero, so it selects features as it fits. The simulator traces every weight as the price rises, for both, and shows the geometry that makes lasso produce exact zeros.

Adding a price for large weights

   ridge         minimise  ‖y − Xw‖²  +  α Σ wⱼ²                        L2: smooth shrinkage
   lasso         minimise  (1/2n)‖y − Xw‖²  +  α Σ |wⱼ|                 L1: some weights exactly 0
   elastic net   minimise  (1/2n)‖y − Xw‖²  +  α·r Σ |wⱼ|  +  ½ α (1 − r) Σ wⱼ²     a mix, r = l1_ratio

Those are scikit-learn's exact definitions, which is why ridge's α and lasso's α are on different scales: the same number means a much stronger penalty in ridge. α = 0 is ordinary least squares; as α grows, the weights are pulled towards zero and the model becomes simpler, trading a little bias for less variance. The intercept is not penalised.

Two practical rules follow from the formulas. Scale the features first. The penalty treats a unit of every weight the same, so a feature measured in pence gets a weight a hundred times smaller than the same feature in pounds and is barely penalised; standardising puts every weight on the same footing. And choose α by cross-validation, because the right strength depends on the noise and the amount of data.

from sklearn.linear_model import RidgeCV, LassoCV, ElasticNetCV
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline

# 60 rows, 10 features: 3 that matter, 1 correlated decoy, 6 pure noise; noise variance 4
ridge = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), RidgeCV(alphas=np.logspace(-2, 4, 50))).fit(X, y)
lasso = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LassoCV(cv=5, random_state=0)).fit(X, y)

Things to try.

  • Lasso, far left (α = 0.001). All ten coefficients are in use and the validation MSE, 4.60, equals plain least squares. Six of those weights are fitting noise.
  • Lasso, α = 0.1. Six coefficients survive, the correlated decoy x₂ is exactly zero, and validation MSE drops to 4.46. The best α on this data is about 0.2 (4.40).
  • Lasso, α = 1. Exactly the three true features remain, but shrunk hard (x₁ is 1.95, not 3), and validation MSE rises to 6.23: the right features with too much bias. Further right, everything goes to zero.
  • Switch to ridge. No coefficient ever reaches zero. At α = 10, watch x₂: ridge gives the decoy 0.64, taken from x₁ (now 1.88), because it prefers to share weight between correlated features. Ridge's best α here is about 2.5, at 4.56.
  • "Why lasso gives zeros". Shrink the budget: with the diamond, w₂ hits exactly 0 once the budget falls to about 1.85 and stays there. With the circle, w₂ shrinks to 0.56, then 0.21, and never reaches zero.

Why lasso gives exact zeros

Penalised fitting is equivalent to minimising the loss inside a budget: Σ|wⱼ| ≤ t for lasso, Σwⱼ² ≤ t² for ridge. The loss's contours are ellipses around the least-squares solution, and the answer is where the smallest ellipse touches the budget region. A circle is smooth, so the touching point is almost never exactly on an axis: ridge shrinks every weight but zeroes none. A diamond has corners on the axes, and an expanding ellipse very often hits a corner first: lasso lands exactly on w₂ = 0. In many dimensions the diamond has many corners and edges, which is why lasso zeroes many weights at once.

Regularisation as a prior

There is another way to read the penalty. Ridge regression is exactly what you get if, before seeing any data, you believe each weight is probably small, drawn from a normal distribution centred on zero, and then update that belief with the data. Lasso corresponds to a Laplace prior, sharply peaked at zero, which expresses "most features probably do nothing". α is how strongly you hold the belief relative to the evidence. With a lot of data the evidence wins and α matters little; with little data the prior keeps the model from believing every coincidence. This view also explains why scikit-learn's logistic regression is regularised by default (Chapter 7): it is a safe prior for small, messy datasets.

🧬
Use case: thousands of features, a few hundred rows

Robert Tibshirani introduced the lasso in 1996 in the Journal of the Royal Statistical Society, and its most natural home turned out to be problems with far more features than examples. Gene-expression studies measure tens of thousands of genes for a few hundred patients; ordinary least squares has no unique solution at all when features outnumber rows, and even ridge keeps every gene in the model. Lasso returns a model that uses a few dozen genes, which is both a better predictor on new patients and a short list a biologist can investigate. The same pattern appears in software: predicting service latency from thousands of configuration flags and metrics, or ad clicks from millions of sparse indicator features, where the Google ad-click system mentioned in Chapter 5 used an L1 penalty to keep most coefficients at exactly zero and the model small enough to serve.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Regularising unscaled features, so the penalty hits some features far harder than others for no reason.
  • Comparing α between Ridge and Lasso as if they were on the same scale.
  • Choosing α on the test set, or by eye.
  • Reading lasso's choice among correlated features as meaningful. Another sample might pick the other one.
  • Forgetting C is the inverse: in LogisticRegression and SVMs, a larger C means weaker regularisation.
  • Scaling with the full dataset before cross-validation, which leaks; put the scaler in the pipeline.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is the difference between L1 and L2 regularisation?

How a strong answer sounds
Both add a penalty on the size of the weights to the training loss, which discourages large weights and reduces overfitting. L2, ridge, penalises the sum of squared weights; it shrinks all weights smoothly towards zero and shares weight between correlated features, but rarely makes any exactly zero. L1, lasso, penalises the sum of absolute values; because that penalty has a corner at zero, the optimum often sets weights exactly to zero, so lasso also performs feature selection. I would use ridge when I expect many small effects or have correlated features, lasso when I expect only a few features to matter or want a sparse model, and elastic net when there are groups of correlated features and some irrelevant ones. In every case I would standardise features and choose the strength by cross-validation.

Deeper: You fit a lasso, and between two cross-validation folds it picks completely different features from a group of correlated ones. Is that a problem, and what would you do?

How a strong answer sounds
For prediction it may not be a problem: if the features are near-duplicates, any one of them carries the same information, and the folds' errors will be similar. It is a problem if anyone is going to interpret the selected features or act on them, because the choice is essentially arbitrary. I would check the correlation structure and the stability of selection, for example by refitting on many bootstrap samples and counting how often each feature is chosen. Then I would switch to elastic net, whose L2 part tends to keep correlated features together with shared weights, or group the correlated features into one by averaging or with a principal component, or use ridge if sparsity is not actually needed. If the goal is a short, defensible feature list, stability selection, keeping only the features chosen in most resamples, is the principled version of that check.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Regularisation adds a penalty on weight size to the loss; its strength α trades bias for variance.
  • Ridge (L2) shrinks all weights smoothly and stabilises correlated features.
  • Lasso (L1) sets some weights exactly to zero, because its penalty region has corners on the axes.
  • Elastic net mixes the two; it keeps correlated groups together while still selecting.
  • Always standardise features and choose α by cross-validation; scikit-learn's ridge and lasso α are on different scales.
  • A penalty is a prior belief that weights are small; it matters most when data is scarce.

Check your understanding

Which penalty is most likely to set some coefficients exactly to zero?

The L1 penalty's corner at zero means the optimum often lands exactly on an axis. Ridge shrinks weights but almost never to exactly zero.

Why should features be standardised before fitting a regularised linear model?

The penalty acts on weights, and a weight's size depends on its feature's units. Standardising makes the penalty fair across features.

As the penalty strength α increases from zero, what happens?

A stronger penalty pulls weights towards zero: a simpler, more stable model that fits the training data a little less well.

Two features are almost identical. How do ridge and lasso typically treat them?

Ridge spreads weight across correlated features stably; lasso tends to pick one and zero the other. Unregularised least squares is what gives large opposite weights.

In scikit-learn's LogisticRegression, what does increasing C from 1 to 100 do?

C is the inverse of the regularisation strength. A larger C means a weaker penalty and a more flexible model.
