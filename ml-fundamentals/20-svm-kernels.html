Support Vector Machines & Kernels

When two classes can be separated by a straight line, infinitely many lines do it. Logistic regression picks one by maximising likelihood. A support vector machine picks the line that stays as far as possible from the nearest points of both classes: the widest street between them. Only the points on the edge of the street, the support vectors, determine where it goes.

Two ideas turn that into one of the most influential methods of the 1990s and 2000s. A soft margin lets some points sit inside the street or on the wrong side, at a price set by C. And the kernel trick lets the same algorithm draw curved boundaries by working, implicitly, in a much larger feature space. This chapter covers both, trains SVMs in the page, and ends with where they still fit now that gradient boosting and neural networks dominate.

Maximum margin

A linear classifier predicts with the sign of f(x) = w·x + b. Scale w and b so that the closest points of each class have f(x) = +1 and −1. Those two parallel lines are the edges of the street, and its width is 2 / ‖w‖. So maximising the margin means minimising ‖w‖², subject to every training point being on the correct side of its edge: yᵢ(w·xᵢ + b) ≥ 1.

The solution has a striking property. w turns out to be a weighted sum of training points, w = Σ αᵢyᵢxᵢ, and αᵢ is zero for every point beyond the edge of the street. Only the points on the edge or inside it, the support vectors, have nonzero weight. You could delete every other training point and get exactly the same boundary.

Soft margin and C

Real classes overlap, so the hard constraint has no solution. The soft-margin SVM lets each point violate it by a slack ξᵢ ≥ 0 and pays for the total: minimise ½‖w‖² + C·Σξᵢ. That is the same as minimising the hinge loss, max(0, 1 − yᵢf(xᵢ)), plus an L2 penalty on w: the SVM is a regularised linear model with a loss that ignores points comfortably on the right side. C is the inverse of regularisation strength, as in scikit-learn's logistic regression: a small C buys a wide street that tolerates many violations; a large C narrows the street to classify the training points correctly, and overfits if pushed far.

from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.svm import SVC

svm = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), SVC(kernel="rbf", C=1.0, gamma="scale")).fit(X_train, y_train)
svm[-1].n_support_            # support vectors per class: 104 of 426 training rows on breast cancer
# test accuracy 0.958 with scaling, 0.916 without: SVMs measure distances, so scale first

Things to try.

  • Move C on the overlapping blobs with the linear kernel. At C = 0.01 every one of the 120 points is a support vector and the margin is 4.35 wide; at C = 1 there are 41 and the margin is 0.55; at C = 100, 25 and 0.37. Validation accuracy stays at 87.8–88.0% from C = 0.1 upwards: the classes overlap, so only the width of the street changes.
  • Switch to Rings. No straight line works: the linear kernel gets 63.5% with 112 of 120 points as support vectors. The degree-2 polynomial kernel reaches 94.5% at C = 1 and 95.3% at C = 10, because its feature space contains x₁² + x₂², the squared distance from the centre.
  • Try XOR. The polynomial kernel's x₁x₂ term is exactly what XOR needs: 87.8% at C = 10. RBF with gamma = 2 and C = 1 reaches 90.5%.
  • Overfit with gamma. On the blobs with RBF, C = 10: gamma = 2 gives 87.7% on new points, gamma = 120 gives 100% on training data and 78.5% on validation, with 102 support vectors. The boundary turns into islands around single points.
  • Count how few points matter. Rings with RBF, gamma = 2 and C = 10: just 19 of the 120 points are support vectors, and the model reaches 96.7% on new points.

The kernel trick

In the solution, training points only appear inside dot products: the decision function is f(x) = Σ αᵢyᵢ (xᵢ·x) + b, and training itself only needs xᵢ·xⱼ. Now map every point into a bigger feature space with φ(x), say all degree-2 products, where a curved boundary in the original space becomes a flat one. You would need φ(xᵢ)·φ(xⱼ), and for many useful maps that can be computed directly from the original vectors, without ever building φ. For two features, (x·z + 1)² expands to the dot product of φ(x) = (1, √2x₁, √2x₂, x₁², x₂², √2x₁x₂) with φ(z). A function K(x, z) that equals a dot product in some feature space is a kernel, and swapping it in for x·z is the kernel trick.

The RBF (Gaussian) kernel, K(x, z) = exp(−γ‖x − z‖²), corresponds to an infinite-dimensional feature space. In practice it behaves like a smooth similarity: the decision function is a weighted sum of bumps centred on the support vectors, and gamma sets the bump width. Small gamma gives wide bumps and a smooth, nearly linear boundary; large gamma gives narrow bumps that wrap around individual points. C and gamma interact, so tune them together on a logarithmic grid.

from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV

grid = {"svc__C": [0.1, 1, 10, 100], "svc__gamma": ["scale", 0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1]}
search = GridSearchCV(make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), SVC()), grid, cv=5).fit(X_train, y_train)
search.best_params_           # {'svc__C': 100, 'svc__gamma': 0.001} on breast cancer; test accuracy 0.958

When SVMs still make sense

Kernel SVMs store and compute with an n × n kernel matrix, and training time grows between quadratically and cubically with the number of rows. On 20 standardised features, scikit-learn's SVC took 0.14 s for 2,000 rows, 1.76 s for 8,000 and 5.2 s for 16,000, while LinearSVC stayed under 0.04 s. Beyond a few tens of thousands of rows kernel SVMs become impractical, and they do not produce probabilities without extra calibration (Chapter 32). They remain a good choice for small and medium datasets with dense numeric features, for high-dimensional data with few rows, such as genomics or spectra, and, in linear form, for sparse text, where LinearSVC is fast and strong.

📰
Use case: sorting news stories by topic

In 1998 Thorsten Joachims published a study of support vector machines for text categorisation, on the Reuters-21578 collection of newswire stories, each tagged with topics such as earnings, acquisitions or grain. A document became a vector with one dimension for each word, around ten thousand features, almost all of them zero for any given story. That is hard territory for many learners and natural territory for an SVM: in very high dimensions, classes are often close to linearly separable, the maximum-margin solution controls overfitting without needing feature selection, and only the support vectors matter. Joachims's SVMs reached a micro-averaged precision-recall breakeven point of about 86%, against roughly 82% for k-nearest neighbours and 72% for naive Bayes. For the following decade, linear SVMs on word features were a standard baseline for spam filtering, sentiment analysis and document classification, and LinearSVC on TF-IDF features is still a strong first model for text.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Not scaling features, so the largest-valued feature dominates every distance.
  • Tuning C and gamma separately, or on a linear grid instead of a logarithmic one.
  • Using a kernel SVM on hundreds of thousands of rows, where training time explodes.
  • Treating decision_function as a probability, or relying on probability=True without checking calibration.
  • Very large gamma, which turns the SVM into a lookup table of the training points.
  • Forgetting class imbalance; use class_weight="balanced" (Chapter 23).

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is a support vector machine, and what are support vectors?

How a strong answer sounds
An SVM is a classifier that draws the boundary between two classes so that the gap, or margin, to the nearest points on each side is as wide as possible. The points that sit on the edge of that margin, or inside it when the classes overlap, are the support vectors; they alone determine the boundary, and the model can forget every other training point. A parameter C controls how much the model tolerates points inside the margin: small C gives a wide, forgiving margin, large C fits the training data more tightly. With a kernel, the same idea produces curved boundaries.

Deeper: Explain the kernel trick, and how C and gamma interact for an RBF SVM.

How a strong answer sounds
The SVM's training problem and decision function depend on the data only through dot products between points. If we map points into a richer feature space where a linear boundary is enough, we need dot products there, and for many maps those can be computed directly as a kernel function of the original points, without building the features. A degree-2 polynomial kernel, for example, equals a dot product over all pairwise products of features. The RBF kernel corresponds to an infinite-dimensional space and acts like a similarity that decays with distance. Gamma sets how fast it decays: large gamma means each support vector influences only a small neighbourhood, so the boundary can wrap around individual points; small gamma gives a smooth boundary. C sets the penalty for margin violations. High C with high gamma overfits; low values of both underfit. Because they interact, I would search them together on a logarithmic grid with cross-validation, after scaling the features.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • An SVM chooses the boundary with the widest margin; only support vectors determine it.
  • The soft margin is hinge loss plus L2 regularisation; C is the inverse of regularisation strength.
  • Kernels replace dot products, giving curved boundaries without building the feature map.
  • For RBF, gamma sets the reach of each support vector; tune C and gamma together on a log grid.
  • Always scale features first.
  • Kernel SVMs suit small to medium data; linear SVMs remain strong on sparse text.

Check your understanding

Which training points determine a trained SVM's decision boundary?

Points beyond the margin have zero weight in the solution. Removing them and retraining gives the same boundary.

You increase C a lot. What tends to happen?

C is the price of each margin violation. Raising it makes violations expensive, so the model prefers a narrower margin with fewer of them.

What does the kernel trick let an SVM do?

Because the algorithm only needs dot products, a kernel function can supply dot products in a richer space directly from the original features.

An RBF SVM scores 100% on training data and poorly on validation, with a boundary that wraps around individual points. Which change is most likely to help?

Large gamma gives each support vector a tiny radius of influence, which lets the boundary wrap around single points. Lowering gamma, and usually C, smooths it.

Why are kernel SVMs rarely used on datasets with millions of rows?

Kernel methods work with pairwise similarities between rows. Linear SVMs avoid this and scale well, which is why they are still used on large sparse text data.
