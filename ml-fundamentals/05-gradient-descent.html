Gradient Descent

Linear regression has a formula for its best weights. Almost nothing else in machine learning does. Logistic regression, neural networks, and every model with millions of parameters or more data than fits in memory are trained the same way instead: start somewhere, compute which way is downhill on the loss, take a small step that way, and repeat. That loop is gradient descent.

It is simple to write and easy to get wrong. A step that is too large overshoots and the loss explodes; one that is too small takes forever; and features on different scales turn a round bowl into a long, narrow valley where every step zig-zags. The simulator draws the loss surface of a real two-parameter regression and the path gradient descent takes across it, so you can produce each of those failures and then fix them.

The update rule

Gradient descent improves the parameters a step at a time. At each step it computes the gradient of the loss with respect to every parameter, and moves each parameter a little in the opposite direction. The size of the move is the gradient times the learning rate η (eta), a small positive number you choose.

   w  ←  w − η · ∇L(w)                                repeat until the loss stops improving

   for linear regression with mean squared error:
   L(w) = (1/n) ‖Xw − y‖²          ∇L(w) = (2/n) Xᵀ(Xw − y)        the error times the input, averaged
def fit_gd(X, y, lr=0.1, steps=1000):
    w = np.zeros(X.shape[1])
    for _ in range(steps):
        grad = 2 / len(y) * X.T @ (X @ w - y)      # one pass over all rows
        w -= lr * grad
    return w

A = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(X)), X])     # intercept column, features standardised
fit_gd(A, y)                                    # matches np.linalg.lstsq(A, y) to many decimal places

Each step needs only the gradient, which is cheap: two matrix-vector products. Nothing needs to be inverted or even held in memory all at once, which is why the same loop scales to models and datasets where the normal equation is out of the question.

The learning rate

The gradient says which way is downhill, but only locally: it is the slope at this point, and the surface curves away from it. A small step trusts that slope a little; a large step trusts it a lot. Three regimes follow, and you can see all three in the simulator.

  • Too small: every step is safe, and there are far too many of them. The loss falls smoothly and very slowly.
  • About right: the loss falls quickly and settles.
  • Too large: each step overshoots the bottom of the valley and lands higher up the other side. If it overshoots by more than it gained, the loss diverges: it grows every step until the numbers overflow.

For a quadratic loss like least squares, there is an exact limit: the step must be smaller than 2 divided by the curvature in the steepest direction. The steepest direction sets the limit, and that is the root of the scaling problem below.

Batch, mini-batch and stochastic

The gradient of a loss averaged over all n rows needs a pass over all n rows. With a million rows, that is a million rows of work for one step. The fix is to estimate the gradient from a random subset, a mini-batch, typically 32 to 1,024 rows, and take many cheap, slightly noisy steps instead of a few exact ones. With a batch of one row it is called stochastic gradient descent (SGD). One epoch is one full pass over the training data, however many steps that takes.

Why scaling matters

Suppose one feature is in units of 1 and another has values five times larger. The loss changes much faster along the second weight's direction, so the bowl is a long, narrow valley instead of a round dish. The learning rate has to be small enough for the steep direction, or the steps diverge, and that same small step barely moves along the shallow direction. Gradient descent zig-zags across the valley and crawls along it. Standardising every feature to mean 0 and standard deviation 1 (Chapter 14) makes the bowl round, and one learning rate suits every direction.

from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.linear_model import SGDRegressor
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline

model = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), SGDRegressor(learning_rate="invscaling", eta0=0.01,
                                                    max_iter=1000, tol=1e-4, random_state=0))

Things to try.

  • Unscaled, η = 0.01. A smooth, cautious path that still has 7% of its starting excess loss left after 60 steps. Raise η to 0.02: it just makes it in 60 steps.
  • Unscaled, η = 0.035. 34 steps, zig-zagging across the narrow valley. This is about as fast as it gets: one notch higher, at 0.05, the steep direction overshoots and the loss explodes. The limit is 0.042.
  • Now standardise the features. The valley turns almost round (its stretch drops from 29.5 to 1.1), and η = 0.5 converges in a single step. Anything up to about 0.9 still works.
  • Standardised, η = 0.3, then mini-batches and then single rows. Mini-batches of 16 reach the minimum's neighbourhood as fast but jitter around it; single-row steps wander and still have 3.7% of the excess loss left after 60 steps, because the noise never dies down at a constant learning rate.

When to stop

Gradient descent never arrives exactly; it gets closer. Practical stopping rules are a maximum number of steps or epochs; a tolerance, stopping when the loss improves by less than some small amount over several steps (scikit-learn's tol and n_iter_no_change); and, best of all for generalisation, early stopping: hold out validation data, track its loss, and keep the weights from the step where it was lowest. Training loss always keeps falling; validation loss eventually turns up, and that turning point is where the model starts fitting noise (Chapter 11).

Use case: online learning for ad clicks at Google

In 2013 a team at Google published "Ad Click Prediction: a View from the Trenches" (McMahan and colleagues, KDD 2013), describing how they trained the logistic-regression models that predict whether a search ad will be clicked. The data arrived as an endless stream of impressions, far too much to hold or to pass over many times, so the model was updated online, one example at a time, with a variant of stochastic gradient descent called FTRL-Proximal. Two details from the paper connect directly to this chapter. Each coefficient got its own learning rate, which shrank as that feature was seen more often, so that frequent features settled while rare ones could still move: a fix for the same problem that feature scaling addresses here. And an L1 penalty drove most coefficients to exactly zero, keeping the model small enough to serve (Chapter 12). The loop was the one on this page, run at the scale of a search engine.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Adding the gradient instead of subtracting it, so the loss climbs.
  • A learning rate that diverges, showing up as a loss of inf or nan after a few steps.
  • Unscaled features, which force a tiny learning rate and very slow progress.
  • Judging SGD by one noisy step. Look at a smoothed loss over an epoch.
  • A constant learning rate with SGD, which keeps the weights jittering instead of settling.
  • Stopping on training loss alone, when validation loss is what tells you the model is still improving.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How does gradient descent work, and what does the learning rate do?

How a strong answer sounds
Gradient descent minimises a loss by repeatedly computing the gradient of the loss with respect to the parameters, which points uphill, and moving the parameters a small step in the opposite direction. The learning rate scales that step. Too small and training takes a very long time; too large and the steps overshoot the minimum, bounce around, and can diverge so the loss grows without limit. In practice I start with a sensible default, watch the training loss on a log scale for the first few hundred steps, and adjust by factors of three or ten, often with a schedule that reduces the rate over time.

Deeper: Why does feature scaling speed up gradient descent, and what would you do if you could not rescale the features?

How a strong answer sounds
When features have very different scales, the loss surface curves much more steeply along some parameter directions than others; for least squares the curvatures are the eigenvalues of XᵀX, and their ratio, the condition number, measures how elongated the valley is. A single learning rate must stay below two over the largest curvature to avoid diverging, so along the shallow directions each step makes only a tiny fraction of the progress it could, and the number of steps grows roughly with the condition number. Standardising the features makes the curvatures similar, the valley round, and one learning rate works everywhere. If I could not rescale, I would use an optimiser with per-parameter step sizes that adapt to each direction's gradient history, such as AdaGrad, RMSProp or Adam, which is effectively a learned rescaling; momentum also helps by accumulating speed along the shallow valley floor. For linear models specifically, a second-order or closed-form solver avoids the problem entirely.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Gradient descent repeats w ← w − η∇L: compute the gradient, step against it.
  • The learning rate decides everything: too small crawls, too large overshoots and diverges.
  • Mini-batches trade an exact gradient for many cheap steps; one epoch is one pass over the data.
  • SGD's path is noisy and needs a decaying learning rate to settle at the minimum.
  • Unscaled features make a narrow valley that forces tiny steps; standardise them.
  • Stop on a step budget, a tolerance, or best of all, the validation loss.

Check your understanding

What is the gradient-descent update for weights w, learning rate η and loss L?

The gradient points uphill, so the step goes against it, scaled by the learning rate. The loss value itself is not used in the step.

After a few steps the training loss is growing every step and soon becomes inf. What is the most likely cause?

Each step overshoots the minimum by more than it gains, so the loss grows geometrically: divergence. Reduce the learning rate, and check feature scaling.

One feature ranges from 0 to 1 and another from 0 to 10,000. Why does gradient descent struggle?

Different scales stretch the loss surface into a narrow valley. The learning rate is capped by the steepest direction, so progress along the shallow one is tiny.

Compared with batch gradient descent, what does stochastic gradient descent (one row per step) trade?

Each SGD step uses one row, so it is fast but its gradient is a noisy estimate; the path wanders and needs a decaying learning rate to settle.

A dataset has 10,000 rows and you use mini-batches of 100. How many steps make one epoch?

An epoch is one full pass over the data: 10,000 / 100 = 100 steps.
