Overfitting & the Bias–Variance Trade-off

Every model in this course can fail in two opposite ways. It can be too simple to capture the pattern, scoring badly on the training data and on everything else: underfitting. Or it can be so flexible that it captures the noise as well as the pattern, scoring brilliantly on the training data and worse on new data: overfitting. Almost every technique in the Intermediate level, regularisation, cross-validation, ensembles, pruning, exists to steer between the two.

The steering needs a diagnosis, and the diagnosis comes from two numbers, training error and validation error, read together. The simulator fits curves of increasing flexibility to noisy data. You can watch one fit go from a straight line to a wiggle through every point, then draw thirty fresh datasets to see bias and variance directly: how far the average fit is from the truth, and how much the fits disagree with each other.

Two ways to be wrong

Fit a straight line to data that curves, and it misses systematically: too low here, too high there, the same way every time you collect new data. That systematic error is bias. Fit a degree-15 polynomial to twenty noisy points, and it passes through every one of them, and the next twenty points give a completely different curve. That sensitivity to the particular sample is variance. The best model sits between: flexible enough to follow the real pattern, not so flexible that it follows the noise.

The decomposition

For squared error, the expected error of a model on a new example splits exactly into three parts. Imagine training the same kind of model on many independent training sets of the same size, and predicting the same new point each time:

   expected error  =  bias²  +  variance  +  noise

   bias²     how far the AVERAGE prediction is from the truth         falls as the model gets more flexible
   variance  how much predictions spread across training sets           rises as the model gets more flexible
   noise     the randomness in y itself, which no model can predict     fixed: the floor under every curve

As flexibility grows, bias falls and variance rises, so their sum, the part you control, is smallest somewhere in the middle. The noise term sets a floor: on the simulator's data no model, however good, can get below a mean squared error of 0.09, because that is the variance of the noise added to the true curve.

Things to try.

  • 20 points, degree 1. A straight line through a wave: validation MSE 0.256, far above the 0.09 floor. Underfitting.
  • Degree 3. Validation MSE 0.105, close to the floor. Now degree 8: training MSE drops to 0.030, below the noise floor, which is impossible for an honest fit, and validation rises to 0.256. The curve is fitting noise.
  • Switch to "30 fresh datasets". At degree 1, bias² is 0.165 and variance 0.007: thirty lines that agree with each other and are all wrong. At degree 3, 0.004 and 0.017. At degree 10, 0.026 and 0.292: thirty curves that each miss in their own way, wildest at the edges.
  • Same view, 400 points, degree 10. Variance falls to 0.002. More data tamed the flexible model; it did nothing for the biased straight line.
  • "Error against degree" with 20, then 400 points. With 20 the validation curve bottoms out at degree 3 and climbs; with 400 it is nearly flat from degree 3 to 10. How much flexibility you can afford depends on how much data you have.

Validation curves and learning curves

Two plots turn the diagnosis into a routine. A validation curve plots training and validation error against one complexity setting, the polynomial degree here, the tree depth or the regularisation strength elsewhere. Training error falls steadily; validation error falls, bottoms out and rises. Choose the setting at its minimum, using cross-validation (Chapter 13).

A learning curve plots both errors against the size of the training set, for a fixed model. If the two curves have converged at a high error, the model is biased and more data of the same kind will not help: change the model or the features. If there is a wide gap that is still closing, the model is variance-limited and more data will help, as will regularisation.

from sklearn.model_selection import validation_curve, learning_curve

degrees = np.arange(1, 16)
train_sc, val_sc = validation_curve(make_poly_model(), X, y, param_name="poly__degree",
                                    param_range=degrees, cv=5, scoring="neg_mean_squared_error")

sizes, train_sc, val_sc = learning_curve(model, X, y, train_sizes=np.linspace(0.1, 1.0, 8),
                                         cv=5, scoring="neg_mean_squared_error")
# plot -train_sc.mean(axis=1) and -val_sc.mean(axis=1): converged and high = bias; a closing gap = variance

Use case: when simple forecasters beat machine learning

In 2018 Spyros Makridakis, Evangelos Spiliotis and Vassilios Assimakopoulos published a comparison in PLoS ONE of machine-learning and classical statistical methods on about a thousand monthly time series from the earlier M3 forecasting competition. The flexible machine-learning methods, including neural networks, were on the whole less accurate than simple statistical ones such as exponential smoothing, and much more expensive to run. The explanation is this chapter. Each series had only a few dozen to a few hundred observations, and a model fitted to one short series has very little data to pin down many parameters: high variance. The simple methods are biased, since they assume a rigid structure of level, trend and seasonality, but on short series that bias costs far less than the variance it removes. When later competitions supplied many related series to learn from together, as in M5 (Chapter 48), flexible models had enough data, and won.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Judging a model by training error. A model that memorises scores perfectly there.
  • Collecting more data for a biased model, when the learning curve has already flattened.
  • Adding flexibility to an overfitting model because the validation error is high.
  • Tuning complexity on the test set, which overfits the choice itself.
  • Expecting zero error. Noise in the labels sets a floor no model can beat.
  • Reading one noisy validation score as the truth; the validation error has variance too, which cross-validation reduces.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is the bias–variance trade-off?

How a strong answer sounds
A model's expected error on new data comes from three sources. Bias is systematic error from assumptions that are too simple, like fitting a straight line to a curve: it is wrong in the same way whatever data you train on. Variance is error from being too sensitive to the particular training sample: retrain on different data and the predictions change a lot. And noise is randomness in the target that no model can predict. Making a model more flexible lowers bias but raises variance, so there is a sweet spot in between, which we find by comparing training and validation error across complexity settings. The fixes are opposite: bias needs a richer model or better features, variance needs more data, regularisation or averaging.

Deeper: Your model has a training error of 2% and a validation error of 15%. The team proposes a deeper network. What do you say?

How a strong answer sounds
That gap is the signature of high variance: the model fits the training data far better than it generalises, so a more flexible model would most likely widen it. I would first rule out non-modelling causes: a validation set from a different distribution, leakage that makes training too easy, or duplicates. Then I would plot a learning curve; if validation error is still falling as data grows, more labelled data is the most reliable fix, and data augmentation is a cheaper version of it. Otherwise I would reduce variance directly: stronger regularisation, early stopping, fewer or better-selected features, or averaging several models. If the team wants a bigger model anyway, it should come with more regularisation, and the decision should rest on validation error across a few settings, not training error. Finally, 15% might be near the noise floor if labels are inconsistent, so I would check label quality on a sample of the errors.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Underfitting: high training and validation error. Overfitting: low training error, much higher validation error.
  • Expected squared error = bias² + variance + noise; flexibility trades bias for variance.
  • The noise in the labels is a floor no model can go below.
  • A validation curve picks the complexity; a learning curve says whether more data would help.
  • Bias is fixed by richer models and features; variance by more data, regularisation, simplicity and averaging.

Check your understanding

Training error 24%, validation error 25%, and the task's best published models reach 8%. What is the likely problem?

Both errors are high and close: the model cannot fit even its training data. It needs more capacity or better features, not more data.

A learning curve shows training and validation error converged at a high value. Will doubling the training data help much?

Converged curves mean the model has learned all it can of this form. More of the same data leaves the bias untouched.

As a model's flexibility increases, what typically happens to bias and variance?

A flexible model can follow the true pattern (lower bias) but also the noise in each sample (higher variance).

Labels are the true value plus random noise with variance 0.09. What is the lowest mean squared error any model can expect on new data?

The noise is unpredictable by definition. Even a model that knew the true function exactly would have an expected squared error of 0.09.

Which change most directly reduces variance?

Averaging models trained on different samples cancels much of their individual sensitivity to the data; the other three make a model more flexible.
