Time-Series Forecasting

How many orders will arrive next week? How much electricity will the city draw tomorrow at six? Forecasting looks like ordinary regression with time as an extra column, and that impression causes most of its failures. Rows are not independent: today resembles yesterday, Mondays resemble Mondays, and the future is not a random sample of the past. A model that ignores this can look excellent in validation and do worse than "same as last week" in production.

This chapter decomposes a series into trend and seasonality, sets up the baselines every forecast must beat, turns the problem into supervised learning with lag and rolling features, and shows how to validate honestly by backtesting: training only on the past and forecasting the future, again and again. Trend and seasonality

Most business series combine a trend, the slow drift in level; one or more seasonal cycles of fixed length, such as weekly shopping patterns or yearly weather; occasional events and level shifts, like a price change or a new competitor; and noise, which is often autocorrelated, so a high day tends to be followed by another high day. Plotting the series, its seasonal averages and its autocorrelation at different lags comes before any modelling. Decompositions such as STL separate these parts so you can see which matter.

Baselines first

The naive forecast repeats the last observed value. The seasonal naive forecast repeats the value from one season ago: next Tuesday will be like last Tuesday. A moving average repeats the recent mean. They are embarrassingly simple and often hard to beat at short horizons. Report every model's error relative to them; MASE, the mean absolute scaled error, divides your forecast's MAE by the MAE of the seasonal naive forecast, so below 1 means you beat it.

Lag and rolling features

To use regression or gradient boosting, turn the series into rows: the target is the value at time t, and the features are things known before t, such as the values 1, 7 and 14 days earlier (lags), the mean of the previous 7 days (rolling statistics), the day of the week, the time of year, holidays and promotions planned in advance. Every feature must be computable at the moment the forecast is made. To forecast more than one step ahead, either feed predictions back in as lags (recursive) or train a separate model for each horizon, using only lags of at least that horizon (direct).

import pandas as pd

df = pd.DataFrame({"y": sales}, index=dates)
for lag in [1, 7, 14, 28]:
    df[f"lag{lag}"] = df["y"].shift(lag)
df["roll7"] = df["y"].shift(1).rolling(7).mean()        # shift first: only the past
df["dow"] = df.index.dayofweek
# df["y"].rolling(7, center=True).mean() would use the next 3 days: a leak

Things to try.

  • Start with seasonal naive, 28 days ahead, from day 868. This forecast's MAE is 12.9; averaged over the 22 backtest origins it is 10.72, which by definition is MASE 1.00. Every other method is judged against it.
  • Try Naive and the 28-day average. Both ignore the weekly pattern: backtest MASE 1.38 and 1.18, worse than repeating last week.
  • Switch to the linear model. This forecast's MAE drops to 7.8; over the backtest, 9.64, MASE 0.90. At a 7-day horizon it does better still, MASE 0.83. Its error grows across the horizon, from 5.5 on day 1 to 10.6 on day 28, as forecasts are fed back as lags.
  • Move the origin to day 896. The window now contains the +25 level shift on day 900. The linear model's MAE jumps to 20.5 and seasonal naive's to 19.5: nothing trained on the past can foresee it. By day 1050 the linear model has absorbed the new level and is back to 5.6.
  • Open the Validation view. Shuffled 5-fold cross-validation reports 4.99 for the linear model, and 3.94 once a centred rolling mean is added. The honest backtest gives 5.34 one day ahead, 7.16 at 7 days and 9.64 at 28: nearly twice what shuffled cross-validation suggests.

Backtesting without peeking

Shuffled k-fold cross-validation puts days from next month into training and days from last month into validation. Neighbouring days share noise, so the model is effectively tested on days it has nearly seen, and it never has to cope with changes it has not yet observed, such as a level shift. On a synthetic daily series with a gradient-boosted model on lag features, shuffled 5-fold cross-validation reported an MAE of 5.95; scikit-learn's time-ordered TimeSeriesSplit reported 10.68 for the same model and data. Adding a centred 7-day rolling mean, which quietly includes the next three days, lowered the shuffled estimate further, to 4.62.

Honest validation is a backtest: pick a forecast origin, train on everything before it, forecast the next h steps exactly as you would in production, score them, move the origin forward and repeat. Use the horizon you will actually forecast at, keep a gap if your data arrives late, and refit as often as you will in production.

from sklearn.model_selection import TimeSeriesSplit, cross_val_score
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingRegressor

tscv = TimeSeriesSplit(n_splits=5, test_size=28, gap=0)        # each fold trains on the past only
scores = cross_val_score(HistGradientBoostingRegressor(), X, y, cv=tscv,
                         scoring="neg_mean_absolute_error")

Use case: the M competitions

Since 1982 Spyros Makridakis and colleagues have run open forecasting competitions on large collections of real series, the M competitions, and their results have repeatedly surprised the field. The M3 competition in 2000 used 3,003 series from business, finance and demography, and concluded that statistically sophisticated methods did not necessarily forecast more accurately than simple ones, and that combining forecasts helped. The M4 competition in 2018 used 100,000 series. Most of its leading entries combined several methods, the winner was a hybrid of exponential smoothing and a neural network by Slawek Smyl, and the pure machine learning entries did poorly, most failing to beat simple statistical benchmarks. The M5 competition in 2020, on Walmart's daily sales, finally saw gradient-boosted trees on lag and calendar features dominate (Chapter 48). The lesson across forty years is the one this chapter's simulator shows: compare everything against simple baselines, on honest backtests, before believing it is better.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Shuffled cross-validation on time-ordered data.
  • Features that look ahead: centred rolling windows, scalers fitted on all data, targets aggregated over the forecast period.
  • Validating one step ahead when production forecasts 28 days ahead.
  • No baseline, or a baseline weaker than seasonal naive.
  • Tree models on trending data without detrending or differencing.
  • Point forecasts with no uncertainty, when decisions depend on the range.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Why can't you use ordinary cross-validation for time series?

How a strong answer sounds
Ordinary cross-validation shuffles the rows, so the model trains on days after the ones it is tested on. In a time series, neighbouring days are similar, so the model is tested on days it has effectively already seen, and it never has to deal with changes that only appear later. That makes the error estimate too optimistic. Instead, you validate the way you will use the model: train on the past, forecast the next period, score it, then move forward and repeat. That is called backtesting, or rolling-origin evaluation.

Deeper: You need daily demand forecasts 28 days ahead for 5,000 products. How would you approach it?

How a strong answer sounds
First I would set up the evaluation: a rolling-origin backtest over the last several months, forecasting 28 days from each origin with only data available at that time, scored with a scale-free metric such as MASE or weighted absolute error, against seasonal naive and simple exponential smoothing baselines. Then I would build one global model across products, typically gradient boosting on features computed at the origin: lags of at least the horizon or recursive predictions, rolling means and trends, calendar and holiday features, prices and planned promotions, and product and store attributes. I would detrend or model ratios because trees cannot extrapolate. I would compare direct and recursive strategies, check errors by horizon, product volume and intermittency, and produce quantile forecasts if downstream inventory decisions need ranges. In production I would retrain on a schedule, monitor forecast error as actuals arrive, and keep the baseline running as a fallback.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Decompose a series into trend, seasonality, shifts and noise before modelling.
  • Always compare against naive and seasonal naive forecasts; MASE does it in one number.
  • Lag, rolling and calendar features turn forecasting into regression, if they use only the past.
  • Validate with rolling-origin backtests at the real horizon, never shuffled folds.
  • Errors grow with the horizon and jump at level shifts; measure them where you will forecast.
  • Tree models cannot extrapolate trends; detrend or use models that can.

Check your understanding

What does a seasonal naive forecast predict for next Monday's sales, with weekly seasonality?

It repeats the value one season ago. It is the standard baseline, and the denominator of MASE.

Which feature leaks future information into a forecasting model?

A centred window includes the three following days, which are not known when the forecast is made.

Shuffled 5-fold cross-validation reports a much lower error than a rolling backtest for the same model. Which explanation fits best?

Autocorrelated neighbours and future information sit in the training folds. The backtest mimics production; trust it.

A forecast's MASE is 1.3. What does that mean?

MASE divides by the baseline's MAE, so values above 1 are worse than the baseline.

Why does a gradient-boosted tree model struggle with an upward-trending series?

Each leaf predicts an average of training targets. Detrend, difference or predict ratios so the target stays in range.
