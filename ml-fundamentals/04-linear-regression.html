Linear Regression

Linear regression predicts a number as a weighted sum of features: price = £2,000 per square metre of floor area, plus £14,000 per bedroom, minus £9,000 per kilometre from a station, plus a constant. It is the oldest model in this course and still one of the most used, because it is fast, needs little data, and its weights can be read as statements about the world, if you are careful.

This chapter shows where the weights come from: the line that makes the squared errors as small as possible, found by a formula rather than a search. Then it shows how to read them, how residual plots tell you when a straight line is the wrong shape, and why two nearly identical features can make the weights meaningless. In the simulator you fit a line by hand, watch the squared errors as actual squares, and then let least squares find the best one.

The model

With one feature, linear regression is a straight line: ŷ = w·x + b, a slope and an intercept. With d features it is a flat surface in d + 1 dimensions, but the formula is the same shape: one weight per feature, multiplied and added, plus the intercept. For a whole dataset at once it is the matrix-vector product from Prerequisite 4.

   ŷ  =  w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + … + w_d x_d + b        for one example
   ŷ  =  X w + b                              for all n examples: (n, d) @ (d,) → (n,)
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression

X = np.column_stack([area_m2, bedrooms, dist_km])   # 200 houses, 3 features
model = LinearRegression().fit(X, price_k)

model.coef_         # [ 2.01  14.49  -9.01 ]   £k per m², per bedroom, per km
model.intercept_    # 41.1                     £k when every feature is 0 (not a real house)
model.score(X, price_k)    # R² = 0.954 on the training data

Least squares

For any choice of weights, each training example has a residual: the true value minus the prediction. Least squares chooses the weights that make the sum of squared residuals as small as possible. Squaring does three things: it makes every residual count as positive, it makes the total smooth, so calculus can find the minimum, and it punishes big misses much more than small ones, since a residual of 10 costs 100 and a residual of 40 costs 1,600.

That last property is both a feature and a weakness. A model that minimises squared error is pulled hard towards outliers, because one very wrong point costs as much as many slightly wrong ones. The simulator lets you add one.

Things to try.

  • Start from the default line. Its squares cover about 8.5 times the area of the best line's. Get the line through the middle of the cloud first, then tilt it; the biggest squares shrink fastest.
  • Fit by least squares. 2.23x + 22, with an RMSE of £27.8k, close to the £2.1k per m² and £40k the data was generated from. The gap is noise in 40 houses.
  • Add the bad record. One house of 41 drags the best line to 2.02x + 43 and doubles the RMSE to £56.6k. Its single square is larger than all the others put together.
  • Curved data, then the residual plot. The best straight line has an R² of 0.925, which looks excellent, and the residual plot is a clear U. A good R² does not mean the right shape.
  • Spread grows with size, residual plot. A fan: small houses are predicted within a few £k, large ones miss by £50k or more.

Where the weights come from: the normal equation

Setting the gradient of the sum of squared residuals to zero gives a set of linear equations for the weights, the normal equation. Add a column of ones to X to carry the intercept, and it reads:

   minimise  ‖y − Xw‖²        gradient  −2Xᵀ(y − Xw) = 0        so   XᵀX w = Xᵀy

That is the projection from Prerequisite 4: the residual vector y − Xw ends up perpendicular to every feature column. scikit-learn solves it with a numerically careful routine, the same family as np.linalg.lstsq; nobody inverts XᵀX by hand.

A = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(X)), X])         # a column of 1s for the intercept
w, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(A, price_k, rcond=None)
w                                                   # [41.09  2.01  14.49  -9.01]  the same as scikit-learn

A closed-form answer is linear regression's great practical strength: no learning rate, no iterations, the same answer every time. It stops being practical when there are hundreds of thousands of features or more rows than fit in memory, and then gradient descent takes over (Chapter 5).

Reading the coefficients

A coefficient is the change in the prediction for a one-unit increase in its feature, with every other feature held fixed. "£14.5k per bedroom" means: compare two houses with the same floor area and the same distance to a station, one with an extra bedroom, and the model predicts £14.5k more. It does not mean adding a bedroom to your house adds £14.5k; that is a causal claim the data cannot support (Prerequisite 7).

Three cautions follow. Units: a coefficient's size depends on its feature's units, so £2.01k per m² and £14.5k per bedroom cannot be compared for importance; standardise the features first if you want comparable weights. The intercept is the prediction when every feature is zero, often a house that cannot exist, so it is a fitting constant, not a fact. And collinearity, below.

Residual plots and assumptions

Plot the residuals against the predictions, or against a feature. If the model's shape is right, they form a patternless band around zero. Two patterns are worth recognising on sight: a curve means the relationship is not straight and the model needs a transformed feature or a more flexible model (Chapter 14); a funnel means the spread of errors grows with the prediction, common for prices, and often fixed by predicting the logarithm of the target.

Collinearity: when coefficients stop meaning anything

Suppose the dataset records floor area twice, once in square metres and once in square feet, rounded. The two columns are almost perfectly correlated. Predictions are fine, but the model can trade weight between them almost freely, and which split it lands on depends on noise. Refit on two bootstrap samples of the same 200 houses:

# features: area_m2, area_sqft (= area_m2 × 10.764, rounded), bedrooms, dist_km
sample 1:  area_m2 -15.59   area_sqft  1.64    # implied £k per m²: -15.59 + 10.764 × 1.64 = 2.03
sample 2:  area_m2  29.85   area_sqft -2.60    # implied £k per m²:  29.85 - 10.764 × 2.60 = 1.90

Each coefficient flips sign between samples, while their combined effect barely moves. Anyone reading "area in square metres has a negative effect on price" from the first fit would be badly misled. The fix is to remove the redundancy, or to use ridge regression, which picks a stable split; the deeper lesson is that coefficients are only interpretable one at a time when the features are not strongly correlated.

Use case: estimating build times in a CI system

A continuous-integration service wants to show developers how long their build will take before it starts. A linear model is a good first answer: build seconds ≈ a fixed setup cost, plus some seconds per changed file, per test, and per dependency that must be downloaded. It trains in milliseconds on last month's builds, and its coefficients double as a diagnosis, since "each uncached dependency adds 4.2 seconds" is a number the platform team can act on. The residual plot tells the rest of the story. Builds on one runner pool sit consistently above the line, which points to slower machines, so runner type becomes a feature. And the spread fans out for large builds, so the team predicts the logarithm of the time and quotes a range rather than a single number. None of that needed a complex model, only a straight line and a careful look at what it got wrong.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Reading coefficients as causes, or as importances when the features have different units.
  • Interpreting coefficients of strongly correlated features, which can flip sign from sample to sample.
  • Skipping the residual plot, and missing a curve or a funnel that a transformation would fix.
  • Letting a few bad records drag the line, because squared error weights them heavily.
  • Judging the fit by training R², instead of error on held-out data.
  • Extrapolating: trusting the line far outside the range of the training data.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How does linear regression choose its line?

How a strong answer sounds
It predicts a weighted sum of the features plus a constant, and it chooses the weights that minimise the sum of squared residuals, the squared differences between the true values and the predictions on the training data. Because that total is a smooth bowl-shaped function of the weights, the minimum can be found exactly by setting its gradient to zero, which gives the normal equations, XᵀXw = Xᵀy; geometrically, the fitted values are the projection of the targets onto the space spanned by the features. In practice a library solves that with a stable least-squares routine. Squaring the residuals means large errors count a lot, which makes the fit sensitive to outliers.

Deeper: Your regression says that, holding other features fixed, more bathrooms lower the price. How do you investigate?

How a strong answer sounds
A coefficient with a surprising sign usually comes from collinearity, confounding or data problems rather than a real effect. First I would check correlations between bathrooms and the other features: if bathrooms, bedrooms and floor area move together, the model can trade weight between them, and the bathroom coefficient may be negative only because floor area already carries most of the effect. Refitting on bootstrap samples and seeing the sign flip, or computing variance inflation factors, would confirm that. Then I would look for a confounder or a selection effect: maybe houses with many bathrooms in this data are older conversions into flats in cheaper areas. Then data quality: unit errors, placeholders like 0 or 99, or a few outliers dragging the fit, which the residual plot and a fit without them would show. The coefficient also means "holding everything else fixed", which may describe combinations that hardly exist. If the goal is prediction, none of this may matter. If it is interpretation, I would simplify the feature set or use ridge regression, and report effects only for features that are stable across resamples.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Linear regression predicts Xw + b, choosing the weights that minimise the sum of squared residuals.
  • The minimum has a closed form, the normal equation, solved with a least-squares routine; it is a projection.
  • Squared errors make the fit sensitive to outliers: one bad record can tilt the line.
  • A coefficient is the change per unit of its feature with the others held fixed; it is not a cause, and its size depends on units.
  • Residual plots reveal a wrong shape (a curve) or uneven noise (a funnel).
  • Strongly correlated features make individual coefficients unstable even when predictions are fine.

Check your understanding

What does ordinary least squares minimise?

It squares each residual and adds them up. Absolute residuals give least absolute deviations, a different and more outlier-resistant fit.

A model has a coefficient of 14.5 (in £k) for bedrooms. What does it mean?

A coefficient describes the model's prediction with the other features held fixed. It is not a causal effect, and its size cannot be compared with features in other units.

A residual plot shows residuals that are positive at both ends and negative in the middle. What does it suggest?

A U-shaped pattern means a straight line under-predicts at the extremes and over-predicts in the middle: the true relationship bends.

Floor area is included both in m² and in ft². What happens to the two coefficients?

The columns are nearly collinear, so weight can move between them with almost no change in fit. Predictions stay fine; individual coefficients become meaningless.

One record in a dataset of 40 has a price ten times too low. Why does it affect least squares so much?

A residual four times larger costs sixteen times more. Moving the line towards that one point reduces the total more than it costs on all the others.
