Categorical Features & Encodings

Country, device type, merchant, product category, postcode: much of the most useful information in business data is categorical, and models need numbers. How you turn a category into numbers decides what the model can learn from it, how many columns it costs, and whether it quietly leaks the answer.

This chapter covers the main encodings, one-hot, ordinal, target and hashing, and the two problems that make categories hard in practice: columns with thousands of values, most of them rare, and values that appear for the first time in production. The simulator focuses on the most dangerous of these encodings, target encoding, and shows it producing a strong-looking feature out of a column that contains no information at all.

  • Handle high-cardinality columns and rare categories

High cardinality and rare categories

A column such as merchant ID, postcode or product SKU can have tens of thousands of values, following a long tail: a few appear constantly, most appear a handful of times. One-hot encoding them all creates one sparse column per value, and the rare ones teach the model almost nothing except noise. Three standard moves: group values below a frequency threshold into one "other" category (min_frequency); replace the category with a statistic about it, such as its target mean or its frequency; or use a model that handles categories natively and groups them itself.

Target encoding, and its leak

Target encoding replaces each category with the average label of the training rows in that category: the merchant's historical fraud rate, the postcode's average price. It turns a column with 10,000 values into one informative number. Done naively, it is also one of the most common sources of leakage there is.

The problem is that each training row's encoding includes that row's own label. A merchant seen once, in a row that is fraud, gets an encoding of exactly 1.0, and the model learns that an encoding of 1.0 means fraud. On the training data that is perfectly true and completely circular. On new data the merchant's single historical row says almost nothing. The fix has two parts. Out-of-fold encoding: split the training rows into folds and encode each fold using only the other folds' labels, so no row ever sees its own label. Smoothing: shrink each category's mean towards the overall mean in proportion to how few rows it has, so a merchant with one row gets an encoding close to the global rate. scikit-learn's TargetEncoder does both inside fit_transform.

# smoothed category mean: m pseudo-rows at the global mean pull small categories towards it
enc = (sum_of_labels_in_category + m * global_mean) / (rows_in_category + m)

# on a merchant column with NO real signal (labels are 10% random):
naive = y.groupby(merchant).transform("mean")              # training AUC 0.623, from nothing
oof = TargetEncoder(target_type="binary").fit_transform(X, y)    # training AUC 0.501: chance, as it should be

Things to try.

  • "Not at all", naive mean. The merchant is pure noise, yet the naive encoding scores an AUC of 0.791 on training rows. On later transactions it is 0.513: chance. 141 merchants appear exactly once, and their encoding is their own label.
  • Switch to out of fold. Training AUC falls to 0.493 and agrees with the later data. Nothing was there, and now nothing appears to be.
  • "A lot". Naive: 0.894 in training, 0.711 later. Out of fold with smoothing: 0.732 in training and 0.745 later, the best of the three on new data, because rare merchants no longer get extreme encodings.
  • "Many" rows per merchant. With 50 merchants of about 60 rows each, the three encodings nearly agree (0.814 against 0.792 in training with a strong effect). The leak is a small-category problem; long tails are where it bites.

Categories you have never seen

New merchants open, new products launch, new app versions ship. An encoder fitted last month meets values it has no column or statistic for. Decide in advance: handle_unknown="ignore" or "infrequent_if_exist" for one-hot, which maps them to all-zeros or to the rare-values column; the global mean for target encoding; and hashing, which simply maps them somewhere. Then monitor the share of unseen values in production, because a sudden rise usually means an upstream change, such as a renamed category, not a real influx (Chapter 39).

Native categorical support

Gradient-boosting libraries can split on categories directly, grouping them into the best two sets at each split instead of needing an encoding. scikit-learn's HistGradientBoostingClassifier(categorical_features="from_dtype"), LightGBM and CatBoost all do this; CatBoost's whole design centres on an ordered form of target statistics that avoids exactly the leak in the simulator. Native support is usually the best option for high-cardinality columns with tree models; linear models still need an explicit encoding.

🐈
Use case: the encoding that became a library

Yandex released CatBoost as open source in 2017, and its accompanying paper, "CatBoost: unbiased boosting with categorical features" (Prokhorenkova and colleagues, NeurIPS 2018), is largely about the problem in the simulator. The authors call it target leakage and prediction shift: when a category's statistics are computed from the same rows the model is then trained on, the training distribution of the encoded feature differs from what the model will see on new data. Their solution, ordered target statistics, processes rows in a random order and encodes each one using only the rows before it, the same idea as out-of-fold encoding taken to its limit, with a prior to smooth the early rows. It made CatBoost a strong default for data dominated by high-cardinality categories, such as clicks, transactions and user logs, without any hand-built encoding at all.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Target-encoding with each row's own label, producing a feature that is brilliant on training data and weak in production.
  • Computing encodings on the whole dataset before the train/test split.
  • Ordinal-encoding unordered categories for a linear model, which then treats "Hull < Leeds < York" as meaningful.
  • One-hot encoding a 50,000-value ID column and getting a huge, mostly empty matrix.
  • No plan for unseen categories, so the pipeline crashes on the first new value.
  • Encoding IDs that are effectively row identifiers, such as order numbers, which can only memorise.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How would you encode a categorical feature with 10,000 distinct values?

How a strong answer sounds
One-hot would create 10,000 sparse columns, most for rare values the model cannot learn from, so I would avoid it. With a gradient-boosted model I would first try the library's native categorical support. Otherwise target encoding is the usual answer: replace each value with the average target for that value, but computed out of fold so that no row's own label goes into its encoding, and smoothed towards the global mean so that rare values do not get extreme encodings; scikit-learn's TargetEncoder does both. Alternatives are grouping rare values into "other", frequency encoding, or hashing into a fixed number of columns. Whatever I choose, I would fit it inside the cross-validation pipeline and decide what happens to values never seen in training.

Deeper: A new target-encoded feature takes training AUC from 0.78 to 0.95, but validation AUC only moves from 0.76 to 0.77. What happened?

How a strong answer sounds
That gap is the signature of target leakage through the encoding. If each training row's category mean includes its own label, then for small categories the encoding nearly is the label, so the model learns to trust it and training AUC soars; on validation rows the encoding comes from other rows and carries only the real signal, which here is small. I would check how the encoding was computed: on all training rows at once, or before the split, or without smoothing. The fix is out-of-fold encoding within training, smoothing towards the global mean with a strength tuned by cross-validation, and computing validation and test encodings only from training data. After fixing it I would expect training AUC to drop back near validation, and the real value of the feature is that 0.01 improvement, which may or may not justify its complexity. I would also check the category's cardinality and row counts, since a column that is close to a unique ID can only ever memorise.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • One-hot for a few unordered values, ordinal for truly ordered ones, target or native support for many.
  • Long-tailed columns need rare values grouped or replaced by statistics.
  • Naive target encoding includes each row's own label and leaks; it can make a useless column look strong.
  • Encode out of fold and smooth towards the global mean; scikit-learn's TargetEncoder does both.
  • Decide how unseen categories are handled before production does it for you.
  • Gradient-boosting libraries can split on categories natively; CatBoost was built around avoiding this leak.

Check your understanding

A merchant appears in exactly one training row, which is fraud. What is its naive target encoding?

Its mean label over its one row is 1.0: the encoding is that row's own label, which is why the model can learn it perfectly on training data.

What does out-of-fold target encoding guarantee?

Each fold is encoded from the other folds' labels, which removes the circularity that inflates training scores.

Why smooth a target encoding towards the global mean?

A category with two rows has a mean that is 0, 0.5 or 1 by chance. Smoothing pulls such estimates towards the global rate in proportion to how little data they rest on.

Which encoding suits "T-shirt size: S, M, L, XL" for a linear model?

The values have a genuine order, so 0, 1, 2, 3 in the right order carries real information; state the order rather than relying on alphabetical sorting.

What is a drawback of the hashing trick?

Hashing maps many possible values into a fixed number of columns, so collisions are inevitable; it handles unseen values gracefully and uses no labels.
