Statistics for ML

Every number a model produces on a test set is an estimate. Test it on a different thousand examples and the accuracy would come out a little different. Statistics is the discipline of saying how different: how much a number would move if you repeated the experiment, and so how much of a difference between two models is real.

This page covers the tools the rest of the course uses to stay honest: summaries that survive skewed data, the central limit theorem, standard errors and confidence intervals, the bootstrap, and the gap between correlation and causation. The simulator builds fifty confidence intervals at once, so you can see what "95% confident" does and does not promise.

Summaries: centre and spread

The mean is the sum divided by the count. The median is the middle value once sorted. On symmetric data they agree. On skewed data, such as incomes, house prices or request latencies, a few very large values drag the mean upwards while the median stays with the typical case. A single billionaire moving into a town changes its mean income enormously and its median income not at all.

prices = np.array([180, 195, 210, 230, 245, 260, 4_200])   # £k; one mansion
prices.mean(), np.median(prices)                 # (788.6, 230.0)
prices.std(ddof=1)                                 # sample standard deviation: divide by n - 1
np.percentile(prices, [25, 50, 75])               # quartiles; the gap 75 - 25 is the IQR

Spread has the same choice. The standard deviation is built from squared distances, so outliers dominate it; quantiles such as the interquartile range are robust. Two habits follow. Look at the distribution before summarising it (Prerequisite 8), and when a column is skewed, report the median and quantiles or work on a log scale. The ddof=1 divides by n − 1 rather than n, which corrects a small downward bias when estimating a population's spread from a sample; NumPy's default is ddof=0 and pandas' is 1, which is a classic source of tiny disagreements.

Samples, and the central limit theorem

The data you have is a sample from a larger population: the customers who happened to be in last month's data, from all the customers who could be. Any statistic you compute from the sample, a mean or an accuracy, would come out differently on another sample. The distribution of those values across imaginary repeated samples is the statistic's sampling distribution, and its standard deviation is the standard error.

   standard error of a mean  =  σ / √n           estimated by   s / √n   (s = sample standard deviation)

The central limit theorem adds the remarkable part: whatever the shape of the population, as long as its variance is finite, the sampling distribution of a mean becomes approximately normal as n grows. That is why the normal distribution appears everywhere in statistics even though little data is normal itself. "Approximately" and "as n grows" carry real weight, though: for strongly skewed data, small samples are not yet normal, and the simulator shows what that does to an interval.

The √n is the other lesson. Quadrupling the data halves the standard error. Going from 1,000 test examples to 4,000 makes an accuracy estimate twice as precise, not four times; from 100 to 10,000 makes it ten times as precise.

Confidence intervals

A 95% confidence interval is a range computed from the sample by a method that, over many repeated samples, contains the true value 95% of the time. For a mean, the standard recipe uses the t distribution, which is a little wider than the normal for small samples to allow for estimating σ from the same data:

from scipy import stats

x = rng.exponential(50, size=30)                  # 30 response times, skewed, true mean 50 ms
m, se = x.mean(), x.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(len(x))
t = stats.t.ppf(0.975, df=len(x) - 1)               # 2.045 for 29 degrees of freedom
(m - t * se, m + t * se)                               # the 95% interval

The precise meaning is easy to garble. The 95% describes the method, not any one interval: it is not "a 95% chance the true mean is in this particular range" in the classical sense, because the true mean is a fixed number and this interval either contains it or does not. What you can say is that intervals built this way catch the truth 95% of the time, if the method's assumptions hold. The simulator makes that literal.

Both populations have a true mean of 50. The t interval is mean ± t × s/√n with the exact t critical value; the bootstrap takes the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of 400 resampled means. "Long-run coverage" was measured offline with NumPy by repeating the whole procedure 4,000 times (2,000 for the bootstrap), so it does not change when you draw new samples.

Things to try.

  • Normal population, t interval, any n. Draw a few times. Usually two or three of the fifty miss, and the long-run figure sits at 95%: the method keeps its promise.
  • Change n from 10 to 30 to 100. The intervals narrow from about 14 wide to about 7 to about 4: roughly 1/√n.
  • Skewed population, n = 5. Now about 12% miss, not 5%, and they mostly miss on the same side. The central limit theorem has not kicked in yet. Coverage climbs to about 93% at n = 30 and 100, and is back at 95% by n = 500.
  • Bootstrap at n = 5. Worse still: under 85% even for normal data, because five values cannot show how spread out the population really is. The bootstrap is a large-sample method.

The bootstrap

The t interval works for means. For a median, a correlation or a model's AUC there is often no simple formula. The bootstrap replaces the formula with computation: treat your sample as a stand-in for the population, draw many new samples of the same size from it with replacement, compute the statistic on each, and use the spread of those values as the uncertainty.

def bootstrap_ci(x, stat=np.median, n_boot=2000, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    idx = rng.integers(0, len(x), size=(n_boot, len(x)))   # resample row indices
    vals = np.array([stat(x[i]) for i in idx])
    return np.percentile(vals, [2.5, 97.5])

Resampling indices rather than values matters when each row carries several numbers, such as a prediction and a label: resample the rows together and the bootstrap gives an interval for accuracy or AUC on a test set. Chapter 36 uses exactly that to decide whether one model really beats another. The simulator's last setting is the warning label: with very small samples, the bootstrap is overconfident.

Correlation is not causation

The Pearson correlation r, between −1 and 1, measures how close two variables are to a straight-line relationship. It is useful and easily misread. It misses relationships that are strong but not linear, it can be dominated by a few outliers, and above all it does not say which variable causes which, or whether either does.

ObservationTempting conclusionWhat is actually going on
Servers with more RAM crash more oftenRAM causes crashesA confounder: the biggest machines run the heaviest workloads, which crash more
Users who open the help page churn moreThe help page drives people awayReverse causation: people already having trouble look for help
Customers who received a discount spent moreDiscounts raise spendingSelection: the discount was sent to the most active customers
Two metrics both rose every month this yearOne drives the otherA shared trend: anything that grows over time correlates with anything else that does

For prediction, correlation is often enough: a feature that is correlated with the label helps predict it, whatever the reason. For decisions, it is not. "Customers who get this email churn less" is a fine feature, and a terrible reason to send the email to everyone; Chapter 47 separates "who will churn" from "who would be saved by an offer". The tool for causal questions is a randomised experiment, an A/B test, which breaks every confounder by assigning the treatment by coin flip.

Use case: is the new model's accuracy gain real?

A team retrains a classifier and the test accuracy goes from 91.2% to 91.8% on a test set of 2,000 examples. Is that an improvement? The standard error of an accuracy near 91% on 2,000 examples is √(0.91 × 0.09 / 2000), about 0.64 percentage points, so a 95% interval around each figure is roughly ±1.3 points. The two intervals overlap almost entirely. The comparison is better done on the same examples, pairing the two models' predictions row by row, which removes the shared noise from which examples happened to be in the test set, and a paired bootstrap of the difference is the usual method (Chapter 36). Done that way, a 0.6-point gain may or may not survive, depending on how often the models disagree. What the statistics rules out immediately is the version of the conversation that ends with "0.6 points better, ship it", based on one number from each model.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Reporting the mean of skewed data as if it were the typical value.
  • Saying "a 95% probability the true value is in this interval" about one interval, as if the true value were the random thing.
  • Assuming the central limit theorem at n = 5 on heavily skewed data.
  • Forgetting the √n. Four times the data buys only twice the precision.
  • Bootstrapping values instead of rows when each row holds a prediction and a label.
  • Reading causation into a correlation and acting on it without an experiment.
  • Mixing ddof=0 and ddof=1 between NumPy and pandas and chasing a tiny discrepancy.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What does a 95% confidence interval mean?

How a strong answer sounds
It is a range calculated from a sample by a procedure that, if you repeated the whole study many times with fresh samples, would capture the true value in about 95% of the repetitions. The 95% is a property of the procedure. For any one interval the true value is either in it or not; you just do not know which. In practice I read it as the range of values consistent with the data at that level, and I pay attention to its width: a wide interval means I do not have enough data to pin the number down. I also check that its assumptions are reasonable, because with small or skewed samples the real coverage can be well below 95%.

Deeper: How would you put an error bar on a model's AUC, measured on a test set of 5,000 examples?

How a strong answer sounds
AUC has no simple standard-error formula that I would trust in every case, so I would bootstrap it. Resample the 5,000 test rows with replacement, keeping each row's score and label together, recompute the AUC, and repeat a couple of thousand times; the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles give a 95% interval. Five thousand rows is plenty for the percentile bootstrap, though if positives are rare I would check how many are in the test set, because the AUC's uncertainty is driven by the smaller class: with 50 positives the interval will be wide no matter how many negatives there are. If the rows are not independent, say several rows per customer, I would resample customers rather than rows, otherwise the interval is too narrow. And if the real question is whether this model beats another, I would bootstrap the difference in AUC on the same resampled rows, which is much tighter than comparing two separate intervals.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • On skewed data, the median and quantiles describe the typical case better than the mean and standard deviation.
  • Every statistic has a sampling distribution; its standard deviation is the standard error, and for a mean it is σ/√n.
  • The central limit theorem makes means approximately normal for large n, but small skewed samples are not there yet.
  • A 95% interval comes from a method that catches the truth 95% of the time; it is not a statement about one interval.
  • The bootstrap gives an interval for almost any statistic by resampling rows with replacement.
  • Correlation is enough for prediction, not for decisions; causal claims need an experiment.

Check your understanding

Which statement about a correctly computed 95% confidence interval is right?

The 95% describes the procedure over repeated samples. It is not about the spread of the data, and the true value is fixed, not random.

You want the confidence interval for a mean to be half as wide. Roughly how much more data do you need?

Width is proportional to 1/√n, so halving it needs √n to double, which means n quadruples.

House prices in a town are strongly right-skewed. Which single number best describes a typical house?

A few expensive houses pull the mean up; the median stays with the middle of the market.

Machines with more memory show more crashes. Which explanation is a confounder?

A confounder drives both variables. The second option is reverse causation, a different trap.

In a bootstrap of a test set's accuracy, what does each resample consist of?

Each bootstrap sample has the original size, drawn with replacement, and keeps each prediction with its own label.
