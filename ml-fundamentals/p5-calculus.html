Derivatives & Gradients

Training a model means adjusting its parameters to make a loss smaller. To know which way to adjust them, you need to know how the loss changes when each parameter moves a little. That rate of change is a derivative, and with many parameters at once it is the gradient. Almost every learning algorithm in this course follows a gradient downhill, starting with Chapter 5.

This page builds the three tools you need: the derivative as a slope, the gradient as a vector of slopes, and the chain rule for functions built from other functions. Then it shows how to check a gradient you derived by hand against a numerical estimate. The simulator does that check live, and shows why making the step "as small as possible" eventually makes the estimate worse.

  • Read a derivative as the slope of a curve at a point
  • Compute partial derivatives and assemble them into a gradient
  • Explain why stepping against the gradient decreases a function
  • Apply the chain rule to the loss of a simple model
  • Check a gradient with central differences and choose a sensible step size

A derivative is a slope

Take a function of one number, such as a loss L(w) that depends on one weight w. The derivative L′(w) is the slope of the curve at that point: how much L changes per unit change of w, for a very small change. Positive slope means the loss rises if w increases; negative means it falls; zero means the curve is flat there, as it is at a minimum.

                 L(w + h) − L(w)
   L′(w)  =  lim  ───────────────       the slope of the line through two points
             h→0         h               on the curve, as the points move together

For the functions in this course you rarely take that limit by hand, because a few rules cover nearly everything:

Partial derivatives and the gradient

A real model has many parameters. A partial derivative is the slope with respect to one of them while the others are held still, written ∂L/∂w₁. Collect one partial derivative per parameter and you have the gradient, written ∇L: a vector with the same shape as the parameters.

import numpy as np

def f(v):                      # f(x, y) = x² + 3y²: a bowl, steeper along y
    x, y = v
    return x**2 + 3 * y**2

def grad_f(v):                 # ∂f/∂x = 2x, ∂f/∂y = 6y
    x, y = v
    return np.array([2 * x, 6 * y])

grad_f(np.array([1.0, 1.0]))     # [2. 6.]

The gradient has a geometric meaning that is the whole reason ML uses it: it points in the direction in which the function increases fastest, and its length is how steep that increase is. At (1, 1) above, f rises three times faster along y than along x, so the gradient points mostly in the y direction. The negative gradient points straight downhill. A small step v − η · ∇f(v), for a small positive step size η, lowers f; repeat it and you have gradient descent.

Why the fastest direction? For a small step d, the change in f is approximately the dot product ∇f · d. From Prerequisite 4, a dot product with a fixed-length d is largest when d points the same way as ∇f and most negative when it points the opposite way.

The chain rule

Models are functions built from functions: a weighted sum, passed through a squashing function, compared with a label by a loss. The chain rule says how to differentiate such a composition: multiply the derivatives of the pieces, each evaluated at the point where it is used.

   L(w) = (w·x − y)²         one example: feature x, label y, prediction w·x

   let  e = w·x − y          the error             de/dw = x
        L = e²                                      dL/de = 2e

   dL/dw  =  dL/de · de/dw  =  2(w·x − y) · x
def loss(w, x=3.0, y=5.0):
    return (w * x - y) ** 2

def grad(w, x=3.0, y=5.0):      # from the chain rule
    return 2 * (w * x - y) * x

loss(1.0), grad(1.0)              # (4.0, -12.0): prediction 3 is too low, so increase w

Read the result as a sentence: the gradient is the error times the input. A large error on an example with a large feature value produces a large push on that feature's weight, in the direction that shrinks the error. Over a whole dataset the losses add up, so their gradients add up too. That sentence will reappear, with matrices, as the gradient of linear regression in Chapter 4 and of logistic regression in Chapter 7.

Checking a gradient numerically

A hand-derived gradient with a sign error or a missing factor of 2 does not crash anything. Training simply goes the wrong way, or too slowly, and you blame the data. The protection is to compare your formula with a numerical estimate built straight from the definition, using a small step h.

   forward difference:   ( f(w + h) − f(w) ) / h              error shrinks like h
   central difference:   ( f(w + h) − f(w − h) ) / (2h)       error shrinks like h²
def numeric_grad(f, v, h=1e-5):
    g = np.zeros_like(v)
    for i in range(len(v)):           # one parameter at a time
        e = np.zeros_like(v); e[i] = h
        g[i] = (f(v + e) - f(v - e)) / (2 * h)
    return g

v = np.array([1.0, 1.0])
np.allclose(numeric_grad(f, v), grad_f(v), rtol=1e-6)    # True: the formula is right

The step size is the subtle part. Too large, and the estimate measures the slope of a chord, not of the curve. Too small, and something else takes over: computers hold numbers to about sixteen significant digits, so f(w + h) and f(w) agree in almost all of them, and subtracting them leaves mostly rounding noise. Things to try (on the default function, at w = 1.5, where the true slope is −0.625).

  • h = 0.1. The forward difference is off by 0.2, the central difference by 0.015.
  • Shrink h one notch at a time. Each tenfold smaller step cuts the forward error tenfold and the central error a hundredfold. That is "error like h" against "error like h²".
  • Keep going. The forward error bottoms out near h = 10⁻⁸ (about 4 × 10⁻⁸) and the central near h = 10⁻⁵ (about 2 × 10⁻¹⁰). Below that, both get worse: at h = 10⁻¹³ they are wrong in the third decimal place. Rounding has taken over.
  • Switch to (w − 2)² + 1. The central difference is exact at every reasonable h, because a parabola's chord through w − h and w + h is always parallel to its tangent at w.
  • Switch to |w| and set w = 0. The forward difference says 1, the central says 0, and the truth is that there is no derivative at a sharp corner. Lasso's penalty (Chapter 12) has exactly this corner, and it is why lasso can set weights to exactly zero.

Use case: a custom loss for a gradient-boosting model

XGBoost and LightGBM, the libraries behind Chapter 19, let you train on your own objective: you pass a function that returns, for every training example, the first derivative of your loss with respect to the model's current prediction, and the second derivative. Teams use this for losses the libraries do not ship, such as an asymmetric loss for demand forecasting, where predicting too little stock costs more than predicting too much. The derivatives are worked out by hand with exactly the rules on this page, and a sign error does not raise anything: the model trains, and each new tree pushes predictions the wrong way. The standard defence is the check in the flow above. Evaluate the loss at a few predictions, compare the hand-written derivative with a central difference, and only then start training.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Stepping with the gradient instead of against it, so the loss goes up.
  • Dropping the inner derivative in the chain rule: the derivative of (wx − y)² is 2(wx − y)·x, not 2(wx − y).
  • Checking a gradient with h = 10⁻¹⁰ or smaller, where rounding error swamps the estimate.
  • Using the forward difference for a check when the central one is far more accurate for the same h.
  • Comparing with absolute error instead of relative error, so large gradients always "fail" and small ones always "pass".
  • Checking only at w = 0, where terms that multiply by w vanish and a wrong formula can look right.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is a gradient, and why do we step in the negative gradient direction?

How a strong answer sounds
For a function of many parameters, the gradient is the vector of its partial derivatives: for each parameter, how fast the function changes when only that parameter moves. Together they point in the direction of steepest increase, and the gradient's length says how steep. The reason is that for a small step, the change in the function is approximately the dot product of the gradient with the step, and a dot product with a fixed-length step is most negative when the step points opposite to the gradient. So to make a loss smaller as fast as possible locally, we take a small step against the gradient, and repeat. The step size matters, because the approximation only holds for small steps.

Deeper: You have implemented a new loss and its gradient by hand. How do you check it, and what step size do you use?

How a strong answer sounds
I compare the analytic gradient with a numerical one computed from the loss itself, using central differences: for each parameter, (L(w + h·eᵢ) − L(w − h·eᵢ)) / 2h. Central differences have error proportional to h squared rather than h, so they are much more accurate at the same step. In double precision I use h around 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻⁶. Smaller steps make it worse, because L(w + h) and L(w − h) agree in nearly all their digits and the subtraction leaves rounding noise, which is amplified when divided by a tiny h. I evaluate at several random points rather than at zero, compare using relative error and expect something like 10⁻⁶ or better, and look out for kinks such as an absolute value or a ReLU, where the numerical estimate is unreliable if a point lands near the corner. In single precision I would use a larger h and a looser tolerance. And it is a test I run once, not during training, since it costs two loss evaluations per parameter.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • A derivative is the slope of a function at a point; zero slope means a flat spot, such as a minimum.
  • The gradient collects one partial derivative per parameter and points uphill; the negative gradient points downhill.
  • The chain rule multiplies the derivatives of the pieces: for squared error, the gradient is error times input.
  • Check hand-written gradients against central differences, with relative error, at random points.
  • Central difference error shrinks like h², forward like h; in float64 use h near 10⁻⁵, not the smallest h possible.
  • A sharp corner, such as |w| at 0, has no derivative; the numerical estimates disagree there.

Check your understanding

What is the derivative of L(w) = (w − 2)² at w = 5?

By the chain rule the derivative is 2(w − 2) × 1, which at w = 5 is 2 × 3 = 6. The loss itself is 9.

What is the gradient of f(x, y) = x² + 3y² at the point (1, 1)?

∂f/∂x = 2x = 2 and ∂f/∂y = 6y = 6. A gradient is a vector with one entry per variable, not a single number.

To make a loss smaller, in which direction should a small step change the parameters?

The gradient points uphill, so a small step against it is the fastest local way down. A step perpendicular to it barely changes the loss.

For one example with feature x and label y, what is the derivative of (w·x − y)² with respect to w?

Outer derivative 2(w·x − y), times the inner derivative of w·x − y with respect to w, which is x. Forgetting the inner factor is the classic chain-rule bug.

You check a gradient with a central difference in float64. Why is h = 10⁻¹³ a worse choice than h = 10⁻⁵?

f(w + h) and f(w − h) then agree in almost every digit, so their difference is mostly rounding error, and dividing by 2h magnifies it.
