Clustering with k-Means

Every model so far learned from labels. Often there are none: a pile of customers, documents or sensor readings, and the question is simply which of them belong together. Clustering looks for groups of similar points without being told what the groups are. k-means, the oldest and most widely used method, describes each group by its centre and assigns every point to the nearest centre.

The algorithm fits in two lines, runs on millions of points in under a second, and has well-known weaknesses: it needs k in advance, can get stuck in poor solutions, and assumes clusters are round blobs of similar size. This chapter runs it step by step, shows how k-means++ fixes the starting point, compares two ways of choosing k, and shows the shapes it cannot see.

  • Explain inertia and why k-means only finds a local minimum
  • Use k-means++ and multiple restarts
  • Choose k with the elbow method and the silhouette score, and know their limits
  • Recognise data that k-means will cluster wrongly

Lloyd's algorithm

Choose k starting centres. Then repeat two steps until nothing changes. Assign: give each point to its nearest centre. Update: move each centre to the mean of the points assigned to it. Each step can only lower the inertia, the sum of squared distances from points to their centres (also called the within-cluster sum of squares), so the algorithm always converges, usually in a few dozen iterations.

It converges to a local minimum, though, and which one depends on the starting centres. Two centres that start in the same true cluster may never separate, leaving another cluster to share a centre with its neighbour. Finding the global minimum is NP-hard in general, so in practice we start well and start several times.

k-means++ and restarts

k-means++ (David Arthur and Sergei Vassilvitskii, 2007) picks the first centre at random from the data, then picks each next centre at random with probability proportional to its squared distance from the nearest centre chosen so far. Far-away points are likely to be picked, so the starting centres spread out across the data. It comes with a guarantee: the expected inertia is within a factor of O(log k) of the optimum. On 2,000 points in 8 blobs, only 4% of single runs from random starting points ended within 1% of the best inertia found, and the worst was 8.3 times worse; with k-means++, 54% did and the worst was 1.55 times worse. Run several starts (n_init) and keep the one with the lowest inertia.

from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline

km = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KMeans(n_clusters=8, init="k-means++", n_init=10, random_state=0))
labels = km.fit_predict(X)
km[-1].inertia_, km[-1].cluster_centers_
# 1,000,000 points with 10 features, k = 10: about 0.8 s

Choosing k

Inertia always falls as k grows, reaching zero when every point is its own cluster, so you cannot simply minimise it. The elbow method plots inertia against k and looks for the point where adding clusters stops helping much. The elbow is often not sharp. The silhouette score compares, for each point, how close it is to its own cluster against the nearest other cluster, from −1 (wrong cluster) to +1 (well separated), and you pick the k with the highest average.

Neither knows what you want. On 2,000 points generated from 8 overlapping blobs, the silhouette was highest at k = 5 (0.73), not 8 (0.59), because neighbouring blobs merge into larger, better separated groups. That is a fair description of the data's geometry; whether 5 or 8 segments is right depends on what the clusters are for. Treat these scores as evidence, then look at the clusters, check that they are stable across restarts and subsamples, and ask whether they are useful.

from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score

for k in range(2, 11):
    km = KMeans(k, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit(X)
    print(k, km.inertia_, silhouette_score(X, km.labels_))
# 8 true blobs: silhouette 0.700 (k=3), 0.710 (4), 0.730 (5), 0.692 (6), 0.592 (8)

What k-means cannot see

Assigning each point to the nearest centre splits space into convex regions with straight borders between centres (a Voronoi diagram). So k-means assumes clusters that are roughly round, of similar spread, and separable by straight lines. It splits elongated clusters across their length, gives a large sparse cluster's edge to a small dense neighbour, and cannot follow curved shapes: on two interleaved half-moons it reached an adjusted Rand index of only 0.25 against the true groups, where 1 is perfect agreement. Because it uses Euclidean distance, unscaled features with large ranges dominate. For other shapes use density-based or hierarchical clustering (Chapter 26), or Gaussian mixture models, which allow elongated clusters of different sizes.

Use case: from telephone signals to image palettes

The algorithm now called k-means was worked out in 1957 by Stuart Lloyd at Bell Labs for a problem in telephone engineering. Pulse-code modulation sends a signal as a stream of numbers, each rounded to one of a small set of levels, and Lloyd wanted the levels that minimised the average squared rounding error. His answer, alternately assigning each sample to its nearest level and moving each level to the mean of its samples, circulated as an internal memo and was only published in 1982; James MacQueen had meanwhile named the method "k-means" in 1967. The same idea, under the name vector quantisation, became a workhorse of compression. Its most visible use is colour quantisation: an image with millions of distinct colours must be shown with a palette of, say, 256 for a GIF or an old display. Treat every pixel as a point in red-green-blue space, cluster with k = 256, and replace each pixel by its cluster's centre. Here k is not a question at all; it is fixed by the file format, and inertia is exactly the distortion you want to minimise, which is why k-means is so well suited to the job.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Clustering unscaled features, so one large-valued column decides everything.
  • Running once from random starting points instead of k-means++ with several restarts.
  • Treating the elbow or silhouette as the answer rather than as one piece of evidence about k.
  • Using k-means on curved, elongated or very unequal clusters.
  • Comparing inertia across different k or different scalings as if lower meant better.
  • Reading cluster labels as stable identities; cluster 3 in one run may be cluster 1 in the next.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How does k-means work?

How a strong answer sounds
You choose how many groups you want, k, and place k starting centres. Then you repeat two steps: assign every point to its nearest centre, and move each centre to the average of the points assigned to it. When the assignments stop changing, you are done. Each step reduces the total squared distance from points to their centres, so it always settles, but it can settle on a poor solution depending on where it started, so in practice you use a smart starting method called k-means++ and run it several times, keeping the best result.

Deeper: You are asked to segment customers with k-means. How do you choose k and check the result is meaningful?

How a strong answer sounds
First I would choose and scale the features, since k-means uses Euclidean distance and the clusters will reflect whichever features dominate it, and check for outliers and heavily skewed features. Then I would run k-means++ with several restarts for a range of k and look at inertia for an elbow and at the silhouette score, knowing that both describe geometry, not usefulness. I would check stability: do similar clusters appear across restarts and bootstrap samples? Then I would profile each cluster, its size and its feature means, and review them with the business to see whether they suggest different actions; a statistically neat k that nobody can act on is not the right answer. If the clusters look elongated or very unequal in size, I would try a Gaussian mixture or a density-based method and compare.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • k-means alternates assigning points to the nearest centre and moving centres to their points' mean.
  • It always converges, but only to a local minimum of inertia that depends on the start.
  • Use k-means++ initialisation and several restarts, and keep the lowest inertia.
  • The elbow and silhouette are evidence about k, not answers; usefulness decides.
  • It assumes round, similar-sized, linearly separable clusters; scale features first.
  • For other shapes, use Gaussian mixtures, DBSCAN or hierarchical clustering.

Check your understanding

What are the two steps of each k-means iteration?

Both steps can only reduce inertia, which is why Lloyd's algorithm always converges.

Why does k-means++ usually beat random starting centres?

Spread-out starts make it unlikely that two centres begin in the same true cluster. It improves the expected result but does not guarantee the optimum.

Why can't you choose k by minimising inertia?

More centres always fit the points at least as closely. The elbow method looks for where the improvement levels off instead.

Two interleaved half-moon shapes are clustered with k-means, k = 2. What typically happens?

Nearest-centre assignment makes straight borders between centres, so curved clusters are split across their shape.

A point has silhouette value close to −1. What does that suggest?

Silhouette is (b − a) / max(a, b): negative when the nearest other cluster (b) is closer than its own (a). Near 0 means it sits on a border.
