Ensembles & Stacking

Random forests and gradient boosting are ensembles of one kind of model. The same idea works across different kinds: average a gradient-boosted model, a neural network and a regularised linear model, or train a small model to combine their predictions. When the members make different mistakes, the combination is more accurate than any of them, and that trick has won a great many competitions.

It does not always work, and it is never free. Averaging a strong model with weak ones can make it worse; combining correlated models gains little; and stacking done carelessly leaks training data into the combiner and picks the most overfitted member. Every extra model also has to be trained, served, monitored and debugged. This chapter shows when combining helps, how to stack correctly with out-of-fold predictions, and how to weigh the gain against the cost.

Why diverse models help

Suppose several classifiers are each right 65% of the time, and they vote. If their mistakes were independent, the majority would be right far more often than any one of them, and more so the more voters there were; this is the Condorcet jury theorem from 1785. Real models trained on the same data are not independent: they make many of the same mistakes, on the same hard cases. What an ensemble gains depends on how correlated its members' errors are, exactly as in the variance formula of Chapter 18. Diversity comes from different model families, different features, different training samples, or different random seeds, roughly in decreasing order of usefulness.

Voting and averaging

Hard voting takes the majority class. Soft voting averages the predicted probabilities, which uses the members' confidence and usually works better, provided the probabilities are on comparable scales (Chapter 32). For regression, average the predictions. Weighted averages give better members more say; the weights can be tuned on validation data. Equal weights are a surprisingly strong default when members are similarly good, and a poor one when they are not: on California housing, the plain average of a gradient-boosted model (RMSE 0.468), a random forest (0.520), k-NN (0.630) and ridge regression (0.735) had RMSE 0.536, worse than the boosted model alone.

Stacking with out-of-fold predictions

Stacking (David Wolpert, 1992) trains a second-level model, the meta-learner, to combine the base models' predictions. The trap is what it learns from. If base models predict on the same rows they were trained on, an overfitted member looks perfect, and the meta-learner gives it most of the weight; on new data that member is the worst. The fix is out-of-fold predictions: split the training data into k folds, train each base model k times, each time predicting only the fold it did not see, so every training row gets an honest prediction from every model. The meta-learner, usually something simple like ridge or logistic regression, is fitted on those, and the base models are then refitted on all the training data. Blending is the cheaper variant: fit the meta-learner on a single held-out slice instead of out-of-fold predictions.

Things to try.

  • Voting, 65% accuracy, ρ = 0.3, 9 voters. The majority is right 72.3% of the time. With independent voters it would be 82.8%, and with 25 independent voters 94.0%; at ρ = 0.3, 25 voters reach only 74.7%.
  • Raise ρ to 0.9. Nine voters reach 65.7%, almost exactly one model's accuracy: near-copies add nothing.
  • Try 55% and 75% accuracy. Nine independent voters lift 55% to 62.3% and 75% to 95.0%. Strong, independent members are where voting pays off most.
  • Stacking, in-sample predictions. 1-NN has a training error of 0.00, since every training point is its own nearest neighbour, so the meta-learner gives it all the weight (1.00). Test RMSE 0.468: exactly 1-NN's, and worse than 15-NN alone (0.329).
  • Switch to out-of-fold predictions. 1-NN's honest error is 0.455 and its weight drops to 0.00; the meta-learner uses 15-NN (0.68) and the cubic (0.33). Test RMSE 0.320, better than the best single model, 0.329, and than the plain average of all four, 0.371.

The cost side

On California housing, a carefully built stack of four models matched the best single model to within 0.0002 RMSE and took 85 times as long to train. That is common: once one strong model dominates, combining adds little. When the gain is real, the bill still includes training and serving several models, larger latency and memory, more ways for features to drift, and harder debugging and explanation. Ensembles shine where small accuracy gains are worth a lot, such as competitions, or where the members are genuinely different, such as a model on images combined with one on tabular data. In everyday production, a single well-tuned gradient-boosted model, or a two-model average, is often the better trade.

Use case: the ensemble that was too expensive to ship

The Netflix Prize (Chapter 42) was won by enormous ensembles: the teams at the top blended hundreds of models. In 2012 Xavier Amatriain and Justin Basilico of Netflix explained what the company had actually used. After the first Progress Prize in 2007, the winning team handed over a blend of 107 algorithms, the product of more than 2,000 hours of work. Netflix took the two best underlying methods, a matrix factorisation that scored an RMSE of 0.8914 on its own and a restricted Boltzmann machine at 0.8990, and found that a linear blend of just those two reached 0.88. They put that into production, after engineering it to handle billions of ratings. For the grand prize ensemble, which improved accuracy further, they wrote that the additional gains they measured offline did not seem to justify the engineering effort needed to bring them into production; by then their business was also shifting from DVD rentals towards streaming, where other signals mattered more than predicted star ratings. Two diverse, strong models captured most of the value; the other hundred-odd were a competition's luxury.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Fitting the meta-learner on in-sample predictions, which rewards the most overfitted member.
  • Averaging a strong model with much weaker ones at equal weight.
  • Combining near-identical models and expecting a large gain.
  • Soft voting with uncalibrated scores on different scales.
  • Evaluating the ensemble on data used to choose its weights.
  • Ignoring serving cost and latency when comparing to a single model.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Why does combining several models often beat the best one?

How a strong answer sounds
Each model makes some mistakes. If different models make different mistakes, then when they are averaged or vote, the errors partly cancel while the parts they get right add up, so the combination is more accurate than any one of them. The key word is different: if all the models make the same mistakes, combining them changes nothing. That is why ensembles work best when the members are similarly good but built differently, for example a gradient-boosted model, a neural network and a linear model.

Deeper: How would you build a stacked ensemble without leaking, and how would you decide whether to deploy it?

How a strong answer sounds
I would generate out-of-fold predictions: split the training data into k folds, train each base model on k minus one folds and predict the remaining one, so every training row has a prediction from a model that never saw it. The meta-learner, usually a regularised linear model or logistic regression, perhaps with non-negative weights, is fitted on those predictions, and the base models are then refitted on all the training data for use at prediction time. If I tune anything on the stack, I would evaluate it on a separate test set or with nested cross-validation. To decide on deployment, I would compare the stack with the best single model on the test set with a confidence interval, check that the gain is stable across segments and time, and weigh it against the extra training cost, serving latency, memory and maintenance of several models. If the gain is within noise, or the weights put nearly everything on one member, I would ship the single model.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Ensembles help when members are accurate and their errors are not too correlated.
  • Soft voting or averaging is a strong default for similarly good, diverse members.
  • Weak members can drag an equal-weight average below the best model.
  • Stack on out-of-fold predictions; in-sample stacking rewards overfitting.
  • Weigh every accuracy gain against training, serving and maintenance cost.

Check your understanding

Nine classifiers are each 65% accurate. When does majority voting help most?

Independent mistakes rarely line up into a wrong majority. Correlated mistakes do, so the gain shrinks as correlation rises.

A meta-learner is fitted on the base models' predictions on their own training rows. What tends to happen?

A model that memorised its training data has near-zero in-sample error, so it appears to be the best predictor.

How do out-of-fold predictions work?

Every row gets honest predictions from models that never saw it, which is what the meta-learner needs.

What distinguishes blending from stacking?

It is simpler and cheaper, at the price of fewer rows for training base models and a noisier meta-learner.

A stack improves test RMSE by 0.1% over the best single model and needs four models in production. What is the sensible conclusion?

Check the gain's confidence interval (Chapter 36) and weigh it against latency, memory and maintenance. A tiny gain rarely pays.
