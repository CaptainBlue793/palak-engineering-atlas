Regression Metrics & Baselines

Two forecasting models, the same test set, and three metrics: one says model A is better, another says model B, and the third says both are excellent when neither is. Nothing is wrong with the arithmetic. Each regression metric weighs errors differently, and each one hides something.

This chapter covers the five metrics you will meet, MAE, RMSE, R², MAPE and WAPE, what each rewards and conceals, and the baselines that turn any of them into something meaningful. The simulator gives the same predictions three different verdicts, depending only on which metric you ask.

  • Recognise when MAPE breaks, and use WAPE or a scaled error instead
  • Read R² correctly, and know why it can mislead across datasets
  • Build dummy and naive baselines, including a seasonal one for time series

The five metrics

   MAE   =  mean |y − ŷ|                      the typical miss, in the target's units
   RMSE  =  √( mean (y − ŷ)² )                 also in the target's units, but large misses weigh more
   R²    =  1 − Σ(y − ŷ)² / Σ(y − ȳ)²          the share of the target's variance explained
   MAPE  =  mean |y − ŷ| / |y|                 the typical miss as a percentage of the actual
   WAPE  =  Σ|y − ŷ| / Σ|y|                    total miss as a percentage of the total actual

Things to try.

  • One big miss, size 60. MAE prefers B (3.95 against A's 6.00); RMSE prefers A (6.00 against B's 13.45). Slide the miss down to 20 and B wins on everything; up to 100 and B's R² turns negative, worse than predicting the mean, while its MAE still edges A's.
  • Some products sell almost nothing. MAPE ranks B far ahead (10.1% against 47.7%), entirely because of three products that sell one or two units. WAPE and MAE rank A ahead (7.1% against 12.7% WAPE): A loses far fewer units in total. B also forecasts low on every big seller, the bias MAPE rewards.
  • Same errors, different spread. The model's errors never change (MAE £6.9k). With prices spread ±15 its R² is 0.77; at ±40 it is 0.97; at ±120 it is 0.996. Nothing about the model improved.

RMSE and MAE disagree for a reason

RMSE is always at least as large as MAE, and the gap between them measures how uneven the errors are: equal when every miss is the same size, far apart when a few misses are huge. When the two rank models differently, they are telling you the models have different error shapes, which is useful information. Decide which shape you can live with. A delivery-time model that is usually within two minutes but occasionally off by an hour may be worse for customers than one that is always within five.

Why MAPE breaks

MAPE divides each error by its actual. For an item that sold 1 unit, predicting 3 is a 200% error, which counts as much in the average as being wrong by 200% on an item that sold 10,000. Where an actual is zero the error is infinite or undefined. MAPE is also asymmetric: an under-forecast can never be worse than 100%, while an over-forecast has no limit, so a model trained to minimise MAPE learns to forecast low. WAPE (also called weighted MAPE) fixes the first two problems by dividing total error by total volume, so each product counts in proportion to how much it sells; scaled errors, which divide by a naive forecast's error, fix all three and are the standard in forecasting competitions.

What R² does and does not say

R² compares the model with a single constant, the mean of the test targets. 1 is perfect, 0 is no better than that constant, and on a test set it can be negative, meaning the model is worse than predicting the mean. Two warnings. R² depends on how spread out the targets are, not only on how good the model is: the same predictions, with the same errors in pounds, score a high R² on a test set of very different houses and a low one on a set of similar houses. So R² can compare models on the same data, but not one model across datasets. And "high R²" says nothing about whether the errors are acceptable for the decision: an R² of 0.98 can still mean misses of £40,000.

Baselines

A metric becomes meaningful next to a baseline: the score of the simplest prediction anyone could make. For a regression model that is the training mean or median (DummyRegressor). For time series it is the naive forecast, tomorrow equals today, or the seasonal naive forecast, next Monday equals last Monday, which is remarkably hard to beat for anything with a weekly pattern.

from sklearn.dummy import DummyRegressor

base = DummyRegressor(strategy="median").fit(X_train, y_train)

# time series: seasonal naive, the same day last week
df["naive_7"] = df.groupby("store")["sales"].shift(7)
mae_naive = (df["sales"] - df["naive_7"]).abs().mean()

📏
Use case: a forecasting error that works everywhere

In 2006 Rob Hyndman and Anne Koehler published "Another look at measures of forecast accuracy" in the International Journal of Forecasting, surveying the problems with the metrics forecasters then used. Percentage errors were undefined or exploded when series touched zero, which intermittent sales data does constantly; relative errors could divide by zero too; and scale-dependent errors such as MAE could not be compared across series measured in different units. Their proposal was the mean absolute scaled error (MASE): the model's MAE divided by the in-sample MAE of a naive one-step forecast. It is defined whenever the series is not completely constant, it means the same thing for every series (below 1 beats the naive method), and it can be averaged across thousands of products. Scaled errors in this family became standard in large forecasting competitions, including the M5 competition on Walmart data (Chapter 48).

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Reporting one metric and not saying which errors it emphasises.
  • Using MAPE when some actuals are near zero, or averaging it across items of wildly different size.
  • Comparing R² across datasets with different spreads of the target.
  • No baseline, especially no seasonal naive baseline for time series.
  • Reporting MSE in squared units to people who need to act on it.
  • Forgetting that mean_absolute_percentage_error returns a fraction, and reporting 0.12 as 0.12%.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is the difference between RMSE and MAE, and which would you report?

How a strong answer sounds
Both measure the typical size of an error in the target's units. MAE averages the absolute errors, so every unit of error counts the same. RMSE squares errors before averaging and takes the square root at the end, so large errors count disproportionately; it is always at least as big as MAE, and the gap shows how uneven the errors are. I would report MAE when the cost of a mistake grows roughly in proportion to its size and I want a number stakeholders understand, and RMSE when large errors are especially damaging, or when the model was trained on squared error. Often I report both, plus a baseline, because when they disagree about which model is better it means the models have different error shapes.

Deeper: A demand-forecasting team reports a MAPE of 48% and wants a better model. What would you look at first?

How a strong answer sounds
I would check whether the number is driven by the metric rather than the model. MAPE divides by actual sales, so products that sell zero to a few units a day produce enormous percentage errors and dominate the average, even if they are a trivial share of volume. I would compute WAPE, which weights by volume, and the MAPE restricted to high-volume items; if WAPE is, say, 15%, the business problem is very different from what 48% suggests. I would also compare against a seasonal naive baseline with a scaled error like MASE, which tells us whether the model beats "same day last week" at all. Then error by segment: by product velocity, store and day of week, to find where the real misses are. I would also point out MAPE's bias towards under-forecasting, which matters if the team is about to train on it; for stocking decisions, a quantile loss at the service level they need is usually the better target.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • MAE is the typical miss; RMSE weights large misses more; the gap between them shows how uneven the errors are.
  • When RMSE and MAE rank models differently, the models have different error shapes; choose the shape you can live with.
  • MAPE breaks near zero and rewards under-forecasting; prefer WAPE or a scaled error.
  • R² measures improvement over the mean and depends on the target's spread; compare it only on the same data.
  • Every metric needs a baseline beside it: the mean or median, or the seasonal naive forecast for time series.
  • Break errors down by segment before trusting an overall number.

Check your understanding

When is RMSE equal to MAE?

RMSE ≥ MAE always, with equality only when all absolute errors are equal. The more uneven the errors, the bigger the gap.

Why is MAPE a poor metric for products that often sell 0 or 1 unit?

An error of 2 units on an item that sold 1 is a 200% error, and an actual of 0 makes it undefined. Those items dominate the average.

A model's R² on the test set is 0. What does that mean?

R² compares the model's squared error with that of the constant mean. 0 means equal; a negative R² means worse than that constant.

Which baseline should a daily sales forecast with a strong weekly pattern beat?

The seasonal naive forecast captures the weekly pattern for free and is a demanding baseline. A model that cannot beat it adds nothing.

Model A has the lower MAE; model B has the lower RMSE. What does that tell you?

Lower MAE means smaller typical errors; lower RMSE means fewer or smaller big errors. A's error distribution has a heavier tail.
