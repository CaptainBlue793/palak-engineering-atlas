Logistic Regression

Linear regression predicts a number. For a yes-or-no question, will this customer cancel, is this transaction fraud, you want a probability instead: a number between 0 and 1. Logistic regression gets one by taking the same weighted sum of features and passing it through the sigmoid, an S-shaped curve that squashes any score into that range.

It is the first classifier to try on almost any problem: fast, hard to overfit with sensible regularisation, its probabilities are usually well calibrated, and its coefficients read as effects on the odds. The simulator fits one to churn data. Shape the curve by hand, let gradient descent find the best one, move the decision threshold, and then give it data where the classes do not overlap at all and watch the weights try to run off to infinity.

From a score to a probability

Start with the linear score z = w·x + b, which can be any number. The sigmoid function turns it into a probability:

                 1
   σ(z)  =  ───────────        z = 0 → 0.5      z = 2 → 0.88      z = −2 → 0.12      z = ±∞ → 1 or 0
             1 + e^(−z)

   P(y = 1 | x)  =  σ(w·x + b)

Where the score is zero the probability is exactly one half, and the set of points where that happens is the decision boundary: a single point for one feature, a straight line for two, a flat plane for more. Logistic regression is a linear classifier: however you set the weights, the boundary between "more likely yes" and "more likely no" is straight. Curved boundaries need transformed features or a different model.

Read in reverse, the model says something simple: the log-odds of the event is linear in the features. The odds are p / (1 − p), so a probability of 0.8 is odds of 4 to 1, and log(odds) = w·x + b.

Fitting: log loss and gradient descent

There is no normal equation for logistic regression. Its loss is the log loss from Chapter 6, averaged over the training examples, and setting its gradient to zero gives equations with no closed-form solution. They are solved iteratively. The loss is convex, a single bowl with no false minima, so gradient descent or a smarter relative of it always finds the best weights. The gradient has the same shape as linear regression's: the error times the input.

   L(w) = −(1/n) Σ [ y log p + (1 − y) log(1 − p) ],   p = σ(Xw + b)
   ∇L(w) = (1/n) Xᵀ (p − y)                            predicted probability minus label, times input
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

X = np.column_stack([days_since_login, support_tickets, tenure_months])
model = LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000).fit(X_train, churned_train)

model.coef_                       # [[ 0.079  0.506 -0.049]]  log-odds per day, per ticket, per month
np.exp(model.coef_)               # [[ 1.082  1.657  0.952]]  odds ratios
model.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1]  # probability of churn for each customer
model.score(X_test, churned_test)   # 0.916 accuracy, but 90.9% of customers do not churn at all

Two details in that listing matter. scikit-learn's LogisticRegression is regularised by default, with an L2 penalty of strength set by C=1.0; on this data it barely changes the coefficients, but on small or separable data it matters (below, and Chapter 12). And look at that accuracy: 91.6% sounds good until you notice that predicting "no churn" for everyone would score 90.9%. Accuracy is the wrong lens for this model, which is why the next chapter exists.

Reading the coefficients

A coefficient is the change in log-odds per unit of its feature, with the others held fixed. Exponentiate it and you get an odds ratio: the factor by which the odds multiply. From the listing above, each extra support ticket multiplies the odds of churning by 1.66, and each extra month of tenure multiplies them by 0.95, a 5% reduction per month. These are multiplicative effects on odds, not additive effects on probability: an extra ticket raises a 2% churn probability to about 3.3%, and a 50% probability to about 62%.

Things to try.

  • Shape the curve by hand, then press "Fit by gradient descent". The best curve crosses 50% at 24.9 days with a steepness of 0.21 per day, against 25 and 0.17 in the process that generated the data. Its log loss is 0.238, down from 0.489 at the starting curve; it catches 33 of 36 churners at 90% accuracy.
  • Move the threshold to 0.2, then to 0.8. At 0.2 all 36 churners are flagged and accuracy falls to 87% from the extra false alarms; at 0.8 only 29 are caught. The curve itself never moved.
  • Fit with L2 (C = 1). The steepness drops to 0.15 per day: on 60 examples the penalty is noticeable. The training log loss rises slightly (0.253), which is the price of a less confident model.
  • Perfectly separable data, then "Fit". The weight reaches 0.41 after 100 steps, 0.76 after 1,000 and 1.09 after 5,000, still climbing, while the log loss creeps towards zero. Now "Fit with L2": the weight stops at 0.20 and a customer at 30 days gets 0.74 instead of 0.99.

Probability first, threshold second

A classifier that outputs probabilities makes two separate decisions easy to keep separate. The model estimates how likely churn is; the business decides at what probability it is worth acting. If a retention offer is cheap and losing a customer is expensive, act at 20%; if the offer is costly, act at 60%. scikit-learn's predict silently uses 0.5, which is right only when both kinds of mistake cost the same and the classes are balanced. Use predict_proba and choose the threshold on purpose (Chapter 8; the costs in Chapter 47).

p = model.predict_proba(X_val)[:, 1]
act = p >= 0.2                    # a business decision, not a property of the model

Perfect separation

If some straight boundary separates the classes with no mistakes at all, the log loss can always be made smaller by making the sigmoid steeper: every training probability moves closer to exactly 0 or 1. The best unregularised weights are infinite. Gradient descent never stops increasing them, and libraries either stop at a tolerance with huge coefficients and a warning, or report that the fit did not converge. The probabilities become absurdly confident, which is a problem for any new example near the boundary. The fix is regularisation: an L2 penalty makes large weights cost something, so the optimum is finite. It is one reason scikit-learn regularises by default.

💳
Use case: credit scorecards

Most retail lending decisions in the UK and the US have long run on scorecards: logistic regression models predicting the probability that an applicant defaults, built from features such as payment history, utilisation of existing credit and length of credit history. Lenders keep using them, despite more flexible models being available, for reasons this chapter makes concrete. The output is a probability that feeds directly into pricing and limits. The model is a sum of per-feature contributions, so it can be published as a table of points, and each declined applicant can be told which factors counted most against them, which consumer-credit regulation in many countries requires. And its behaviour is predictable: no feature can have an effect that flips direction between applicants. Building a fair and accurate scorecard still takes care, around which features are allowed, how missing data is handled and how the model is monitored, and Chapter 44 looks at what happens when risk scores meet fairness questions.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Using predict and its hidden 0.5 threshold when the costs or the class balance say otherwise.
  • Judging the model by accuracy on imbalanced data, where "always no" scores nearly as well.
  • Reading a coefficient as a change in probability. It is a change in log-odds; the effect on probability depends on where you start.
  • Forgetting that scikit-learn regularises by default, and then comparing coefficients with an unregularised statistics package.
  • Unscaled features, which make the default penalty hit some coefficients much harder than others, and slow the solver.
  • Ignoring a convergence warning, which often signals separation or a feature that perfectly predicts the label: a leak.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How does logistic regression produce a probability, and why is it called a linear classifier?

How a strong answer sounds
It computes a weighted sum of the features plus a constant, like linear regression, and passes that score through the sigmoid function, which maps any number into the range 0 to 1, with a score of zero giving exactly 0.5. Equivalently, it models the log-odds of the positive class as a linear function of the features. It is a linear classifier because the decision boundary, where the probability crosses a threshold, is where the linear score equals a constant, which is a straight line or flat plane in feature space. It is trained by minimising log loss with an iterative optimiser, since there is no closed-form solution, but the loss is convex, so the optimum is unique.

Deeper: Your logistic regression gives a coefficient of 9.7 for one feature and prints a convergence warning. What is going on?

How a strong answer sounds
A very large coefficient together with a convergence warning usually means separation: some combination of features splits the classes perfectly or almost perfectly in the training data, so the log loss keeps improving as the weights grow and the unregularised optimum is at infinity. The first question is whether that feature is legitimate. Very often it is a leak, something recorded after the outcome, like a cancellation date in a churn model, and it must be removed. If it is legitimate but the data is small, the model is overconfident: I would add or strengthen L2 regularisation, which makes the optimum finite, or use a penalised method designed for separation. I would also check feature scaling, since an unscaled feature with tiny units can legitimately need a large coefficient, and an under-iterated solver, by raising max_iter. Then I would look at the predicted probabilities on validation data: separation shows up as many predictions at 0.00 and 1.00, and calibration (Chapter 32) will be poor near the boundary.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Logistic regression passes a linear score through the sigmoid to get a probability; the log-odds are linear in the features.
  • Its decision boundary is straight; it is a linear classifier.
  • It is fitted by minimising log loss iteratively; the loss is convex, so the optimum is unique.
  • exp(coefficient) is an odds ratio; effects on probability depend on the starting point.
  • Output probabilities with predict_proba and choose the threshold from the costs.
  • Perfectly separable data sends unregularised weights to infinity; L2 regularisation, on by default in scikit-learn, keeps them finite.

Check your understanding

The linear score for a customer is z = 0. What probability does logistic regression give?

σ(0) = 1 / (1 + e⁰) = 0.5. The decision boundary at a 0.5 threshold is exactly where the score is zero.

A coefficient for "support tickets" is 0.5. What does exp(0.5) ≈ 1.65 mean?

Coefficients act on the log-odds, so their exponentials multiply the odds. The change in probability depends on the starting probability.

Why is logistic regression fitted iteratively rather than with a formula?

The equations are non-linear in the weights, so they are solved numerically. The loss is convex, so there are no false minima to worry about.

A model reaches 91.6% accuracy on churn data where 9.1% of customers churn. What should you conclude?

Always predicting "no" scores 90.9%. Accuracy hides how well the rare class is caught; precision, recall and the model's probabilities tell you that.

On training data where the classes are perfectly separable, what happens to unregularised logistic regression's weights?

A steeper sigmoid always lowers the loss when no example is misclassified, so the optimum is at infinity. An L2 penalty makes it finite.
