Cross-Validation & Hyperparameter Search

A single validation split gives one noisy number. Cross-validation gives several, by rotating which part of the training data is held out, and averages them into an estimate that depends much less on the luck of one split. It is how every model and every setting in the rest of this course is compared.

Then comes the search. A model with a few hyperparameters has thousands of possible settings, and the obvious way to choose one, try many and keep the best cross-validated score, has a catch: the best score out of many is an overestimate, because some of its lead is luck. This chapter covers the CV schemes, grid and random search, and nested cross-validation, the honest way to report a tuned model. The simulator shows the folds, the inflation, and why random search usually beats a grid.

k-fold cross-validation

Split the training data into k equal folds. Train on k − 1 of them and score on the one left out; repeat k times so every fold is held out once; average the k scores. Every training row is used for validation exactly once and for training k − 1 times. The standard deviation of the k scores is a rough measure of how much the estimate depends on which rows were held out.

from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score, StratifiedKFold, GroupKFold, TimeSeriesSplit

cv = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)
scores = cross_val_score(model, X_train, y_train, cv=cv, scoring="roc_auc")
print(f"AUC {scores.mean():.3f} ± {scores.std():.3f}")

cross_val_score(model, X_train, y_train, cv=GroupKFold(n_splits=5), groups=customer_id)
cross_val_score(model, X_train, y_train, cv=TimeSeriesSplit(n_splits=5, gap=7))

Searching hyperparameters

A grid search tries every combination of a list of values for each hyperparameter, cross-validates each, and keeps the best. A random search draws a fixed number of combinations from distributions you specify, usually log-uniform for things like learning rates and penalties. A Bayesian or adaptive search, in libraries such as Optuna, uses the results so far to choose the next combination to try.

from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV, RandomizedSearchCV
from scipy.stats import loguniform

pipe = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), SVC())
grid = GridSearchCV(pipe, {"svc__C": np.logspace(-2, 3, 12), "svc__gamma": np.logspace(-4, 1, 12)}, cv=inner)
rand = RandomizedSearchCV(pipe, {"svc__C": loguniform(1e-2, 1e3), "svc__gamma": loguniform(1e-4, 1e1)},
                          n_iter=30, cv=inner, random_state=0)
grid.fit(X_train, y_train).best_score_     # 144 combinations: 0.710 on a noisy 200-row problem
rand.fit(X_train, y_train).best_score_     # 30 combinations:  0.705, at a fifth of the cost

The winner's curse, and nested cross-validation

Every cross-validated score is a true performance plus some noise. When you try many settings and keep the highest score, you preferentially keep settings whose noise happened to be positive. The more you try, the further the winning score drifts above the winner's real performance. That is not a bug in grid search; it is what selecting a maximum does. So the best score from a search must never be reported as the model's performance.

Nested cross-validation fixes it by putting the whole search inside an outer loop. Each outer fold holds out data the search never sees; the search runs on the rest with its own inner folds; the tuned model is scored on the held-out outer fold. The average outer score estimates how well the procedure, search included, performs on new data.

# inner loop chooses hyperparameters; outer loop scores that choice on data the search never saw
outer = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=2)
nested = cross_val_score(GridSearchCV(pipe, param_grid, cv=inner), X_train, y_train, cv=outer)
nested.mean()        # 0.635 ± 0.082, against the search's own best score of 0.710

On that small, noisy problem the search's best score overstated the procedure by 0.075 of accuracy. With more data or fewer combinations the gap shrinks; it never goes negative on average. A held-out test set touched once does the same job as the outer loop when you have enough data.

Things to try.

  • The folds: KFold, then Stratified, then Grouped, then Time series. Plain KFold cuts through groups; stratified evens out the positives per fold; grouped keeps every group on one side; time series never validates on rows older than its training data.
  • The winner's curse, 300 validation rows. One setting: 80.0% on average, the truth. Best of 30 equally good settings: 84.7%. Best of 100: 85.8%. Best of 500: 87.0%. None of them is better than 80%.
  • Keep 100 settings, change the validation size. With 100 rows the winner reports 90.0%; with 5,000 rows, 81.4%. Noisier scores leave more room for luck to win.
  • Grid vs random. The 3 × 3 grid's best is 70.8%, because none of its three learning rates lands in the narrow good band. Nine random trials average 77.2% over 2,000 draws, and some draws come close to the best possible 82.8%.

Use case: why random search beats a grid

In 2012 James Bergstra and Yoshua Bengio published "Random Search for Hyper-Parameter Optimization" in the Journal of Machine Learning Research. They tuned neural networks on several datasets and found that, for the same number of trials, random search found settings as good as or better than grid search, often with far fewer trials. Their explanation is geometric. In most problems only a few hyperparameters matter much, and which ones varies by dataset. A 3 × 3 grid over two hyperparameters tries only three distinct values of each; if only the first matters, six of the nine trials are wasted repeating values already tested. Nine random points try nine distinct values of every hyperparameter. The paper is a large part of why random search, and then adaptive methods built on it, replaced grids as the default for anything beyond two or three hyperparameters.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Reporting the search's best CV score as the model's expected performance.
  • Preprocessing outside the cross-validation loop, so every fold sees statistics computed with its own validation rows (Chapter 24).
  • Using plain KFold on grouped or time-ordered data.
  • Linear grids for rates and penalties, wasting most trials in one corner of a log-scaled space.
  • Fine grids over many hyperparameters, whose cost multiplies with every one added.
  • Comparing two models whose scores differ by less than their fold-to-fold spread as if one clearly won (Chapter 36).

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is k-fold cross-validation and why use it instead of a single validation split?

How a strong answer sounds
You split the training data into k parts, train on k − 1 of them and validate on the remaining one, and repeat so each part is the validation set once, then average the k scores. Every row is used for validation exactly once, so the estimate uses all the data and depends much less on the luck of which rows ended up in one validation set; the spread across folds also tells you how stable it is. The cost is k model fits. The folds must respect the data's structure: stratified for imbalanced classes, grouped when several rows belong to one entity, and forward-chaining in time for forecasting.

Deeper: You tried 500 hyperparameter settings and the best had a CV accuracy of 92%. What accuracy do you expect in production, and how would you get a trustworthy estimate?

How a strong answer sounds
Somewhat below 92%. Selecting the maximum of 500 noisy estimates picks settings whose noise happened to be favourable, so the winning score is biased upwards, and the bias grows with the number of settings tried and the noise in each estimate, which depends on how much validation data there is. Many of the top settings are probably equivalent within noise. For a trustworthy number I would either score the chosen setting once on a held-out test set that played no part in the search, or run nested cross-validation, where an outer loop scores the whole search procedure on data it never saw. I would also look at the spread of the top settings' scores and prefer a simpler or more stable setting among those statistically tied with the best, and for production I would check the evaluation split mirrors production, in time or by entity, because that usually matters more than the selection bias.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • k-fold CV rotates the validation fold and averages; report the mean with its spread.
  • The CV scheme must mirror production: stratified, grouped or time-series as needed.
  • Random search covers more distinct values of the hyperparameters that matter than a grid with the same budget.
  • The best score from a search is optimistic; the more settings tried and the noisier each score, the more so.
  • Nested CV, or an untouched test set, gives an honest estimate of a tuned model.
  • Search on log scales for rates and penalties, and keep preprocessing inside the CV loop.

Check your understanding

In 5-fold cross-validation, how many times is each training row used for validation?

Each row belongs to exactly one fold, which is held out once; in the other four rounds the row is used for training.

Why is the best score from a large hyperparameter search optimistic?

Each score is truth plus noise. Taking the maximum preferentially keeps positive noise, so the winning score overstates the winner's real performance.

What does nested cross-validation estimate?

The inner loop does the search; the outer loop scores its result on data the search never saw, so the search's own optimism is excluded.

Only one of two hyperparameters really affects performance. With 9 trials, why does random search usually do better than a 3 × 3 grid?

The grid repeats the same three values of each hyperparameter. Random points have distinct values in every dimension, so the important one is explored three times more finely.

Which CV scheme fits a model that forecasts next week's sales from past weeks?

Each validation fold must come after its training data, as in production. Shuffled folds let the model learn from the future.
