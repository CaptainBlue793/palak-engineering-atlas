Probability

A classifier's output is a probability. A dataset is a sample from some process you cannot see. A test score is one draw from a distribution of scores you might have got with different data. Probability is the language for all three, and a few of its ideas carry most of the weight in machine learning: distributions, conditional probability, Bayes' rule, and expectation.

This page teaches them by simulation rather than proof. NumPy can draw a million random examples in a fraction of a second, and counting what happened is often the clearest way to see why a formula is right. The simulator does that for the single most useful and most misjudged idea here: what a positive result means when the thing you are looking for is rare.

  • Describe a random variable by its distribution, and pick the right one for counts, yes/no outcomes and measurements
  • Compute conditional probabilities and recognise when events are independent
  • Apply Bayes' rule, and explain the base-rate effect with natural frequencies
  • Compute an expectation and a variance, and simulate them with NumPy
  • Check a probability calculation by simulation

Random variables and distributions

A random variable is a quantity whose value depends on chance: whether the next transaction is fraud, how many requests arrive in the next second, a house's sale price. Its distribution says how likely each value is. A handful of distributions describe most of what you will model.

DistributionDescribesParametersNumPyIn ML
BernoulliOne yes/no outcomep, the chance of yesrng.random(n) < pEvery binary label; logistic regression predicts p
BinomialNumber of yeses in n triesn, prng.binomial(n, p)Correct answers out of n test examples
PoissonCount of events in an intervalλ, the average countrng.poisson(lam)Clicks, arrivals, sales per day
UniformEvery value in a range equally likelylow, highrng.uniform(a, b)Random search over hyperparameters
Normal (Gaussian)Measurements that cluster around a meanmean μ, standard deviation σrng.normal(mu, sigma)Noise in regression; averages of many things
 
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

x = rng.normal(0, 1, size=1_000_000)       # a million draws from N(0, 1)
np.mean(np.abs(x) < 1)                    # 0.6823  about 68% within one standard deviation
np.mean(np.abs(x) < 2)                    # 0.9544  about 95% within two
np.mean(np.abs(x) < 3)                    # 0.9973  almost everything within three

The trick in that last block, taking the mean of a boolean array to get a fraction, is how you turn any question of the form "how often does this happen?" into one line of NumPy.

Conditional probability and independence

Conditional probability is the probability of one thing given that another is known to be true, written P(A | B). It is the probability of both, divided by the probability of the condition:

                 P(A and B)
   P(A | B)  =  ───────────           "out of the cases where B happened, how often did A?"
                    P(B)

Two events are independent when knowing one tells you nothing about the other: P(A | B) = P(A), or equivalently P(A and B) = P(A) · P(B). Two fair coin flips are independent. A customer's first and second purchase amounts are not. Much of machine learning is the search for features that are not independent of the label, and Naive Bayes (Chapter 21) is built on assuming, wrongly but usefully, that features are independent of each other once the label is known.

The order of the condition matters, and confusing P(A | B) with P(B | A) is one of the most common reasoning errors there is. "Most fraudulent transactions are made at night" is P(night | fraud). It does not mean most night-time transactions are fraud, P(fraud | night), which is likely tiny. Bayes' rule is the bridge between the two.

Bayes' rule and the base rate

                    P(B | A) · P(A)              P(flagged | fraud) · P(fraud)
   P(A | B)  =  ─────────────────────   e.g.   ─────────────────────────────────
                        P(B)                             P(flagged)

P(A) is the prior, what you believed before the evidence; P(B | A) is the likelihood of the evidence if A is true; and P(A | B) is the posterior. The denominator, the overall chance of the evidence, adds up both ways it can happen: P(B) = P(B | A)·P(A) + P(B | not A)·P(not A).

The striking consequence is the base-rate effect. Suppose 1% of cases are positive, a detector catches 99% of positives, and it wrongly flags 5% of negatives. What fraction of flagged cases are really positive? Most people guess around 95%. Think in counts instead of percentages, which researchers such as Gerd Gigerenzer showed makes this kind of problem far easier for doctors and students alike. Out of 10,000 cases, 100 are positive and the detector flags 99 of them. Of the 9,900 negatives, it flags 5%, which is 495. So 99 of the 594 flags are real: 16.7%. The detector is excellent; the positives are just rare, and the false alarms come from a much larger pool.

n = 1_000_000
positive = rng.random(n) < 0.01                       # base rate 1%
flagged = np.where(positive, rng.random(n) < 0.99,   # catches 99% of positives
                              rng.random(n) < 0.05)   # flags 5% of negatives
(positive & flagged).sum() / flagged.sum()          # 0.167, exactly what Bayes' rule says

Things to try.

  • The defaults: 1% positive, 99% caught, 5% false alarms. One flag in six is real, while the overall accuracy is 95%.
  • Lower the base rate to 0.1%, as for card fraud. About one flag in fifty is real, and accuracy barely moves. This is why Chapter 23 says accuracy lies.
  • Now raise sensitivity to 99.9%. Almost nothing changes. The problem is not missed positives; it is the false alarms.
  • Cut the false-positive rate to 0.1%. Precision jumps. With rare positives, the false-positive rate is the number that decides whether the flags are worth reading.
  • Set the base rate to 50%. Now nearly every flag is real. The same detector, on a different population, means something completely different.

Expectation and variance

The expected value of a random variable is its long-run average: each possible value weighted by its probability. A fair die has E[X] = (1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6) / 6 = 3.5, a value it can never actually show. The variance is the expected squared distance from that mean, and the standard deviation is its square root, back in the original units.

rolls = rng.integers(1, 7, size=100_000)    # 1..6; the upper bound is excluded
rolls.mean(), rolls.var()                    # about 3.5 and 2.92 (exactly 35/12 = 2.917 in theory)

Two properties are used constantly. Expectations add: E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y], always. Variances add only when the variables are independent: Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y). That second rule is why averaging helps. The mean of n independent measurements has variance σ²/n, so its spread shrinks like 1/√n, the fact behind confidence intervals in Prerequisite 7 and behind why a random forest of many trees is steadier than one (Chapter 18).

Expectation is also how ML defines what it is optimising. The "true" error of a model is its expected loss over all the data it will ever see. You cannot compute it, because you do not have all that data, so you estimate it by an average over a test set. Every metric in this course is such an estimate.

Use case: sizing a fraud-review queue

A card issuer processes 2 million transactions a day, and about 0.1% are fraudulent: 2,000. A new model catches 90% of fraud and flags 1% of legitimate transactions. Before launch, someone has to staff the review team, and Bayes' rule in counts gives the answer in a minute. True alerts: 90% of 2,000 is 1,800. False alerts: 1% of 1,998,000 is 19,980. The queue is about 21,800 alerts a day, of which about 8% are fraud. If each review takes three minutes, that is over a thousand analyst-hours a day, mostly spent on legitimate customers whose cards are being blocked. The useful conversation that follows is not about catching more fraud; it is about the false-positive rate, which Chapter 46 sets by working backwards from how many alerts the team can actually review.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Confusing P(A | B) with P(B | A). "Most fraud happens at night" says nothing about how much night-time activity is fraud.
  • Ignoring the base rate, and reading a detector's sensitivity as the chance that a flag is real.
  • Assuming independence that does not hold, such as treating repeated purchases by one customer as independent examples.
  • Adding variances of correlated quantities as if they were independent.
  • Forgetting that rng.integers(1, 6) excludes 6. The upper bound is exclusive.
  • Trusting intuition over a five-line simulation. When a probability argument feels slippery, simulate it.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: A test for a rare condition is 99% accurate. If someone tests positive, are they 99% likely to have it?

How a strong answer sounds
Not necessarily, and usually not. It depends on how rare the condition is. Say 1 in 1,000 people have it, the test catches 99% of them, and it wrongly flags 1% of healthy people. In 100,000 people, 100 have the condition and about 99 test positive; 99,900 do not, and about 999 of them test positive anyway. So of roughly 1,100 positives, only 99 are real, about 9%. The test is accurate, but there are so many more healthy people that their small false-positive rate produces most of the positives. That is Bayes' rule, and thinking in counts rather than percentages makes it obvious.

Deeper: A fraud model was evaluated on a balanced dataset, half fraud and half legitimate, and showed 95% precision. What precision should the business expect in production, where fraud is 0.2% of transactions?

How a strong answer sounds
Much lower, because precision depends on the base rate and a balanced test set hides that. Precision is P(fraud | flagged), which by Bayes is TPR × prevalence divided by TPR × prevalence plus FPR × (1 − prevalence). The TPR and FPR are properties of the model and the threshold, so I would recover them from the balanced evaluation and plug in the production prevalence. If precision was 95% at 50% prevalence, then TPR / FPR = 19. With, say, TPR = 0.9 and so FPR about 0.047, at 0.2% prevalence the precision is 0.9 × 0.002 divided by (0.0018 + 0.047 × 0.998), roughly 3.7%. So about one alert in 27 would be fraud. I would tell the business that, re-evaluate on data with the real class mix, and choose the threshold from the alert volume the review team can handle, since reducing the false-positive rate matters far more than raising recall here.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • A distribution says how likely each value is: Bernoulli for yes/no, binomial and Poisson for counts, normal for measurements.
  • P(A | B) is the share of B-cases in which A also happens; it is not P(B | A).
  • Bayes' rule turns a likelihood and a prior into a posterior; write it in counts to reason about it.
  • With a rare positive class, false alarms come from the huge negative pool, so even a good detector's flags can be mostly wrong.
  • Expectations always add; variances add when the variables are independent, which is why averages are steadier.
  • When in doubt, simulate a million draws and count.

Check your understanding

1% of cases are positive. A detector catches 99% of positives and flags 5% of negatives. Roughly what share of flagged cases are positive?

Per 10,000: 99 true flags and 495 false ones, so 99 / 594 ≈ 16.7%. The large negative pool supplies most of the flags.

A and B are independent, with P(A) = 0.3 and P(B) = 0.5. What is P(A and B)?

For independent events the joint probability is the product, 0.3 × 0.5 = 0.15. Without independence you would need more information.

Which distribution best describes the number of support tickets opened in an hour?

A count of independent events in a fixed interval, with some average rate, is the classic Poisson case.

What is the expected value of one roll of a fair six-sided die?

The average of 1 to 6 is 21 / 6 = 3.5, even though no single roll can show it. 2.92 is the variance.

X and Y are independent, each with variance 4. What is the variance of X + Y?

Variances of independent variables add: 4 + 4 = 8. The standard deviation is √8 ≈ 2.83, not 2 + 2.
