Visualising High Dimensions: t-SNE & UMAP

A scatter plot can show two dimensions, and most data has dozens or thousands. PCA gives the best flat view in the sense of variance, but when the structure is curved or the groups differ in ways no single plane captures, a flat view piles them on top of each other. t-SNE and UMAP take a different approach: they place points on a page so that points that were neighbours in the original space stay neighbours, and they bend and stretch everything else to make that possible.

The resulting pictures are striking: well-separated islands of similar items that would be invisible in PCA. They are also among the most misread plots in data science. Cluster sizes, the gaps between clusters and sometimes the clusters themselves are products of the method's settings rather than facts about the data. This chapter runs t-SNE in the page so you can watch that happen.

Why linear maps fail

PCA projects onto a flat plane, keeping the directions of greatest variance. If ten groups of points each lie in their own region of a 64-dimensional space, no single plane may separate them all: on the handwritten digits, the 2-D PCA view lets a 5-nearest-neighbour classifier recover the digit only 60.3% of the time, because most digits overlap. The groups are separate; the projection just cannot show them. Non-linear methods give up preserving global geometry in exchange for preserving local neighbourhoods.

t-SNE and perplexity

t-SNE (t-distributed Stochastic Neighbour Embedding; Laurens van der Maaten and Geoffrey Hinton, 2008) turns distances into probabilities. In the original space, each point i spreads a Gaussian over its neighbours, so P(j | i) is high for close points and near zero for distant ones. In the 2-D map, similarities use a Student-t distribution with one degree of freedom, which has heavy tails. Points are then moved by gradient descent to minimise the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the two sets of probabilities, which mostly punishes placing true neighbours far apart. The heavy tails let moderately distant points sit very far apart in the map, which is what opens up clear gaps between groups.

The width of each point's Gaussian is set so that its neighbour distribution has a given perplexity, roughly the number of effective neighbours. Small perplexity focuses on very local structure and can break real groups into fragments, or invent clusters in noise; large perplexity takes in more of the global picture. Typical values are 5 to 50; scikit-learn's default is 30. t-SNE costs grow with n² in the exact form; the Barnes-Hut approximation used by default brings it to about n log n.

from sklearn.manifold import TSNE, trustworthiness

Z = TSNE(n_components=2, perplexity=30, init="pca", random_state=0).fit_transform(X)   # digits: 4.3 s
trustworthiness(X, Z, n_neighbors=10)   # 0.993, against 0.830 for a 2-D PCA projection
# no transform() for new points: t-SNE embeds only the rows it was given

Things to try.

  • Start with Clusters, t-SNE, perplexity 30. Three tidy islands of the same size, evenly spaced. In the 10-dimensional data, A is 5.2 times as spread out as B, and C is 4.0 times as far from A as B is; in the picture both ratios are 1.0. Press New random start: the layout turns and moves, the ratios stay near 1.0.
  • Switch to PCA. The ratios come back, 5.6 and 4.0, because a linear projection keeps global geometry. But B and A are squeezed together at the scale needed to show C, and trustworthiness drops from 0.957 to 0.901.
  • Raise the perplexity to 100. With a third of the data as each point's neighbourhood, some global structure returns: spread ratio 1.75, distance ratio 2.28. Still far from the truth.
  • Digits: compare PCA and t-SNE. In PCA's flat view, the 5 nearest neighbours of a point share its digit only 59.0% of the time (trustworthiness 0.810). t-SNE at perplexity 30 gives 93.0% and 0.977, and every perplexity from 5 to 100 lands between 93% and 94.3%: the islands are robust, their arrangement is not.
  • Noise, perplexity 2. Points that all came from one Gaussian break into pairs, triples and short filaments. At perplexity 30 the picture is closer to one even blob, and trustworthiness rises from 0.771 to 0.863.

UMAP

UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection; Leland McInnes, John Healy and James Melville, 2018) builds a weighted k-nearest-neighbour graph of the data, then lays the graph out in 2-D with an objective that both pulls neighbours together and pushes non-neighbours apart. In practice it is much faster than t-SNE on large data, scales to millions of points, keeps somewhat more of the global arrangement, and can embed new points with transform. Its main settings are n_neighbors, which plays the role of perplexity (local versus global), and min_dist, how tightly points may be packed. It lives in the separate umap-learn package. The cautions below apply to it just as much.

import umap                                 # pip install umap-learn

reducer = umap.UMAP(n_neighbors=15, min_dist=0.1, random_state=0)
Z = reducer.fit_transform(X_train)
Z_new = reducer.transform(X_new)              # unlike t-SNE, new points can be placed

What the plots do not mean

You seeIt does not necessarily meanWhy
A big, spread-out clusterThe group is more variedt-SNE adapts each point's scale to its local density, evening out sizes
Two clusters far apartThey are more different than closer onesDistances between clusters are barely constrained
Separate islandsReal, distinct groupsLow perplexity can split continuous data or noise into islands
A clear picture onceThe same picture every timeRandom starts and settings change the layout; check several runs
Good neighbour accuracyA good feature set for a modelThe map was fitted on every point, test points included

Use these maps to explore and form hypotheses: which items are near which, whether labelled groups are mixed, where outliers sit. Then confirm with methods on the original data: clustering, distances, models. Run several perplexities and seeds before believing a structure, colour by known variables, and never feed t-SNE coordinates to a model evaluated on the same points. On digits, a 5-nearest-neighbour classifier scored 97.6% in the t-SNE map and 96.3% in the original 64 dimensions; the map only seems better because it was computed with every test point in view.

Use case: maps of single cells, and their critics

Single-cell biology measures thousands of genes, or dozens of proteins, in each of hundreds of thousands of individual cells, and the first thing researchers want is a picture. In 2013 El-ad David Amir, Dana Pe'er and colleagues introduced viSNE, which applied t-SNE to mass cytometry data and showed bone marrow cells separating into islands that matched known cell types, with leukaemia samples revealing unusual populations. Within a few years a t-SNE plot, and after 2018 more often a UMAP plot, opened almost every single-cell paper. The maps are genuinely useful for spotting cell types and checking that samples mix as expected. They have also been over-read: as evidence that two cell types are closely related because their islands sit side by side, or that one population is more diverse because its island is larger. In 2023 Tara Chari and Lior Pachter, in a paper titled "The specious art of single-cell genomics", measured how badly such 2-D maps distort the original neighbourhoods and distances, and to make the point produced an embedding of the same data in the shape of an elephant. The lesson for any field is the one in this chapter: the islands are a starting point for questions, and the answers have to come from the original data.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Interpreting cluster sizes or the distances between clusters in a t-SNE or UMAP plot.
  • Trusting one run at one perplexity; try several settings and seeds.
  • Reporting clusters found only in the 2-D map without confirming them in the original space.
  • Using t-SNE coordinates as model features, especially when evaluated on the same points.
  • Running t-SNE on raw, unscaled features or thousands of noisy dimensions without PCA first.
  • Stopping too early, before the layout has settled.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is t-SNE, and what is it good for?

How a strong answer sounds
t-SNE is a way of drawing high-dimensional data on a flat page. It looks at which points are neighbours in the original data and then arranges points in two dimensions so that neighbours stay close together. That makes groups of similar items show up as separate islands, even when a simple projection like PCA would pile them on top of each other. It is good for exploring data: seeing whether labelled classes separate, spotting clusters or outliers, and checking for problems such as batches that do not mix. It is not good for measuring anything, because sizes and distances in the picture are distorted.

Deeper: A colleague shows a UMAP plot and says cluster A is twice as diverse as cluster B and much closer to cluster C than to D. How do you respond?

How a strong answer sounds
Neither conclusion follows from the plot. Both t-SNE and UMAP prioritise local neighbourhoods and adapt to local density, so the area a cluster covers does not reflect its spread in the original space, and the gaps between clusters are only loosely constrained, so relative distances between islands can change with settings and random seeds. I would rerun with a few values of n_neighbors and several seeds to see whether the arrangement is stable, then test the claims in the original feature space: measure each cluster's spread, for example the mean distance to its centroid, and compute distances between cluster centroids or the share of nearest neighbours that cross between clusters. If the claims hold up there, they can be reported with those numbers; the plot can illustrate them but not prove them.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Linear projections can hide groups that are well separated in high dimensions.
  • t-SNE matches neighbour probabilities between the data and a 2-D map; perplexity sets the neighbourhood size.
  • UMAP builds a neighbour graph and lays it out; it is faster, scales further and can embed new points.
  • Cluster sizes, gaps between clusters and even islands can be artefacts of settings.
  • Run several settings and seeds, and confirm findings in the original space.
  • Do not use embeddings fitted on all points as features for evaluating a model.

Check your understanding

What does t-SNE mainly try to preserve?

Its objective heavily penalises placing true neighbours far apart, and cares much less about distances between far-apart points.

In a t-SNE plot, cluster X covers four times the area of cluster Y. What can you conclude?

Each point's neighbourhood scale is set by perplexity, which evens out dense and sparse regions. Measure spread in the original space instead.

What does lowering the perplexity tend to do?

Perplexity is roughly the number of effective neighbours. With very few, small chance groupings get their own islands.

Which is a practical advantage of UMAP over t-SNE?

UMAP scales well and supports transform, but its plots carry the same interpretive cautions as t-SNE's.

Why is 5-NN accuracy measured on a t-SNE map of all the data not a fair estimate of a classifier's accuracy?

The embedding was shaped with the test points in view, so their neighbourhoods are not independent of training. It is leakage, as in Chapter 24.
