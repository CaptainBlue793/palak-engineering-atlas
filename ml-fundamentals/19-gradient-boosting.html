Gradient Boosting

A random forest grows many deep trees independently and averages them. Boosting does the opposite: it grows small trees one after another, and each new tree is trained to fix what the trees before it still get wrong. Start with a constant prediction, look at the errors, fit a small tree to those errors, add a fraction of it, and repeat a few hundred times.

Gradient-boosted trees, in the form of XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost and scikit-learn's histogram-based estimators, are the strongest general-purpose models for tabular data. This chapter builds the idea from residuals up, shows why a small learning rate with many trees beats a large one with few, and uses early stopping to choose the number of trees. Fitting residuals

Take squared-error regression. Start with the simplest model, F₀(x) = the mean of y. The residuals rᵢ = yᵢ − F₀(xᵢ) are what it gets wrong. Fit a small regression tree h₁ to predict those residuals from x, and update the model: F₁(x) = F₀(x) + η·h₁(x), where η is the learning rate, typically 0.05 to 0.1. Compute the new residuals and fit another tree to them. After M rounds the model is the starting constant plus a weighted sum of M small trees.

Each tree is weak, often only 3 to 6 levels deep, and on its own would be a poor model. The sequence corrects itself: regions the model still under-predicts have positive residuals, so the next tree pushes them up. Where a random forest reduces variance by averaging, boosting reduces bias step by step, which is why it uses shallow trees and why, unlike a forest, it can overfit as trees are added.

Why "gradient"

For squared error, L = ½(y − F)², the residual y − F is exactly the negative gradient of the loss with respect to the prediction F. So fitting a tree to residuals is gradient descent, carried out in function space: each tree is a step in the direction that most reduces the loss, and the learning rate is the step size. Jerome Friedman's 2001 paper made this the general recipe: for any differentiable loss, compute the negative gradient for each row (the "pseudo-residuals"), fit a tree to them, take a small step. For log loss in classification, the pseudo-residual is y − p, the label minus the predicted probability, and the trees add up on the log-odds scale. XGBoost and LightGBM also use the second derivative, a Newton step, to set each leaf's value.

Things to try.

  • Step through the first rounds. At round 0 the amber tree follows the shape of the curve, because the residuals do. With depth 2 and η = 0.1, validation MSE falls from 0.732 after 10 rounds to 0.386 after 30, and reaches its best, 0.313, at round 126.

  • Set η = 1.0 and depth 4. The best point comes at round 7 (0.324); by round 300 training MSE is 0.006 and validation has climbed to 0.392. Each full-size step memorises a little more noise.
  • Try stumps with η = 0.03. After 300 rounds validation is still 0.631 and falling: the steps are too small and the trees too weak to get there in time. Raise η to 0.3 and the stumps reach 0.312.
  • Compare the green dots. Across settings the best validation MSE lands between 0.306 and 0.324. What changes is when it happens: round 15 for depth 4 at η = 0.3, round 121 at η = 0.03. Early stopping finds that round for you.

The knobs that matter

The learning rate and the number of trees work together: halve η and you need roughly twice the trees to reach the same fit. In exchange, the best validation error is usually as good or slightly better, and the model overfits more slowly once past it, because many small steps average out the noise in each tree. Large rates such as 1.0 are clearly worse. The standard recipe is to fix a small learning rate, allow far more trees than you need, and let early stopping pick the number: hold out a validation set, track its loss after every round, and stop when it has not improved for, say, 50 rounds. On California housing, a learning rate of 1.0 reached its best test MAE, 0.346, after 124 trees and got worse after that (0.396 at 1,000), while 0.1 was still improving at 1,000 trees (0.313).

XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost

All three implement the same idea with engineering that makes it fast and robust. XGBoost (Tianqi Chen and Carlos Guestrin, 2016) added a regularised objective, second-order leaf values and sparsity-aware splits that learn where missing values should go. LightGBM (Microsoft, 2017) buckets each feature into histograms of at most 255 bins and grows trees leaf-wise, always splitting the leaf with the largest gain, which makes it very fast on large data. CatBoost (Yandex, 2018) uses ordered target statistics for categorical features (Chapter 15) and symmetric trees that are fast to evaluate. scikit-learn's HistGradientBoosting estimators follow LightGBM's histogram design and handle missing values and categorical features natively.

import xgboost as xgb, lightgbm as lgb
from catboost import CatBoostRegressor

m1 = xgb.XGBRegressor(n_estimators=5000, learning_rate=0.05, max_depth=6, subsample=0.8,
                      colsample_bytree=0.8, early_stopping_rounds=50)
m1.fit(X_tr, y_tr, eval_set=[(X_val, y_val)], verbose=False)

m2 = lgb.LGBMRegressor(n_estimators=5000, learning_rate=0.05, num_leaves=31)
m2.fit(X_tr, y_tr, eval_set=[(X_val, y_val)], callbacks=[lgb.early_stopping(50)])

Why it wins on tables

Tabular features are heterogeneous: prices, counts, categories and flags with different scales, skewed distributions and sharp thresholds. Trees handle all of these without scaling or transformation, ignore irrelevant features reasonably well, and model interactions automatically, while boosting drives the bias down step by step. In 2022 Léo Grinsztajn and colleagues benchmarked tree ensembles against deep learning on 45 tabular datasets and found tree-based models, especially gradient boosting, still ahead on medium-sized data. Deep learning wins on images, text and audio, where the structure in the raw input is what matters.

🏆
Use case: the library that took over Kaggle

XGBoost began in 2014 as a research project by Tianqi Chen at the University of Washington and became known after it was used in the winning solution of the Higgs Boson Machine Learning Challenge, in which competitors classified simulated particle-collision events from the ATLAS experiment at CERN. In their 2016 paper, Chen and Carlos Guestrin counted the 29 winning solutions described on Kaggle's blog during 2015: 17 of them used XGBoost, 8 of those on its own, the rest in ensembles, often with neural networks. In the KDD Cup 2015, every team in the top 10 used it. The reasons were practical: on tables of mixed features it was more accurate than other methods with little feature preparation, handled missing values on its own, and was fast enough to try hundreds of settings. LightGBM and CatBoost followed, and the pattern has held: for tabular problems, gradient-boosted trees are where serious attempts start.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • A large learning rate with no early stopping, so the model memorises noise.
  • Early stopping on the test set, which leaks it into model selection; use a separate validation split.
  • Deep trees, which make each round overfit; boosting wants weak learners.
  • Tuning everything at once before fixing the learning rate and letting early stopping choose the rounds.
  • Expecting extrapolation: the sum of trees is still flat outside the training range.
  • Comparing to a weak baseline only, instead of a random forest and a regularised linear model.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is the difference between bagging and boosting?

How a strong answer sounds
Bagging, as in a random forest, trains many deep trees independently on resampled data and averages them; each tree has low bias and high variance, and averaging reduces the variance. Boosting trains small trees one after another, each fitted to the errors the current model still makes, and adds them up with a small learning rate; each tree has high bias, and the sequence reduces bias step by step. Practically, bagging parallelises and barely overfits as you add trees, while boosting is sequential, can overfit with too many rounds, so you use early stopping, and is usually the more accurate of the two on tabular data once tuned.

Deeper: Why is it called gradient boosting, and what changes when you switch from regression to classification?

How a strong answer sounds
Each round fits a tree to the negative gradient of the loss with respect to the current predictions, so the model takes gradient-descent steps in the space of functions, with the learning rate as the step size. For squared error the negative gradient is just the residual y minus F, which is why the simple picture is fitting residuals. For binary classification with log loss, the model's output is on the log-odds scale, the pseudo-residual for each row is y minus the predicted probability, and libraries like XGBoost and LightGBM use the second derivative, p times one minus p, to compute each leaf's value as a Newton step. The structure of the algorithm stays the same; only the loss, its derivatives and the final link to probabilities change.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Boosting adds small trees in sequence, each fitted to what the model still gets wrong.
  • Residuals are the negative gradient of squared error, so boosting is gradient descent in function space, for any differentiable loss.
  • Boosting reduces bias and can overfit as trees are added; forests reduce variance and do not.
  • Fix a small learning rate, allow many trees, and let early stopping on a validation set choose the number.
  • XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost and HistGradientBoosting share the idea and differ in speed, categorical handling and tree growth.
  • On tabular data, gradient-boosted trees are the model to beat.

Check your understanding

In gradient boosting with squared error, what is each new tree trained to predict?

Each tree fits y − F(x), the part the model has not yet explained, which for squared error is the negative gradient of the loss.

You lower the learning rate from 0.3 to 0.03. What should you expect?

Smaller steps need more of them. The model can still overfit eventually, but more slowly, and the best validation error is usually as good or slightly better.

Why do boosted models usually use shallow trees while random forests use deep ones?

Boosting builds accuracy round by round, so each tree only needs to capture a little; deep trees would make each round overfit. Averaging needs low-bias trees to start with.

How should the number of boosting rounds be chosen?

Validation loss falls and then rises as the model starts fitting noise. Stopping at its minimum on a separate validation split keeps the test set honest.

What is distinctive about LightGBM's tree growth?

Leaf-wise growth with histogram-binned features is what makes LightGBM fast; symmetric trees are CatBoost's design.
