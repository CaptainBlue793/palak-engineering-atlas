Loss Functions

A loss function is the number training makes small. It decides what "wrong" means: whether a miss of 40 is four times as bad as a miss of 10, or sixteen times; whether predicting too high costs the same as predicting too low; whether a confident wrong answer is worse than a hesitant one. Change the loss and the same algorithm, on the same data, learns a different model.

This chapter covers the losses you will meet throughout the course, for regression and for classification, and the one question to ask of each: which prediction does it reward? The simulator answers that directly. Give it a set of delivery times, and it shows that one loss is minimised by the mean, another by the median, and another by whatever percentile your business needs.

  • Explain why squared error is sensitive to outliers and absolute error is not
  • Choose a quantile loss when over- and under-prediction cost different amounts
  • Explain log loss and hinge loss for classification, and why confident mistakes cost so much
  • Distinguish the loss a model trains on from the metric you report

Regression losses

For one prediction ŷ of a true value y, each regression loss is a function of the error e = y − ŷ. The model's training loss is the average over all training examples.

e = y_true - y_pred
mse   = np.mean(e ** 2)
mae   = np.mean(np.abs(e))
huber = np.mean(np.where(np.abs(e) <= d, 0.5 * e**2, d * (np.abs(e) - 0.5 * d)))
pinball = np.mean(np.maximum(tau * e, (tau - 1) * e))     # tau = 0.9: under-predicting costs 9× more

The middle column is the useful way to think about them. Take a model with no features, so it can only predict one constant for everyone. The constant that minimises squared error is the mean of the targets; for absolute error it is the median; for the quantile loss at τ = 0.9 it is the 90th percentile. A model with features does the same thing conditionally: an MSE model predicts the average outcome for examples like this one, an MAE model the typical one, a quantile model a chosen percentile. Which of those you want is a business question, not a statistical one.

Things to try.

  • Ten deliveries. MSE is lowest at 31.0 minutes, the mean. MAE is equally low anywhere from 30 to 31, the middle two deliveries, which is why the median of an even-sized set is defined as their midpoint, 30.5. The quantile loss at τ = 0.8 is flat from 35 to 38, between the 8th and 9th deliveries.
  • Add the two-hour parcel. The MSE minimum jumps to 39.1 minutes, beyond 9 of the 11 deliveries. The MAE minimum moves to 31.0, one parcel's worth. Huber with δ = 5 lands in between.
  • Slide Huber's δ. Small δ behaves like MAE, large δ like MSE: δ is how big an error has to be before the loss stops treating it as normal.
  • Switch to classification and slide the margin to −3. The example is confidently wrong. Hinge loss charges 4, log loss 3.05, the 0-1 loss just 1. Then slide to +3: log loss still charges a little, hinge charges nothing once the margin passes 1.

Matching the loss to the cost of a mistake

Symmetric losses assume that over-predicting and under-predicting by the same amount are equally bad. Often they are not. A supermarket that orders too little bread loses the sale and annoys customers; one that orders too much throws some away. If running out costs four times as much as a wasted loaf, the order that minimises expected cost is the 80th percentile of demand, not the mean: τ = cost of under-prediction / (cost of under + cost of over) = 4 / (4 + 1). That is the classic newsvendor result, and it is exactly what training with the quantile loss at τ = 0.8 produces.

from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingRegressor

# under-stocking costs 4× over-stocking: predict the 80th percentile of demand
model = HistGradientBoostingRegressor(loss="quantile", quantile=0.8).fit(X_train, units_sold)

# a prediction interval: fit the 10th and 90th percentiles as two models
lo = HistGradientBoostingRegressor(loss="quantile", quantile=0.1).fit(X_train, y_train)
hi = HistGradientBoostingRegressor(loss="quantile", quantile=0.9).fit(X_train, y_train)

Classification losses

For a binary label written as y = +1 or −1 and a model score f(x), the margin y·f(x) is positive when the score has the right sign and large when it is confidently right. Classification losses are functions of the margin.

  • 0-1 loss: 1 for a wrong sign, 0 for a right one. It is what accuracy measures, but it is flat almost everywhere, so its gradient says nothing about which way to move. Nobody trains on it directly.
  • Log loss, also called cross-entropy: for a predicted probability p of the true class, −log p. As a function of the margin it is log(1 + e−y·f). It never reaches zero, so it keeps pushing correct predictions to become more confident, and it punishes confident mistakes severely: predicting 1% for something that happens costs −log(0.01) = 4.6, ten times more than predicting 63%. Logistic regression and nearly every neural classifier train on it (Chapter 7).
  • Hinge loss: max(0, 1 − y·f). Zero once an example is on the right side with a margin of at least 1, linear otherwise. Support vector machines use it (Chapter 20); it cares only about examples near or across the boundary, and does not produce probabilities.
from sklearn.metrics import log_loss

y_true = [1, 0, 1]
log_loss(y_true, [0.9, 0.2, 0.7])    # 0.228  (mean of -log 0.9, -log 0.8, -log 0.7)
log_loss(y_true, [0.9, 0.2, 0.01])   # 1.645  one confident mistake costs more than everything else combined

Training loss versus reported metric

The loss a model trains on is chosen for optimisation: smooth, cheap, differentiable. The metric you report is chosen for the decision: accuracy, recall at a fixed alert volume, error in pounds. They are often different, and that is fine. Logistic regression trains on log loss and is judged by precision and recall (Chapter 8); a forecaster trains on squared error and is reported in percentage error (Chapter 9). The mistake is not having two functions; it is forgetting that the model optimised the first one, and being surprised when it does not optimise the second.

🛒
Use case: forecasting percentiles in the M5 competition

The M5 forecasting competition, run in 2020 on ten years of Walmart sales data, had two tracks. The accuracy track asked for a single number per product per day. The uncertainty track asked for nine quantiles, from the 0.5th to the 99.5th percentile, and scored them with a weighted, scaled version of exactly the pinball loss on this page. That second track exists because a single forecast is not what a store needs. A replenishment system wants to know how much to stock so that it runs out only, say, 5% of the time, which is a high quantile of demand, and the mean forecast systematically undershoots it for products with lumpy, skewed sales. Many strong entries trained gradient-boosted models directly on the quantile loss for each percentile. Chapter 48 covers the competition in full.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Using MSE on data with large outliers, and getting predictions dragged towards rare extremes.
  • Predicting the mean when the business needs a percentile, as in stock levels or capacity planning.
  • Reporting MSE to stakeholders. Squared minutes or squared pounds mean nothing to anyone; report MAE or RMSE in the original units.
  • Predicting probabilities of exactly 0 or 1 with log loss, which turns one mistake into an infinite loss. Clip them.
  • Expecting a model trained on one loss to be best at a different metric.
  • Calling log loss "accuracy-like". It rewards calibrated confidence, not just the right side of 0.5.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: When would you use mean absolute error instead of mean squared error as a loss?

How a strong answer sounds
Squared error grows with the square of each miss, so a few large errors dominate it and the model leans towards outliers; the best constant prediction under MSE is the mean. Absolute error grows linearly, so every unit of error counts the same and outliers have limited pull; its best constant is the median. I would use MAE, or the Huber loss as a smooth compromise, when the data has heavy tails or occasional bad records that I do not want the model to chase, or when the typical case matters more than the average. I would use MSE when large errors really are disproportionately costly, or when I want predictions of the mean, for example because they will be summed up into totals.

Deeper: A warehouse says running out of an item costs three times as much as holding one unit too many. How should the demand model be trained, and how would you check it?

How a strong answer sounds
The order that minimises expected cost is the quantile of demand at τ = cost of under-stocking divided by the total, 3 / (3 + 1) = 0.75, so I would train with the pinball loss at τ = 0.75, for example gradient boosting with the quantile loss, rather than training for the mean and adding a safety margin by hand. To check it, the right metric is the business cost itself: on a time-based validation set, compute for each item and day three times the shortfall plus one times the excess, and compare the quantile model with the mean model and with simple rules like "last week's sales plus 20%". I would also check calibration of the quantile: on validation data, demand should be below the prediction about 75% of the time, overall and by product group, because a quantile model can be well calibrated on average and badly off for slow-moving items. If the costs differ by product, τ can differ too, so I might train a few quantiles and pick per product.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • The loss defines what training optimises: MSE predicts the mean, MAE the median, the quantile loss a percentile.
  • Squared error lets outliers dominate; absolute error and Huber limit their influence.
  • When over- and under-prediction cost differently, train on the quantile loss at τ = cost under / total cost.
  • Log loss rewards calibrated probabilities and punishes confident mistakes; hinge loss ignores examples beyond the margin.
  • The 0-1 loss is what accuracy measures, but it cannot be optimised directly.
  • The training loss and the reported metric can differ; choose each for its own job.

Check your understanding

A model with no features must predict one constant. Which constant minimises mean squared error?

The mean minimises the sum of squared deviations. The median minimises the sum of absolute deviations.

You add one extreme outlier to the training data. Which loss's best constant prediction moves the most?

Squared error's minimum is the mean, which moves in proportion to the outlier's size. The other three move by at most one position in the sorted data.

Running out of stock costs four times as much as one unit of excess. Which quantile of demand should the model predict?

τ = cost of under-prediction / total = 4 / (4 + 1) = 0.8: stock enough to cover demand 80% of the time.

A classifier gives probability 0.01 to an event that then happens. What is its log loss for that example?

Log loss is −log(p) for the true class: −ln(0.01) ≈ 4.6. A confident mistake costs far more than a hesitant one.

What does the Huber loss do?

Inside ±δ it behaves like squared error, smooth near zero; beyond δ it grows linearly, so outliers have limited pull.
