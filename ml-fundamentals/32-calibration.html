Probability Calibration

A fraud model gives one transaction a score of 0.9 and another 0.2. If all you do is review the highest scores first, only the order matters. But often the number itself is used: to price a loan from the chance of default, to decide whether a 4% risk of readmission justifies a follow-up call, or to combine a model's output with costs as in Chapter 23. Then you need the scores to be calibrated: among all the cases given 0.2, about 20% should turn out positive.

Many models rank well and calibrate badly. Naive Bayes is overconfident, forests and boosted models are often too cautious near 0 and 1, SVMs output distances rather than probabilities, and any model trained on rebalanced data is shifted. This chapter shows how to see miscalibration with a reliability diagram, how to measure it, and how to fix it after training with Platt scaling or isotonic regression.

  • Distinguish ranking quality from calibration
  • Read a reliability diagram and compute the expected calibration error
  • Use the Brier score and log loss as proper scoring rules
  • Calibrate with Platt scaling and isotonic regression, on held-out data
  • Choose between them based on data size and the shape of the error

Calibrated versus well ranked

ROC AUC only asks whether positives tend to score higher than negatives. Any increasing transformation of the scores, squaring them, taking logs, squeezing them towards 0.5, leaves the order and so the AUC unchanged, while making the numbers mean something quite different. A model is calibrated if, for every value p, the share of positives among cases scored p is p. Calibration and ranking are separate properties: on a synthetic problem, Gaussian naive Bayes ranked better than logistic regression (AUC 0.865 against 0.805) but its probabilities were far less reliable.

Reliability diagrams and scores

A reliability diagram groups predictions into bins by score, then plots each bin's mean predicted probability against the share of positives actually observed. A calibrated model lies on the diagonal; an S-shaped curve below then above it means overconfidence, the reverse means underconfidence. The expected calibration error (ECE) summarises the gaps, weighted by how many predictions fall in each bin. The Brier score, the mean squared difference between predicted probability and outcome, and log loss are proper scoring rules: they are minimised by reporting true probabilities, so they reward both calibration and sharpness.

from sklearn.calibration import calibration_curve, CalibrationDisplay
from sklearn.metrics import brier_score_loss

frac_pos, mean_pred = calibration_curve(y_test, p_test, n_bins=10)   # points of the reliability diagram
brier_score_loss(y_test, p_test)
CalibrationDisplay.from_predictions(y_test, p_test)                # plots it

Platt scaling and isotonic regression

Calibration methods learn a mapping from the model's score to a probability, fitted on data the model was not trained on: a held-out calibration set, or cross-validation folds. Platt scaling (John Platt, 1999, for SVMs) fits a logistic regression with one input, the model's score or its log-odds: two parameters, a slope and an intercept. It fixes overall over- or underconfidence and shifts, needs little data, but can only bend the scores in that one sigmoid way. Isotonic regression fits the best non-decreasing step function from score to probability (pool-adjacent-violators). It can correct any monotone distortion, but with few calibration examples it overfits into a coarse mapping of a few big steps.

from sklearn.calibration import CalibratedClassifierCV
from sklearn.naive_bayes import GaussianNB

cal = CalibratedClassifierCV(GaussianNB(), method="isotonic", cv=5).fit(X_train, y_train)
p = cal.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1]
# GaussianNB on 20,000 test rows: Brier 0.192 raw, 0.167 Platt ("sigmoid"), 0.144 isotonic; AUC 0.865 throughout

Things to try.

  • Overconfident model, no calibration. The dashed curve is S-shaped around the diagonal: ECE 0.145, Brier 0.194. AUC is 0.7924, and it stays exactly that whatever you do below: calibration changes what the scores mean, not their order.
  • Platt scaling on 2,000 cases. ECE falls to 0.010 and Brier to 0.1687. Even 50 cases bring ECE to 0.050: two parameters need little data. All three models end at the same place, because each distortion is a simple change of the log-odds that a sigmoid can undo.
  • Isotonic regression on 50 cases. The mapping on the right is coarse, a few big steps pinned to 0 and 1 at the ends. ECE improves to 0.078, but log loss gets worse, 0.778 against 0.716 uncalibrated, because some steps sit at extreme values. AUC moves slightly, to 0.7911, as steps create ties.
  • Isotonic regression on 10,000 cases. ECE 0.009 and Brier 0.1687, as good as Platt. Isotonic needs data, and then it can fix shapes Platt cannot.
  • Try the model trained on balanced data. Its ranking is identical, AUC 0.7924, but its raw ECE is the worst of the three, 0.202: every probability is too high for the real 30% base rate.

Calibrate on data that reflects deployment, including its class balance; a model calibrated on rebalanced data will be miscalibrated in production. Recheck calibration over time, since it drifts when the base rate changes even if the ranking holds. And do not calibrate a model that is already well calibrated: for logistic regression above, isotonic calibration slightly lowered ECE but raised log loss (0.536 to 0.538).

🌦️
Use case: the forecasters who learned to say "30%"

Weather forecasting is where probability calibration was first taken seriously. In 1950 Glenn Brier, a statistician at the US Weather Bureau, proposed scoring probability forecasts by the mean squared difference between the forecast and what happened, the score that now carries his name, because it rewards honest probabilities rather than confident guesses. From 1965 the US National Weather Service issued precipitation forecasts as probabilities, and in the 1970s Allan Murphy and Robert Winkler studied how reliable those numbers were. They found the forecasters remarkably well calibrated: of the days given a 30% chance of rain, close to 30% had rain, and so on across the scale. That came from practice and feedback: forecasters saw their verification statistics regularly and adjusted. It is a useful benchmark for machine learning systems that produce probabilities. A score is only a probability if someone checks it against outcomes, keeps checking as conditions change, and corrects it when it drifts.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Treating model scores as probabilities without checking a reliability diagram.
  • Fitting the calibrator on the training data, which reflects the model's overfitting rather than correcting it.
  • Isotonic regression on a few hundred cases, giving a jagged, overfitted mapping.
  • Calibrating on rebalanced data, then deploying at the true base rate.
  • Judging calibration by AUC, which ignores it entirely.
  • Too few bins or too few cases per bin, so the diagram shows noise rather than miscalibration.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What does it mean for a model to be well calibrated?

How a strong answer sounds
It means its probabilities can be taken at face value. If you collect all the cases where the model said 70%, about 70% of them should really be positive; of those where it said 10%, about 10%. A model can be good at ranking, putting positives above negatives, and still be badly calibrated, for example by saying 99% when the real rate is 80%. Calibration matters whenever the number itself drives a decision, such as pricing a risk or comparing an expected cost.

Deeper: Your gradient-boosted model will be used to set insurance premiums from predicted claim probabilities. How do you make sure the probabilities are trustworthy?

How a strong answer sounds
I would hold out data that matches the deployment population, including the real claim rate, and check a reliability diagram, the expected calibration error, the Brier score and log loss, overall and for important segments such as age bands or regions, since a model can be calibrated on average but not within groups. If it is miscalibrated, I would fit a calibrator on a separate calibration set or with cross-validation: Platt scaling if the distortion is a simple over- or underconfidence or the data is limited, isotonic regression if I have many thousands of cases and the distortion is irregular, then confirm on an untouched test set that calibration improved without hurting ranking. In production I would monitor predicted against observed claim rates by segment over time and recalibrate when the base rate drifts.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Ranking quality and calibration are different; AUC ignores calibration.
  • Reliability diagrams, ECE, Brier score and log loss measure calibration.
  • Platt scaling fits a two-parameter sigmoid; isotonic regression fits any monotone step function.
  • Fit calibrators on data the model was not trained on, at the deployment base rate.
  • Isotonic needs more data; Platt is safer when calibration data is scarce.
  • Calibration drifts; monitor predicted against observed rates.

Check your understanding

A model has excellent ROC AUC. What does that tell you about its calibration?

Any increasing transformation of the scores keeps AUC the same while changing what the numbers mean.

In a reliability diagram, the bin with mean prediction 0.9 has only 70% positives. What does that indicate there?

The model claims 90% but the observed rate is 70%: it is more confident than it should be.

You have 300 labelled cases for calibration. Which method is the safer choice?

Two parameters can be estimated from a few hundred cases; an isotonic step function would overfit.

Why must a calibrator be fitted on data the model was not trained on?

The calibrator should learn how the model's scores relate to outcomes on unseen cases, which is how it will be used.

What is the Brier score?

It is a proper scoring rule: lowest in expectation when the predicted probabilities are the true ones.
