Data Leakage

Leakage is information in training or validation that will not be available, in the same form, when the model is used. It is the most common reason a model that looked excellent in development disappoints in production, and it is insidious because nothing fails: the code runs, the validation score is high, and every individual step looks reasonable. The model has simply learned to use a clue it will not have later.

Earlier chapters met leakage piece by piece: in splitting, in target encoding, in feature selection, in time series. This chapter collects them into one catalogue, shows four of them inflating the same churn model at once, and ends with a checklist for catching leaks before they reach production.

  • Recognise target leakage from features recorded after the outcome
  • Prevent train-test contamination from duplicates and shared entities
  • Keep every fitted preprocessing step inside the training data
  • Split by time and by group when production does
  • Use a checklist and simple detectors to find leaks

Target leakage

A feature that is recorded because of, or after, the outcome leaks the outcome. A "retention offer sent" flag is set when a customer calls to cancel; "account closed reason" is filled in only for customers who left; a lab test is ordered only once a doctor already suspects the disease; an insurance claim's "investigator assigned" field exists only for suspicious claims. In historical data these columns sit innocently next to the others, but at the moment a prediction is needed they do not yet exist. The test is not "is this feature predictive?" but "would I know this value at prediction time?" Write down the prediction moment, and check every feature's timestamp against it.

Train-test contamination

The same entity on both sides of a split lets the model recognise rather than predict. Duplicate rows, near-duplicates such as monthly snapshots of the same customer, several images of the same patient, or augmented copies of the same example all do it. Flexible models such as nearest neighbours, deep trees and neural networks exploit it most. Deduplicate before splitting, and split by group (customer, patient, device, session) whenever production will see new groups (GroupKFold, GroupShuffleSplit).

Leakage through preprocessing

Any step that learns from data, an imputer's median, a scaler's mean, a target encoding, a feature selection, a PCA, an oversampler, must be fitted on training data only, and refitted inside each cross-validation fold. Unsupervised steps usually leak little; steps that look at the target can leak enormously, as Chapter 24 showed with features selected from pure noise. A pipeline makes this automatic.

Leakage across time and groups

A random split of time-ordered data puts the future in training. Rolling statistics computed over windows that extend past the prediction moment, labels that were revised later, and features built from a database snapshot taken after the fact all leak across time. If production predicts next month from this month, validate by training on earlier months and testing on later ones; if the world changed between them, that validation will show it, and a random split will hide it.

from sklearn.model_selection import GroupShuffleSplit, TimeSeriesSplit

# every snapshot of a customer on the same side of the split
tr, te = next(GroupShuffleSplit(test_size=0.25, random_state=0).split(X, y, groups=customer_id))

# earlier months to train, later months to validate

Things to try.

  • Start with no leaks but a random split. Validation says 0.755, production delivers 0.720. Switch the split to months 17–20: validation drops to 0.714, close to production, because it now includes the price rise in month 18 that a random split blends into training.
  • Turn on the retention-offer flag (time split). Validation jumps to 0.867; production falls to 0.652, below the honest model's 0.720, because the flag is never set when the model runs. Detector 1 shows the flag alone scoring 0.82, above any real feature (the best, days since login, scores 0.71).
  • Turn on the postcode encoding instead. Validation 0.877, production 0.619. Postcodes have no real effect, yet the encoding alone scores 0.85, because each row's own label went into its value.
  • Keep the monthly snapshots, with a random split. 307 of the 702 validation rows belong to customers whose near-identical twin is in training, and validation rises from 0.755 to 0.768. With 15 neighbours one twin matters only a little; more flexible models, such as 1-NN or deep trees, exploit duplicates far more.
  • Switch all four on. Validation says 0.946, which would look like a breakthrough; production delivers 0.595.

Detection checklist

LeakSymptomCheck
Target leakageOne feature alone is nearly perfectSingle-feature AUC; timestamp of every feature vs prediction time
Duplicates / shared entitiesValidation far above a group split'sHash rows; count entities in both splits; group split
PreprocessingCV score drops when steps move into a pipelineFit everything inside the pipeline; permutation test on shuffled labels
TimeRandom-split score above time-split scoreBacktest; compare feature distributions across periods
IdentifiersIDs, row numbers or file names are predictiveDrop or test them; check how they were assigned

Two general detectors help. A model that is too good to be true usually is: compare it with what experts or the literature consider achievable. And adversarial validation trains a classifier to tell training rows from production or test rows; if it can, the two differ in ways your validation is not testing.

Use case: the patient ID that predicted cancer

The KDD Cup 2008, run with Siemens Medical Solutions, asked teams to detect breast cancer from features extracted from mammograms. The team from IBM Research led by Saharon Rosset, which won, found something the organisers had not intended: the patient ID number was strongly predictive of malignancy. The data had been assembled from different sources, such as different hospitals and screening programmes, whose cases had different shares of cancer, and IDs had been assigned in ranges by source. A model could do well partly by learning which ranges were high-risk, a clue with no medical meaning that would be useless, or misleading, on patients from a new hospital. The winners reported the leak openly, and in 2012 Shachar Kaufman, Saharon Rosset and Claudia Perlich used it as a central example in "Leakage in Data Mining: Formulation, Detection, and Avoidance", the paper that gave the field a shared vocabulary for the problem. Their advice is the essence of this chapter: decide what information is legitimately available at prediction time, and treat any feature that reflects how the data was collected, rather than the thing being predicted, as a suspect until proven otherwise.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Features recorded after the outcome, because they sit in the same table.
  • Random splits of time-ordered data or of data with repeated entities.
  • Preprocessing fitted before splitting: scalers, encoders, selectors, oversamplers.
  • Identifiers and metadata left in as features.
  • Celebrating a surprisingly high score instead of investigating it.
  • Tuning on the test set until it stops being a test.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is data leakage?

How a strong answer sounds
Leakage is when a model is trained or evaluated with information it will not have when it is actually used. The classic case is a feature that is only filled in after the outcome happens, such as a "cancellation reason" field in a churn model. Another is having the same customer, or a copy of the same row, in both training and test data, so the model recognises rather than predicts. The result is a model that looks excellent in testing and disappoints in production. The way to prevent it is to ask, for every feature and every step, whether it would be available in exactly that form at prediction time, and to split the data the way production will see it.

Deeper: A new model reaches 0.98 AUC where the previous best was 0.80. How would you investigate before celebrating?

How a strong answer sounds
I would assume a leak until shown otherwise. First, single-feature checks: fit or score each feature on its own and look for one that is nearly perfect, then trace how and when that field is populated relative to the prediction moment. Second, the split: check for duplicate or near-duplicate rows and for entities, customers, patients, devices, appearing on both sides, and rerun with a group split and, if the data is time-ordered, a time-based backtest. Third, the pipeline: make sure every fitted step, encoding, scaling, selection, resampling, runs inside the cross-validation folds, and run a permutation test with shuffled labels to see what the procedure scores on noise. Fourth, look for identifiers and collection artefacts among the important features. If the score survives all of that and a run on genuinely new data, it may be real, and I would still want domain experts to explain why it is possible.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Leakage is information available in development but not at prediction time.
  • Ask of every feature: would I know this value, in this form, when predicting?
  • Deduplicate and split by entity; split by time when production predicts the future.
  • Fit every learning step inside the training data, in a pipeline.
  • Too-good-to-be-true scores, single-feature AUCs and adversarial validation expose leaks.

Check your understanding

Which feature is target leakage in a model predicting whether a loan will default?

Collections happen because of default, after the prediction moment. In historical data it looks extremely predictive.

Several X-ray images per patient are split randomly into train and test. What is the risk?

Patient-specific features carry over. Split by patient so that test patients are new, as in production.

A model scores much higher with a random split than with a time-based split. What does that most likely indicate?

If production predicts the future, the time split is the honest estimate.

What is adversarial validation?

If the two sets are easy to tell apart, the features that separate them reveal drift or leakage your validation is not capturing.

Which preprocessing done before the train-test split is most dangerous?

Target encoding puts each test row's own label into its feature. The others learn nothing from the data.
