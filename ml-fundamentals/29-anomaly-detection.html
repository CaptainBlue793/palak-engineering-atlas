Anomaly Detection

A failing bearing, a compromised account, a sensor stuck at one value, a transaction unlike anything the customer has made before: the events worth catching are often the ones nobody has seen enough of to label. Anomaly detection looks for rows that do not fit the pattern of the rest, usually with few or no labelled examples of what "wrong" looks like.

That makes it a different problem from classification. There is no single right definition of "unusual": far from the centre, alone in empty space, in a sparse patch next to a dense crowd, or easy to separate from everything else. Each method encodes one of those ideas, and each misses the anomalies that fit the others. This chapter compares four methods on three kinds of anomaly, in the page.

Statistical baselines

Start simple. For a single feature, flag values more than 3 standard deviations from the mean, or, more robustly, outside the median ± 3 × MAD or beyond 1.5 × IQR from the quartiles. For several correlated features, the Mahalanobis distance measures how far a point is from the centre in units that account for the data's spread and correlation; a point can be unremarkable on each feature separately and still far out jointly. Fit the centre and covariance robustly (scikit-learn's EllipticEnvelope uses the minimum covariance determinant), otherwise the anomalies drag the estimates towards themselves. These baselines are fast, explainable and often enough; they assume one roughly elliptical cloud of normal data.

Isolation forest

An isolation forest (Fei Tony Liu, Kai Ming Ting and Zhi-Hua Zhou, 2008) turns the question around: instead of modelling normal data, it measures how easy each point is to isolate. Each tree takes a random subsample, typically 256 rows, and splits it recursively on a random feature at a random value between that feature's minimum and maximum. An unusual point tends to end up alone after very few splits; a point inside a crowd needs many. The anomaly score comes from the average path length over many trees, normalised so that scores near 1 mean anomalous and below 0.5 mean normal. It needs no distances, handles many features, and is fast: on 200,000 rows with 10 features, scikit-learn's IsolationForest fitted in 0.6 seconds.

from sklearn.ensemble import IsolationForest
from sklearn.neighbors import LocalOutlierFactor

iso = IsolationForest(n_estimators=200, max_samples=256, random_state=0).fit(X_train)
scores = -iso.score_samples(X_new)            # higher = more anomalous

lof = LocalOutlierFactor(n_neighbors=20, novelty=True).fit(X_train)
lof_scores = -lof.score_samples(X_new)        # novelty=True is required to score new rows

Local outlier factor

Distance-based methods flag points far from their nearest neighbours. That fails when normal data has regions of very different density: a point at a modest distance from a tight cluster is strange for that cluster, but the same distance is normal inside a loose one. The local outlier factor (Markus Breunig, Hans-Peter Kriegel, Raymond Ng and Jörg Sander, 2000) compares each point's local density, estimated from its k nearest neighbours, with the local densities of those neighbours. A score near 1 means "as dense as my neighbours"; well above 1 means "much sparser than my neighbours", a local outlier. The price is computing nearest neighbours, which gets slow on large data: on the same 200,000 rows, LocalOutlierFactor took 464 seconds.

One-class SVM

A one-class SVM (Bernhard Schölkopf and colleagues, 2001) uses the kernel machinery of Chapter 20 to draw a boundary around the bulk of the training data; points outside it are anomalies. The parameter ν is an upper bound on the share of training points treated as outliers and a lower bound on the share that become support vectors. It can fit complicated shapes with an RBF kernel, but is sensitive to gamma and scaling, scales poorly (17 seconds for just 20,000 rows here) and did worst of the four on the tests below.

Things to try.

  • Start with anomalies scattered far out. All four methods reach average precision 1.00, and flagging the top 4% (21 rows) catches all 20. When anomalies are simply far away, the simplest method is enough.
  • Switch to anomalies near a dense cluster. Mahalanobis scores 0.04, no better than random, and catches none even at 15%: the anomalies are close to the overall centre. k-NN distance reaches 0.14 and the isolation forest 0.32 (8 of 20 at 4%), because the loose cluster's edges look stranger in absolute terms. LOF reaches 1.00, catching all 20 at 4%.
  • Switch to a tight group. Now LOF scores 0.05 and k-NN distance 0.02, catching none even at 15%: each anomaly's neighbours are other anomalies, just as dense. Mahalanobis is back to 1.00, and the isolation forest reaches 0.92, catching 19 at 4%.
  • Lower the flag rate to 2%. Only 10 rows are flagged, so at most 10 anomalies can be caught however good the ranking. The threshold is an alert budget; average precision is what compares the rankings.

Evaluating with few labels

Detectors produce a ranking; deciding how many to flag is a separate, operational decision, usually set by how many alerts people can investigate. If you have even a few dozen confirmed anomalies, use them: measure precision at k (of the top k alerts, how many were real) and recall, and compare methods by average precision rather than accuracy. If you have none, label a sample of the top-scored rows and a random sample of the rest, plant synthetic anomalies of the kinds you fear, and check that scores are stable across retrains. Expect drift: what is normal changes, so retrain on recent data and watch the alert rate.

When labelled anomalies become plentiful, the problem has become classification, and a supervised model trained on them will usually beat any unsupervised detector, at least on the kinds of anomaly it has seen. Many production systems combine both: a supervised model for known patterns and an unsupervised detector for new ones.

Use case: the anomaly that was the discovery

In May 1985 Joseph Farman, Brian Gardiner and Jonathan Shanklin of the British Antarctic Survey reported in Nature that springtime ozone over their Halley station had fallen dramatically since the 1970s: the ozone hole. NASA's Nimbus-7 satellite had been measuring ozone over Antarctica since 1978. In an often-retold and somewhat simplified version of the story, the satellite data processing treated extremely low readings as probably erroneous and flagged them as suspect, and the significance of those readings was not recognised until the ground-based report appeared; NASA scientists then re-examined the satellite data and mapped the hole across the whole continent. Whatever the exact sequence, it is a lasting lesson for anomaly detection: a rule that flags values outside the expected range is only as good as what happens next. Flagged points should be reviewed, not silently discarded, because the most important anomaly in a dataset may be a real change in the world rather than a broken sensor.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Assuming one method fits all anomalies; each encodes a different idea of "unusual".
  • Not scaling features for distance- and density-based methods.
  • Setting contamination to a guess and treating the flagged share as a measurement.
  • Evaluating by accuracy, which a detector that flags nothing almost maximises.
  • Fitting LOF and scoring new data without novelty=True, or fitting on data that includes the test rows.
  • Discarding flagged points automatically instead of reviewing them.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How does an isolation forest find anomalies?

How a strong answer sounds
It builds many random trees. Each tree keeps splitting a sample of the data on a random feature at a random value, until points are on their own. A normal point sits in a crowd, so it takes many random splits to separate it from its neighbours; an unusual point sits off by itself, so a few random cuts are enough. The average number of splits needed to isolate a point, across all the trees, becomes its score: the fewer the splits, the more anomalous. It does not need to model what normal data looks like or compute distances, which makes it fast and a good default for tabular data.

Deeper: You need to detect unusual server behaviour from 30 metrics, with no labelled incidents. How do you build and evaluate a detector?

How a strong answer sounds
I would start by agreeing with the operations team what counts as unusual and how many alerts per day they can handle, since that sets the threshold. I would clean and scale the metrics, handle daily and weekly patterns, for example by modelling residuals from expected behaviour, and build a robust baseline, such as per-metric robust z-scores and a Mahalanobis distance, before trying an isolation forest and, if the data is small enough, LOF. Without labels, I would evaluate by planting realistic synthetic anomalies, replaying past periods with known incidents from postmortems, and having engineers label a sample of the top alerts to estimate precision at k. In production I would monitor the alert rate and score distribution for drift, retrain on recent data, and feed confirmed incidents back so that a supervised model can eventually handle the known patterns.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Anomaly detection ranks rows by how unusual they are, usually without labels.
  • Mahalanobis distance is a strong, explainable baseline for one elliptical cloud.
  • Isolation forests score by how few random splits isolate a point; fast and a good default.
  • LOF compares local densities, catching outliers next to dense regions, but it is slow at scale.
  • Each method misses some kinds of anomaly; test with the kinds you care about.
  • Thresholds are alert budgets; evaluate with precision at k and average precision.

Check your understanding

Why does an isolation forest give anomalies short path lengths?

Random cuts separate a point sitting apart from the rest after only a few splits; points in dense regions need many.

Which method is designed to catch a point slightly outside a very dense cluster, when another cluster nearby is much looser?

LOF compares a point's density with its neighbours' densities, so "sparse for its neighbourhood" stands out even if the absolute distance is modest.

Twenty anomalies form a tight little group away from normal data. Which method is most likely to miss them?

Each anomaly's 20 neighbours are mostly other anomalies, equally dense, so its local outlier factor is close to 1.

You have no labelled anomalies. Which is the most useful way to evaluate a detector?

Precision at k from reviewed alerts, plus recall on planted cases, gives real evidence. Contamination is only a setting.

What does contamination (or the flagged share) control in most scikit-learn detectors?

It sets the cut-off on the score; the ranking is unchanged. It is a business choice, not an estimate of the true rate.
