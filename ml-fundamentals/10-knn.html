k-Nearest Neighbours

The simplest learning algorithm there is: to classify a new example, find the k training examples most similar to it and take a vote. No training step, no weights, no assumptions about the shape of the boundary. A customer whose ten most similar past customers mostly churned is probably going to churn.

Its simplicity makes k-nearest neighbours the clearest place to learn three ideas that every later model depends on. "Most similar" is a distance, and distances are wrecked by features on different scales. The choice of k trades a jagged boundary against a blurry one, a first look at bias and variance. And in many dimensions every point is far from every other, so "nearest" stops meaning anything. The simulator shows all three.

Predicting from neighbours

To predict for a query point: compute its distance to every training example, take the k closest, and return the majority class (classification) or the average target (regression). Optionally weight each neighbour by the inverse of its distance, so the closest count most. That is the whole algorithm.

from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline

knn = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=15, weights="distance"))
knn.fit(X_train, y_train)                 # "fitting" just stores the training data (and builds a search index)
knn.predict_proba(X_new)                  # the share of neighbour votes for each class

Because nothing is learned in advance, kNN is called a lazy learner. Training is instant; prediction does all the work. That is the opposite of most models, and it matters in production.

Distance is a modelling choice

DistanceFormulaBehaviourTypical use
Euclidean√Σ(aᵢ − bᵢ)²Straight-line; large differences in one feature dominateContinuous, scaled features: the default
ManhattanΣ|aᵢ − bᵢ|Grid distance; less dominated by one big differenceMany features, some noisy
Cosine1 − a·b / (‖a‖‖b‖)Angle only; ignores lengthText, embeddings, usage profiles
HammingNumber of positions that differFor binary or categorical codesOne-hot or bit-vector features

Whatever the formula, it adds up differences across features, so it silently assumes a unit of one feature is as important as a unit of another. Monthly spend in pounds ranges over thousands; visits per month over tens. Unscaled, a difference of £50 in spend outweighs a difference of 20 visits, and the model effectively ignores visits. Standardising each feature to unit variance (Chapter 14) puts them on equal footing; choosing which features to include at all is the real modelling decision.

Choosing k

With k = 1, every training point gets its own little territory: the boundary weaves around each example, including the mislabelled ones, and training accuracy is perfect while test accuracy suffers. As k grows, votes average over more neighbours, the boundary smooths, and noise stops mattering, until k is so large that every prediction is just the majority class nearby, or overall. Small k is high variance; large k is high bias (Chapter 11 makes this precise). Choose k by cross-validation, and prefer odd k for two classes to avoid ties.

Things to try.

  • Raw features, k = 7. 59.7% test accuracy, barely better than guessing. The query's seven neighbours all spend within about £18 of it, but their visits run from 2 to 22 a month: the regions are vertical stripes because visits hardly enter the distance.
  • Standardise. 91.0% at the same k. Nothing about the algorithm changed, only the units.
  • Standardised, k = 1. Training accuracy 100%, test 85.0%: islands around every mislabelled customer. k = 3 to 15 sits around 90%; k = 101 falls to 85.3% as the wave in the boundary is averaged away.
  • Raw features, k = 1 and then 15. 73.3%, then 56.7%. With the wrong scale, more neighbours only means averaging over more of a vertical stripe.
  • Add irrelevant features. With 60 random features beside the two real ones, accuracy falls from 90.3% to 65.7%, and the nearest neighbour is 66% of the way to the farthest, against 3% with two features.

The curse of dimensionality

Distances behave strangely in many dimensions. Scatter points uniformly in a cube and, as the number of dimensions grows, the distance to a point's nearest neighbour approaches the distance to its farthest: everything is roughly equally far from everything. Any feature that does not help adds its own random contribution to every distance, and enough of them drown the features that do. To keep the same density of neighbours you would need exponentially more data per added dimension. The practical consequences: kNN works best with a modest number of relevant, well-scaled features; feature selection (Chapter 34) and dimensionality reduction (Chapter 27) often help it a lot.

The cost of predicting

A brute-force query computes the distance to every training example: n × d work per prediction. With a million training rows that is a million distance computations for each request. Tree indexes (KD-trees, ball trees, which scikit-learn picks automatically with algorithm="auto") prune most of that in low dimensions, but lose their advantage as dimensions grow. At large scale, systems switch to approximate nearest-neighbour indexes, which trade a little accuracy for orders of magnitude in speed; the ML & AI Systems course covers them in its chapter on vector search.

🎧
Use case: "customers like you" without a model

Neighbour methods are the backbone of a lot of recommendation and lookup systems, because "find the most similar items" is often exactly the product. A music service can represent each listener by how much they play each genre, normalise those vectors, and recommend what a listener's nearest neighbours played recently (Chapter 31). A support tool can find the five past tickets most similar to a new one and show how they were resolved. A fraud team can retrieve the transactions most similar to a confirmed fraud. Each is a kNN query, and each depends on the same two decisions as this chapter: how the items are represented, which defines what "similar" means, and how the search is made fast enough to answer while the user waits. At the scale of millions of items, that second decision is why vector indexes exist.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Not scaling features, so the one with the largest units decides every neighbour.
  • Choosing k by test accuracy, or leaving it at a default; cross-validate it.
  • k = 1 on noisy data, which memorises every mislabelled example.
  • Throwing every available feature in, including irrelevant ones that dilute every distance.
  • Using kNN where predictions must be fast on a huge training set, without an index.
  • Scaling with statistics from the whole dataset instead of the training split; put the scaler in a pipeline.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How does k-nearest neighbours work, and how do you choose k?

How a strong answer sounds
To predict for a new example, kNN finds the k training examples closest to it under some distance, usually Euclidean on standardised features, and takes a majority vote for classification or an average for regression, optionally weighting closer neighbours more. There is no training beyond storing the data. A small k follows the training data closely, including its noise, giving a jagged boundary and high variance; a large k averages over many neighbours, giving a smooth boundary and eventually underfitting. I would choose k by cross-validation over a range of values, use an odd k for binary problems, and make sure features are scaled first, because otherwise the distance is dominated by whichever feature has the largest units.

Deeper: kNN works well on 5 features, but accuracy drops when you add 200 more. Why, and what would you do?

How a strong answer sounds
That is the curse of dimensionality. Each additional feature adds its own difference to every distance; if most of the new features are irrelevant to the label, their contributions are essentially noise, and summed over 200 of them they swamp the signal from the original five. In high dimensions distances also concentrate, so the nearest neighbour is barely nearer than the farthest, and "nearest" stops carrying information. I would reduce the dimension: select the features that matter, with domain knowledge, mutual information or a model's importances computed inside cross-validation, or project onto a few principal components or a learned embedding. I would also check scaling, since 200 binary or sparse features behave very differently from a handful of continuous ones, and consider a distance suited to them, such as cosine. If the features genuinely all matter, a model that learns feature weights, like gradient boosting, is usually a better fit than kNN.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • kNN predicts by voting or averaging over the k closest training examples; it stores data instead of learning weights.
  • Distance is a modelling choice, and every distance assumes features are on comparable scales: standardise them.
  • Small k gives jagged, high-variance boundaries; large k gives smooth, high-bias ones; choose k by cross-validation.
  • Irrelevant features dilute every distance; in high dimensions everything is roughly equally far away.
  • All the cost is at prediction time; large systems use tree or approximate nearest-neighbour indexes.

Check your understanding

Features are monthly spend in pounds (0 to 2,000) and visits per month (0 to 30). What happens if kNN uses them unscaled?

A £50 difference in spend outweighs a 20-visit difference in the Euclidean sum, so neighbours are chosen almost entirely by spend.

What does k = 1 tend to do on noisy training data?

Each point, including mislabelled ones, controls its own neighbourhood. Training accuracy is perfect and the boundary is jagged: high variance.

Where does kNN spend most of its computation?

Training only stores the data. Each prediction compares the query with the stored examples, which is why kNN needs indexes at scale.

You add 50 random, irrelevant features to a kNN model. What is the most likely effect?

kNN weights every feature equally in the distance. Irrelevant features add noise to every distance and make neighbours less meaningful.

Why is cosine distance often used for text or embedding vectors?

A long document and a short one on the same topic point the same way; cosine compares that direction, not the vectors' sizes.
