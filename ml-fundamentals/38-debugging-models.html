Debugging a Model That Doesn't Work

The model's score is disappointing. The tempting response is to try a bigger model, tune more hyperparameters or add features, and hope. That is slow and usually aimed at the wrong thing. Most models that disappoint do so because of the data: a unit mixed up in one source, a join that misaligned rows, labels that are partly wrong, or a segment of cases the training data barely covers. No amount of tuning fixes those.

This chapter is a systematic path from "the score is bad" to the cause. Check the data before the model; look for label noise; read the model's worst mistakes; break the score down by segment; and only then decide whether the model itself needs to change. The simulator plants three problems in a fault-detection model and gives you the tools to find each one.

  • Find label noise with out-of-fold predictions
  • Do error analysis on the highest-loss examples
  • Slice performance by segment to find hidden failures
  • Use learning curves to decide between more data, more capacity and stopping

Check the data first

Before any modelling question, answer data questions. Are the shapes, types and row counts what you expect? Are there unexpected nulls, constant columns, duplicate rows, impossible values? Do the distributions look the same across sources, time periods and the train and test splits? Do the labels have the expected base rate? Many bugs show up immediately when a feature is plotted by source: one hospital recording weight in pounds, one sensor reporting Fahrenheit, one region where a field is always zero because an upstream system changed. Also check the simplest baselines: if a model cannot beat predicting the majority class or the mean, something basic is wrong.

Label noise

Labels come from people and processes, and both make mistakes: annotators disagree or tire, a rule-based labelling step misfires, a status field lags reality. Some models degrade sharply with noisy labels: on handwritten digits, flipping 10% of training labels cut logistic regression's test accuracy from 97.2% to 88.7%, while a random forest held 96.9%. A practical detector uses out-of-fold predictions: train with cross-validation, and flag training rows where the model, which never saw that row, gives the recorded label a very low probability. Dropping rows below 0.2 removed 226 rows, 152 of which were among the 153 flipped ones, and restored the logistic model to 96.1%. Review flagged rows rather than deleting them blindly: some are real, informative exceptions. The cleanlab library implements a more careful version of this idea, known as confident learning.

from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_predict
import numpy as np

P = cross_val_predict(model, X, y, cv=5, method="predict_proba")    # each row scored by a model that never saw it
p_given = P[np.arange(len(y)), y]                                       # probability of the recorded label
suspects = np.argsort(p_given)[:100]                                    # review these first

Error analysis and slicing

Error analysis means reading the model's mistakes, starting with the most confident ones, and tagging them by apparent cause: wrong label, missing feature, ambiguous case, data bug, rare situation. A hundred tagged errors usually show which problem is worth fixing first. Slicing breaks the metric down by segment, such as source, region, device, customer type or time period, because an acceptable overall score can hide a segment where the model is useless. Small or new segments are the usual victims: they barely influence training, and they barely move the average.

Things to try.

  • Start with no fixes and read the tools. Overall AUC is 0.791. Plant C's mean temperature is 101.4, against about 60 at A and B: some of its readings are in Fahrenheit. And 81 of the 100 worst training errors come from annotator 3, who labelled only a third of the rows; 55 of those are mislabelled.
  • Turn on Fix 1, the unit conversion. Overall AUC rises to 0.833, and every plant improves, A from 0.753 to 0.812 and C from 0.814 to 0.872. A bug in one source was distorting the temperature effect for all of them.
  • Add Fix 3, the plant terms. Plant C reaches 0.904 and the overall AUC 0.855: with correct units, the model can now learn that vibration matters more at plant C. Try Fix 3 without Fix 1: it helps (0.809), but cannot unscramble the mixed units.
  • Add Fix 2, removing suspected mislabels. It removes about 94 rows, 67 of the 167 wrong labels, and moves AUC by less than 0.001. The error analysis found a real problem, but for this model the fix that matters is auditing annotator 3's labelling, not deleting rows.

When to stop tuning

Once the data is sound, a learning curve, training and validation scores against the amount of training data, tells you where to spend effort. If both scores are low and close together, the model underfits: add features or capacity. If training is high and validation much lower, but the gap narrows as data grows, more data will help. If validation has flattened and the gap is small, you are near what this data supports, and more tuning will buy little. Compare with a sensible ceiling, such as human agreement on the labels or the best published result, and stop when the remaining gap is smaller than the noise in your estimate (Chapter 36) or worth less than the time.

Use case: the orbiter lost to a unit

In September 1999 NASA's Mars Climate Orbiter approached Mars far lower than planned and was lost, after a journey of more than nine months. The investigation board found the root cause in data, not in the spacecraft. Software built by the contractor, Lockheed Martin, reported the effect of small thruster firings in pound-force seconds, while the navigation software at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory expected newton-seconds, a factor of about 4.45. Each firing's effect was underestimated, and the small errors accumulated over months of trajectory corrections. There had been signs: the navigation team noticed discrepancies between expected and observed trajectories, but the cause was not tracked down before arrival. The failure is a reminder of what this chapter asks first when a model misbehaves: check what each source actually sends, in what units and with what meaning, and investigate small, persistent discrepancies instead of tuning around them. A temperature column in Fahrenheit from one plant does to a model exactly what pound-force seconds did to the orbiter's navigation.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Tuning hyperparameters first, before checking the data and labels.
  • Looking only at the overall metric, missing a failing segment.
  • Deleting all "hard" examples as noise, including real exceptions the model should learn.
  • Evaluating on noisy labels and chasing noise; audit a validation sample.
  • Changing several things at once, so you cannot tell which helped.
  • Never stopping: polishing a model past the noise in its own evaluation.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Your model's accuracy is disappointing. What do you do first?

How a strong answer sounds
I check the data before the model. I look at a few raw rows end to end, compare feature distributions across sources and between training and test, check for missing values, duplicates and impossible values, and confirm the labels have the base rate I expect. Then I compare with simple baselines, such as always predicting the most common class. After that I look at the model's biggest mistakes and break the score down by segment, because those usually point to a specific cause, like a data bug, wrong labels or a group the model has barely seen. Only once the data looks right do I start changing the model itself.

Deeper: A model works well overall but badly for one new customer segment. How do you diagnose and fix it?

How a strong answer sounds
First, measure it properly: slice metrics with confidence intervals, since a small segment's score is noisy, ideally on audited labels. Then check whether the segment's data is different: feature distributions, missingness and units compared with other segments, which often reveals a pipeline difference such as a new source sending a field in different units or a field that is always empty. Next, check its labels, since new segments are often labelled by a different process. If the data is sound, the segment may simply behave differently and be under-represented, so I would add segment features and interactions, upweight or collect more of its data, or train a separate or fine-tuned model, and compare those options on held-out segment data. Finally, add the segment to the monitoring dashboard so that the next shift is caught early.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Most disappointing models have data problems; check the data before the model.
  • Plot features by source and split; many bugs are visible immediately.
  • Out-of-fold predictions flag likely mislabelled rows for review.
  • Read the worst mistakes and slice the metric; averages hide failing segments.
  • Use learning curves to choose between more data, more capacity and stopping.

Check your understanding

One source's temperature readings are around 140 while the others are around 60. What is the first thing to suspect?

60 °C is 140 °F. Distribution shifts by source are a classic sign of a data pipeline difference.

How can out-of-fold predictions help find mislabelled training rows?

In-sample predictions would fit the wrong labels; out-of-fold predictions judge each row with a model trained without it.

Overall AUC is 0.85, but one segment that is 5% of traffic scores 0.60. Why might the overall number not reveal it?

Slice the metric by segment, especially new, rare or high-stakes ones.

Training and validation scores are both low and close together. What does that suggest?

A small gap with low scores means the model cannot capture the signal, or the labels do not contain much.

What is the most useful first step of error analysis?

Counting causes, such as wrong labels, missing features or data bugs, shows which fix is worth the most.
