Exploratory Data Analysis

Before any model, look at the data. Exploratory data analysis, EDA, is the habit of summarising and plotting every column, and the most important columns against each other, before deciding anything. It is cheap, and it catches the problems that no amount of tuning will fix later: values in the wrong units, placeholders pretending to be numbers, duplicated rows, and columns that quietly contain the answer.

The simulator hands you a small housing dataset with five problems planted in it. Each one is invisible in a table of numbers and obvious in the right chart. Your job is to find all five by choosing which column to look at, and how.

  • Run a first pass on any table: shape, types, missing values, duplicates, summary statistics
  • Read histograms and box plots, and use a log scale for skewed data
  • Use scatter plots and group means to see how features relate to the target
  • Recognise placeholder values, unit errors, duplicates and target leakage from plots
  • Draw these plots with pandas and matplotlib

The first pass

Every new dataset gets the same five questions, and pandas answers each in one line. Do this before plotting, and write down anything surprising.

df = pd.read_csv("data/houses.csv")

df.shape                         # how many rows and columns? Is that what you expected?
df.dtypes                        # numbers stored as text? dates not parsed?
df.isna().sum()                 # missing values per column
df.duplicated().sum()            # exact duplicate rows
df.describe().T                  # count, mean, std, min, quartiles, max per numeric column

Read describe() column by column with two questions in mind. Are the minimum and maximum possible? A house with 0 square metres, a customer aged 212, a timestamp in 1970: all are placeholders or errors, not data. Is the mean far from the median? Then the column is skewed, or a few extreme values are dragging it, and a plot will tell you which.

One column at a time: histograms and box plots

A histogram splits a column's range into bins and counts the values in each, so you see the shape of the distribution: one hump or two, symmetric or skewed, a suspicious spike at one value. A box plot compresses the same column into its quartiles: the box spans the middle 50% (the interquartile range, IQR), the line inside is the median, the whiskers reach to the most extreme values within 1.5 × IQR of the box, and points beyond are drawn individually as potential outliers.

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import numpy as np

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(12, 3.5))
df["price"].hist(bins=40, ax=axes[0])                 # shape of one column
np.log10(df["price"]).hist(bins=40, ax=axes[1])       # the same on a log scale
df.boxplot(column="area_m2", ax=axes[2])               # quartiles and outliers
plt.tight_layout()

Prices, incomes, file sizes and latencies are usually right-skewed: most values are modest and a long tail stretches to the right. On a linear axis the histogram is a cliff with a smear. On a log scale it often becomes a tidy symmetric hump, which tells you the column varies by multiples rather than by fixed amounts, and that a model may do better predicting its logarithm (Chapter 14).

Two columns at a time: scatter plots and group means

A scatter plot of a feature against the target shows the relationship a model will have to learn: a straight trend, a curve, a threshold, or nothing. For a categorical feature, compare the target's mean or median per category instead. And one more check belongs here: a feature that predicts the target almost perfectly is a warning, not a gift. Real features are noisy. A relationship that looks like a function usually means the feature was computed from the target, or recorded after it was known.

df.plot.scatter(x="area_m2", y="price", alpha=0.4)        # feature vs target
df.groupby("bedrooms")["price"].median()                 # a category vs the target
df.corr(numeric_only=True)["price"].sort_values()          # linear correlation with the target

    🩻
    Use case: a patient ID that predicted cancer

    The 2008 KDD Cup asked teams to detect breast cancer from features extracted from medical images. The winning team, from IBM Research, later described (Rosset, Perlich and colleagues, 2010) one of the things that helped: the patient ID number was strongly predictive of the label. The IDs had been assigned in ranges that reflected how the data was assembled from different sources, and those sources had different proportions of malignant cases. A model given the ID could score well without looking at a single image, and would have been useless on new patients. A scatter plot of label against ID, the kind of chart this page asks you to make for every column, shows the pattern at once. The general lesson is the one behind the stamp-duty column in the simulator: when a column predicts the target suspiciously well, ask how that column came to exist before you celebrate.

    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Modelling before looking, then spending days tuning around a unit error.
    • Treating a placeholder like 0 or 9999 as a real value, so it drags means and distorts the model.
    • Reading a skewed column on a linear axis only, and missing that its structure lives on a log scale.
    • Celebrating a near-perfect feature instead of asking when its value becomes known.
    • Deleting outliers automatically. Some are errors; some are the cases the model most needs to see.
    • Checking duplicates after the train/test split, so the same row sits on both sides.
    🎤
    Interview drill

    Explain it simply: You are handed a new dataset for a prediction task. What do you do before training anything?

    How a strong answer sounds
    I make sure I understand what a row is and what the target means, then I look at the data. Shape and types first, then missing values, duplicates and summary statistics, checking that minimums and maximums are physically possible. Then a histogram of every numeric column, on a log scale where it is skewed, and counts for the categorical ones. Then each feature against the target, with scatter plots or group means. I am looking for placeholders, unit errors, duplicate rows, impossible values, class imbalance, and above all features that predict the target too well, which usually means leakage. I write down what I find and fix what I can at the source before splitting the data, because those fixes tend to matter more than which model I pick.

    Deeper: A feature in your churn dataset has a correlation of 0.95 with the label. What do you do?

    How a strong answer sounds
    I treat it as a suspected leak until proven otherwise, because real behavioural features almost never predict churn that cleanly. First I find out exactly how and when the column is produced: which system writes it, and at what time relative to the moment the prediction would be made. Typical culprits are fields updated as a consequence of churning, like an account status, a cancellation reason, a "last login" that stops changing, or a number of support tickets that includes the cancellation call. I plot it against the label and against time; a leak often looks like a step that lines up with the label. If the value is only known after the outcome, I drop it or rebuild it as of the prediction date, for example "tickets in the 30 days before the snapshot". If it turns out to be legitimate, I still check it does not encode the target through some ID or timing artefact, and I validate on a later time period, which is where leaked features fall apart.

    🧠 Key takeaways

    • First pass on every dataset: shape, dtypes, missing values, duplicates, describe().
    • Check that minimums and maximums are possible; spikes at 0, −1 or 9999 are usually placeholders.
    • Histograms show shape; use a log scale for prices, incomes and latencies.
    • A second hump at a round multiple of the first usually means mixed units.
    • Plot every feature against the target; a near-perfect relationship is a leak until proven otherwise.
    • Fix problems at the source and before splitting, and write down every rule you apply.

    Check your understanding

    A price column has a mean of £789k and a median of £230k. What does that suggest?

    A mean far above the median means a few large values are pulling it up: a right-skewed column. A log-scale histogram is the next view.

    A floor-area histogram shows a spike of values at exactly 0. What is the most likely explanation?

    A house cannot have zero floor area. A spike at a round impossible value is almost always a placeholder, and should become a missing value.

    Most areas fall between 50 and 200 m², but a second cluster sits around 600 to 2,000. What is the likely cause?

    One square metre is about 10.76 square feet, so a hump at roughly ten times the main one is the signature of mixed units.

    In a pandas or matplotlib box plot, how far do the whiskers reach by default?

    The default is 1.5 times the interquartile range beyond each quartile; points further out are drawn individually.

    A stamp-duty column predicts sale price almost perfectly. Why should it not be used to predict prices of houses not yet sold?

    Stamp duty is computed from the agreed price, so it does not exist until the price does. Using it is target leakage.
