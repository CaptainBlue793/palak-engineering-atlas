Classification Metrics

A classifier can be wrong in two different ways, and they almost never cost the same. Flagging a legitimate payment as fraud annoys a customer; missing a real fraud loses money. Accuracy adds the two together and divides by everything, which hides both, and on imbalanced data it can make a useless model look excellent.

This chapter replaces accuracy with the vocabulary every ML team uses: the confusion matrix, precision and recall, F1, and the two curves that summarise a model across every possible threshold, ROC and precision-recall. The simulator puts a model's scores for positives and negatives side by side, so you can move the threshold and watch every one of these numbers, and both curves, respond at once.

  • Read a ROC curve and interpret ROC-AUC as a ranking probability
  • Prefer the precision-recall curve when positives are rare, and say why
  • Choose a threshold from costs, a capacity limit or a required recall

The confusion matrix

At a given threshold, every prediction falls into one of four cells. Everything else in this chapter is arithmetic on these four counts.

Predicted positivePredicted negative
Actually positiveTrue positive (TP): fraud, flaggedFalse negative (FN): fraud, missed
Actually negativeFalse positive (FP): legitimate, flaggedTrue negative (TN): legitimate, left alone
 
   precision  =  TP / (TP + FP)        of everything flagged, how much was real?
   recall     =  TP / (TP + FN)        of everything real, how much was flagged?   (also: sensitivity, TPR)
   FPR        =  FP / (FP + TN)        of everything negative, how much was flagged?
   F1         =  2 · precision · recall / (precision + recall)     the harmonic mean
   accuracy   =  (TP + TN) / everything

Precision is the question the person reviewing alerts asks: is this one worth my time? Recall is the question the person who owns the losses asks: how much are we missing? They pull in opposite directions. Flag more and recall rises while precision usually falls; flag less and the reverse. F1 combines them with the harmonic mean, which is low if either one is low, so a model cannot score well on F1 by maximising one and ignoring the other. When one matters more, the F-beta score weights recall β times as much as precision.

from sklearn.metrics import confusion_matrix, classification_report

y_pred = (model.predict_proba(X_val)[:, 1] >= 0.5).astype(int)
confusion_matrix(y_val, y_pred)          # [[TN, FP], [FN, TP]]  rows are the truth, negatives first
print(classification_report(y_val, y_pred, digits=3))   # precision, recall, F1 per class

Curves across every threshold

A single threshold gives a single confusion matrix. To compare models before anyone has chosen a threshold, sweep it from 1 down to 0 and plot what happens.

  • The ROC curve plots recall (true-positive rate) against the false-positive rate. A perfect model goes straight up the left edge; a random one follows the diagonal. The area under it, ROC-AUC, has a clean meaning: the probability that a randomly chosen positive gets a higher score than a randomly chosen negative. 0.5 is a coin toss, 1.0 is a perfect ranking.
  • The precision-recall curve plots precision against recall. Its area, usually computed as average precision (AP), summarises how precise the model stays as you push recall up. A random model's precision equals the base rate, so for rare positives the baseline is near zero, not 0.5.

The difference matters when positives are rare. The false-positive rate divides by the number of negatives; when negatives outnumber positives a thousand to one, thousands of false alarms are still a small FPR, so the ROC curve looks excellent while the people reading the alerts drown. Precision divides by what was flagged, so the PR curve shows that drowning directly. The simulator makes the point: change the prevalence and the ROC curve does not move at all, while the PR curve collapses.

Scores come from a fixed-seed simulation: the model's score is the sigmoid of a normally distributed log-odds, centred at −1.3 for legitimate and +1.3 for fraud transactions, with standard deviation 1. That separation gives a ROC-AUC of about 0.97 whatever the prevalence. Counts are scaled to 2,000 transactions; curves are computed exactly from the sorted scores.

Things to try.

  • 50% fraud, threshold 0.5. Precision 90.7%, recall 90.0%. ROC-AUC 0.969 and average precision 0.970: with balanced classes the two curves tell the same story.
  • Slide to 0.2, then to 0.8. At 0.2 recall is 99.6% and precision 65.5%; at 0.8 precision is 99.7% and recall 45.3%. The dot runs along each curve; the curves themselves stay put.
  • Now 2% fraud, threshold 0.5. Recall is still 90% and the false-positive rate still 9.2%, but precision has fallen to 16.6%: five false alarms for every real fraud. ROC-AUC has not moved; average precision is down to 0.679. Accuracy reads 90.8%, below the 98% of a model that never flags anything.
  • 0.5% fraud. At 0.5, only 4.7% of flags are real. Raise the threshold to 0.8: precision 60%, recall 45%, and accuracy a meaningless 99.6%. Average precision is 0.519, which is the honest summary of how this model will feel to the people reading its alerts.

Choosing a threshold

The curves compare models; a deployed model still needs one threshold, chosen on validation data, never on the test set. Three common ways to choose it:

  • From costs: pick the threshold that minimises FP × cost of a false alarm + FN × cost of a miss. With calibrated probabilities the answer is simple: act when p > cost of FP / (cost of FP + cost of FN).
  • From capacity: if analysts can review 500 alerts a day, flag the 500 highest scores. This fixes the volume and lets precision fall where it may.
  • From a required recall: "we must catch 90% of fraud". Take the highest threshold whose recall on validation reaches 0.9, and report the precision that buys.
from sklearn.metrics import precision_recall_curve, roc_auc_score, average_precision_score

p = model.predict_proba(X_val)[:, 1]
roc_auc_score(y_val, p), average_precision_score(y_val, p)

prec, rec, thr = precision_recall_curve(y_val, p)
i = np.where(rec[:-1] >= 0.90)[0][-1]      # highest threshold that still gives 90% recall
threshold, precision_at_90 = thr[i], prec[i]

Use case: the alert nobody acted on

In late 2013 attackers stole the payment-card details of tens of millions of Target customers. Reporting by Bloomberg Businessweek in 2014 described how Target's malware-detection system, from the security firm FireEye, had raised alerts about the intrusion, and that the alerts were passed on but not acted on in time. Whatever the full story inside the company, the pattern is a familiar one in security operations: detection tools tuned for high recall produce a stream of alerts, most of them false, and analysts learn to treat each new one with suspicion. A detector's recall is wasted if its precision at the operating threshold is so low that real alerts are lost among false ones. That is why security teams track precision per alert type, cap alert volume to what can be investigated, and judge detectors on the precision-recall curve rather than on accuracy or ROC-AUC alone.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Reporting accuracy on imbalanced data, where predicting the majority class scores nearly as well.
  • Using the default 0.5 threshold without asking what the costs and the class balance imply.
  • Comparing models by ROC-AUC alone when positives are rare, where PR-AUC shows the difference that matters.
  • Choosing the threshold on the test set, which leaks it into the final estimate.
  • Quoting precision without recall, or the reverse: either alone can be made perfect by a useless threshold.
  • Reading confusion_matrix the wrong way round: rows are the truth, and the negative class comes first.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is the difference between precision and recall?

How a strong answer sounds
Precision is about the predictions: of everything the model flagged as positive, what fraction really was positive. Recall is about the actual positives: of all the real positives, what fraction did the model flag. A spam filter with high precision rarely sends a real email to spam; one with high recall lets little spam through. They trade off through the threshold: lowering it flags more, which raises recall and usually lowers precision. Which matters more depends on the cost of each mistake, so I usually report both, along with the threshold, or the full precision-recall curve.

Deeper: A fraud model has a ROC-AUC of 0.97, but the operations team says its alerts are useless. How can both be true, and what would you do?

How a strong answer sounds
ROC-AUC measures how well the model ranks fraud above legitimate transactions, and it is computed from the true- and false-positive rates, which do not depend on how rare fraud is. If fraud is half a percent of transactions, a false-positive rate of 3% still means around six false alarms for every real fraud flagged at a typical threshold, so precision is low even though the ranking is excellent. I would look at the precision-recall curve and average precision, and at precision at the operating threshold the team actually uses. Then I would set the threshold from their constraint: if they can review a fixed number of alerts per day, flag only the top-scoring ones and accept the recall that gives, which a strong ranking makes much better. Longer term, I would improve precision at the top of the ranking, with better features for the cases that are currently confused, and maybe a second-stage model for borderline scores, and I would measure progress with PR-AUC or precision at the review capacity rather than ROC-AUC.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • The confusion matrix has four counts; precision, recall, FPR, F1 and accuracy are all ratios of them.
  • Precision: are the flags real? Recall: are the positives caught? The threshold trades one for the other.
  • ROC-AUC is the chance a random positive outranks a random negative; it ignores prevalence.
  • With rare positives, use the precision-recall curve and average precision; ROC can look excellent while precision is poor.
  • Accuracy is misleading on imbalanced data.
  • Choose the threshold on validation data from costs, capacity or a recall target, and report precision and recall at it.

Check your understanding

A model flags 200 transactions; 50 are fraud. There were 80 frauds in total. What are precision and recall?

Precision = 50 / 200 = 0.25 of the flags are real. Recall = 50 / 80 = 0.625 of the frauds were caught.

You lower the decision threshold from 0.5 to 0.3. What usually happens?

More examples are flagged, so more positives are caught (recall up) and more negatives are swept in (precision usually down).

What does a ROC-AUC of 0.5 mean?

AUC is the probability a random positive scores above a random negative; 0.5 is a coin toss.

Fraud becomes ten times rarer, but the model's scores for each class stay the same. What happens to the curves?

ROC uses rates within each class, which do not change. Precision depends on how many negatives there are to produce false alarms, so the PR curve falls.

Analysts can investigate 300 alerts a day. Which way of choosing the threshold fits best?

A capacity limit fixes the volume. Flagging the top 300 uses the model's ranking where it matters most, and the threshold is chosen without the test set.
