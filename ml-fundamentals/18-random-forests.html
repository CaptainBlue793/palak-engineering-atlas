Random Forests & Bagging

A single deep decision tree overfits, and two trees grown on slightly different samples of the same data can look completely different. That instability turns out to be useful. Train hundreds of deep trees, each on a different random resample of the data and each restricted to a random subset of features at every split, and average their votes: their individual mistakes are largely independent, so they cancel, while the signal they share adds up.

That is a random forest, and with almost no tuning it is one of the most reliable models for tabular data. This chapter explains why averaging works, what the two kinds of randomness do, how the out-of-bag score gives you a free validation estimate, and why the feature importances it reports can mislead. Bagging: averaging away variance

Bagging, short for bootstrap aggregating, trains each model on a bootstrap sample: n rows drawn with replacement from the n training rows. Each sample contains about 63% of the distinct rows, some of them several times; the other 37% are left out. Train a deep tree on each sample, and average the predictions (or the class probabilities). Each tree has low bias and high variance; averaging B of them leaves the bias where it was and reduces the variance, by up to a factor of B if the trees' errors were independent.

They are not independent, because every tree sees mostly the same data and tends to pick the same strong features. The variance of an average of B trees, each with variance σ² and pairwise correlation ρ, is ρσ² + (1 − ρ)σ²/B. As B grows, the second term vanishes and the first remains: correlation is the floor. The random forest's contribution, from Leo Breiman in 2001, is to lower ρ.

Random feature subsets

At every split, a random forest considers only a random subset of the features, by default √p of p features for classification in scikit-learn (and all of them for regression, since version 1.1). A tree can no longer use the single strongest feature at every node; some trees build their structure from other features. The trees become less accurate individually and much less correlated with each other, and the average gets better. Extremely randomised trees (ExtraTreesClassifier) go further and choose thresholds at random too.

from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier

rf = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=500, max_features="sqrt", min_samples_leaf=1,
                            oob_score=True, n_jobs=-1, random_state=0).fit(X_train, y_train)
rf.oob_score_                 # accuracy on the rows each tree did not see: a free validation estimate
# breast-cancer test accuracy: one tree 0.916; forests of 10, 100 and 500 trees all 0.944

Things to try.

  • Start with one tree. A single full-depth tree scores 81.8% on new customers, and its map is a patchwork of small rectangles around noisy points. Slide to 25 trees (85.1%), 50 (85.7%) and 200 (86.4%): the gains come early and then flatten, and the accuracy never turns down.
  • Train every tree on all rows and let it try both features. Every tree is now the same tree, and the forest scores 79.2% whatever the number of trees: averaging copies of one model changes nothing.
  • Keep all rows but try 1 feature at random. Random feature choice alone makes the trees differ, and 100 trees reach 83.3%. Bootstrap samples add the second source of difference and take it to 85.7%.
  • Watch the out-of-bag line. It appears from 5 trees, once almost every row has been left out of at least one sample. At 100 trees it reads 86.7% against 85.7% on 2,000 fresh customers: close, and obtained without setting any data aside.
  • Open the feature importance view. Impurity importance gives the pure-noise column 53% of the credit and the plan tier only 11%. Shuffling them on validation data tells the truth: logins cost 11.8 accuracy points, the plan tier 2.7, the noise column nothing (it moves by 0.4 points).

Feature importance, and its trap

Forests report impurity-based importance (mean decrease in impurity, MDI): for each feature, the total impurity decrease from splits on it, averaged over trees. It is free, and biased. It is computed on the training data, and features with many distinct values offer many more possible splits, so a continuous column, even pure noise, can collect a lot of impurity decrease by fitting noise in deep trees. Low-cardinality features, binary flags and small categories, are systematically undervalued. Permutation importance avoids both problems: shuffle one feature's values in a validation set and measure how much the score drops (Chapter 33).

from sklearn.inspection import permutation_importance

mdi = rf.feature_importances_                                    # biased towards high-cardinality features
perm = permutation_importance(rf, X_val, y_val, n_repeats=20, random_state=0)
perm.importances_mean                                            # score drop when each feature is shuffled

Use case: the forest that wins without tuning

In 2014 Manuel Fernández-Delgado and colleagues published a study in the Journal of Machine Learning Research with a deliberately provocative title, "Do we Need Hundreds of Classifiers to Solve Real World Classification Problems?". They ran 179 classifiers from 17 families on 121 datasets from the UCI repository. Random forests came out as the best family overall, with SVMs close behind, and a forest reached on average around 94% of the best accuracy achieved by any classifier on each dataset. Later analyses argued about details of the method, and gradient boosting, which the study covered only lightly, often edges ahead when it is tuned. But the practical lesson has held up: for a new tabular problem, a random forest with default settings is one of the fastest routes to a strong, honest baseline, and a model that beats it has genuinely earned its complexity.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Too few trees, leaving predictions noisy; adding trees never overfits.
  • Trusting impurity importances, especially with a mix of continuous and binary features.
  • Using the out-of-bag score on grouped or time-ordered data, where bootstrap rows leak just as a random split would.
  • Expecting a forest to extrapolate, which it cannot do any more than a single tree.
  • Skipping a forest baseline before spending days on a complex model.
  • Large forests in latency-critical serving without measuring prediction time and memory.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How does a random forest work, and why is it better than a single tree?

How a strong answer sounds
A random forest trains many decision trees, each on a bootstrap sample of the training rows and each considering only a random subset of features at every split, and averages their predictions. A single deep tree has low bias but very high variance: it changes a lot with small changes in the data. Averaging many such trees keeps the low bias and cuts the variance, as long as their errors are not too correlated, and the two kinds of randomness are what decorrelate them. The result is much more accurate and stable than one tree, needs little tuning, and gives a free validation estimate from the out-of-bag rows each tree did not see.

Deeper: Your forest's feature importances put a customer ID-like numeric column near the top. What is going on?

How a strong answer sounds
Impurity-based importances are computed on the training data and are biased towards features with many distinct values, because those offer many more candidate splits, and deep trees use them to fit noise. An ID-like column is the extreme case: it can separate individual rows. So its high MDI may mean nothing, or it may mean leakage, if the ID was assigned in a way that correlates with the target, such as sequential IDs and a label that changed over time. I would compute permutation importance on a held-out set: if shuffling the ID does not hurt validation performance, it is just the MDI bias and I would drop the column; if it does hurt, I would investigate how IDs were assigned before trusting the model, because a model that relies on an ID cannot generalise to new customers.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Bagging averages models trained on bootstrap samples, cutting variance while keeping bias.
  • Correlation between trees limits what averaging can do; random feature subsets decorrelate them.
  • More trees never overfit; they only make predictions steadier.
  • The out-of-bag score is a free validation estimate, valid when rows are independent.
  • Impurity importance favours high-cardinality features; prefer permutation importance on held-out data.
  • A default random forest is a strong baseline for any tabular problem.

Check your understanding

Roughly what share of distinct training rows does one bootstrap sample of size n contain?

Each row is left out with probability (1 − 1/n)ⁿ ≈ e⁻¹ ≈ 0.37, so about 63% appear at least once; the rest are out-of-bag for that tree.

Why does a random forest consider only a random subset of features at each split?

If every tree could always use the strongest feature, they would look alike and their errors would be correlated. Averaging only removes the uncorrelated part.

What happens to a random forest's validation accuracy as you add more and more trees?

Each extra tree reduces the variance of the average a little less; the forest converges rather than overfitting.

What is the out-of-bag score?

Each row is out of about 37% of the bootstrap samples; scoring it with those trees only gives a validation-like estimate without a holdout.

A column of random noise ranks high in a forest's impurity-based importance. Why?

MDI is measured on training data and rewards features offering many split points. Permutation importance on held-out data shows the noise column is useless.
