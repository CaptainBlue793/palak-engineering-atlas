Naive Bayes & Generative Classifiers

Every classifier so far has learned the boundary between classes directly. Naive Bayes takes a different route: it learns what each class looks like, how often each word appears in spam and in normal mail, and then uses Bayes' rule to ask which class most plausibly produced the message in front of it.

To make that tractable it assumes that, within a class, every feature is independent of every other. That is obviously false: "free" and "prize" go together. Yet the classifier trains in one pass over the data, needs very little of it, and filtered spam for a generation. This chapter shows why it works, why its probabilities should not be trusted, and the one-line fix, Laplace smoothing, without which it breaks on the first unfamiliar word.

Generative versus discriminative

A discriminative model, such as logistic regression, learns P(y | x) directly: given these features, how likely is each class? A generative model learns P(x | y) and P(y): what features does each class tend to produce, and how common is each class? Bayes' rule then turns them around: P(y | x) ∝ P(y)·P(x | y). Generative models can do more, such as generate new examples or handle missing features naturally, but they have to model the whole distribution of x, which is hard when x has thousands of dimensions.

The naive assumption

Naive Bayes makes P(x | y) easy by assuming the features are conditionally independent given the class: P(x₁, …, xₚ | y) = P(x₁ | y)·P(x₂ | y)·…·P(xₚ | y). Each factor is estimated separately, by counting. In logs, the posterior log-odds become a sum: the prior log-odds plus one term per feature, log P(xⱼ | spam) − log P(xⱼ | ham). Every word casts an independent vote, which makes the model fast, easy to read, and, as you will see, overconfident.

The features decide which variant you use: MultinomialNB for word counts, BernoulliNB for present-or-absent flags, GaussianNB for continuous features, modelled as a normal distribution per class, and ComplementNB, a variant of multinomial that is often better on imbalanced text classes.

Multinomial naive Bayes and Laplace smoothing

For text, P(word | class) is estimated as the word's count in that class's messages divided by the total word count of the class. A word that never appeared in ham during training gets P(word | ham) = 0, and since the probabilities are multiplied, a single such word makes the whole message impossible as ham, whatever else it says. This is the zero-frequency problem. Laplace (additive) smoothing adds α to every count: P(w | c) = (count(w, c) + α) / (total(c) + α·|V|), where |V| is the vocabulary size. α = 1 is classic Laplace smoothing; smaller values often do better on large vocabularies.

from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import CountVectorizer
from sklearn.naive_bayes import MultinomialNB
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline

nb = make_pipeline(CountVectorizer(min_df=2, stop_words="english"), MultinomialNB(alpha=0.1))
nb.fit(train_texts, train_labels)       # 20 newsgroups, 11,314 posts: fitting the model takes 0.05 s
nb.score(test_texts, test_labels)      # 0.655 (alpha near 0: 0.601); TF-IDF + logistic regression: 0.692

Things to try.

  • Start with "claim your free prize now" at α = 1. P(spam) is 99.99%. The strongest vote is not "prize" but "your", which appears 9 times in the spam messages and never in the normal ones: with 40 training messages, small words carry accidental signal.
  • Try "are you free for lunch tomorrow". "free" votes for spam (+1.45), but "lunch", "tomorrow" and "you" outvote it, and P(spam) is 3.9%.
  • Slide α to 0. "claim" never appeared in a normal message, so its vote is +∞ and ham is ruled out whatever else the message says. For "lunch prize" the classifier now has +∞ and −∞ at once and cannot answer at all; at α = 0.1 it says 66%.
  • Watch α change a decision. "call me now about the prize" is 19% spam at α = 0.1, 55% at α = 1 and 60% at α = 3: heavier smoothing shrinks the strong ham votes from "the" and "me".
  • Repeat a word. "free" alone gives 81% spam; "free free free free free" gives 99.93%. Each copy counts as fresh evidence.
  • Open Correlated features. With one copy the model is calibrated: log loss 0.580. With 4 copies, accuracy is unchanged at 68.6%, but log loss is 0.922 and 51% of predictions claim over 95% certainty, right only 79% of the time. With 8 copies, 74% claim it and 73% are right.

Where it fails

The independence assumption does its damage in the probabilities more than in the decisions. When features are correlated, naive Bayes counts the same evidence several times, so its posteriors are pushed towards 0 and 1. The ranking of classes is often still right, which is why accuracy holds up, but the numbers are not probabilities you can act on. On 20 newsgroups, 73% of its test predictions claimed more than 99% confidence, and those were right only 77% of the time. If you need probabilities, calibrate them (Chapter 32).

It also struggles when interactions matter (a word that means spam only alongside another), when continuous features are far from the assumed normal shape, and once there is enough data for a discriminative model to learn the boundary directly. Andrew Ng and Michael Jordan showed in 2001 that naive Bayes approaches its best error with far fewer examples than logistic regression, but logistic regression usually reaches a lower error in the end. That makes naive Bayes a strong choice for small data and a fast baseline for text, and rarely the final model when data is plentiful.

Use case: the spam filters of the early 2000s

In 1998 Mehran Sahami, Susan Dumais, David Heckerman and Eric Horvitz at Microsoft Research described a naive Bayes classifier for junk e-mail, using word features plus hand-crafted ones such as particular phrases, heavy punctuation and the sender's domain. The idea spread widely after Paul Graham's essay "A Plan for Spam" in August 2002. Graham trained a close relative of naive Bayes on his own mail, combining the probabilities of the 15 most telling words in each message, and reported that fewer than 5 in every 1,000 spams got through, with no legitimate messages blocked. The appeal was that each user's filter learned from that user's own mail and updated with every message marked as spam, which is trivial for a model that is just a table of counts. Within a year Bayesian filters were in Mozilla's mail client, SpamAssassin and many others. Spammers responded with misspellings and blocks of innocent words to dilute the votes, a direct attack on the independence assumption, and modern filters combine many more signals, but word-count naive Bayes is where statistical spam filtering began.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • No smoothing, so one unseen word zeroes out a class.
  • Treating predict_proba as calibrated, when correlated features push it to extremes.
  • GaussianNB on heavily skewed features without transforming them.
  • Feeding TF-IDF to MultinomialNB and expecting count semantics; it often works, but tune α and compare with counts.
  • Ignoring the class prior on imbalanced data, or setting it from a balanced sample that does not match production.
  • Duplicated or near-duplicate features, each of which adds the same evidence again.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How does a naive Bayes spam filter decide whether a message is spam?

How a strong answer sounds
During training it counts how often each word appears in spam and in normal messages, which gives an estimate of how likely each word is under each class. For a new message, it starts from how common spam is overall and then, for every word, multiplies in how much more likely that word is in spam than in normal mail. The "naive" part is that it treats every word as independent evidence, even though words like "free" and "prize" tend to appear together. It adds a small count to every word, called smoothing, so that one word it never saw in a class does not make that class impossible. The class with the higher resulting probability wins.

Deeper: Naive Bayes often gets good accuracy but poor log loss. Why, and what would you do?

How a strong answer sounds
Because of the conditional independence assumption, correlated features each add their evidence separately, so the same signal is counted several times and the posterior log-odds are inflated. The ranking between classes, which is all accuracy needs, is often still right, but the probabilities are pushed towards 0 and 1, so when it is wrong it is confidently wrong, and log loss punishes that heavily. If I need probabilities I would calibrate on held-out data with Platt scaling or isotonic regression, possibly reduce redundant features, and compare against logistic regression, which learns weights jointly and so discounts redundant features. If ranking is all that matters, for example for a top-k filter, the miscalibration may not matter.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Generative models learn P(x | y) and P(y) and use Bayes' rule; discriminative models learn P(y | x) directly.
  • Naive Bayes assumes features are independent given the class, so each feature adds one log-ratio vote.
  • Training is counting: fast, incremental and effective with little data, especially for text.
  • Without smoothing, one unseen word makes a class impossible; additive smoothing fixes it.
  • Correlated features are double-counted, so its probabilities are overconfident; calibrate them before use.
  • With plenty of data, discriminative models usually overtake it.

Check your understanding

What does the "naive" assumption in naive Bayes state?

Conditional independence lets P(x | y) factor into a product of one term per feature, each estimated separately.

A test message contains a word that appeared in spam but never in ham during training. With no smoothing, what happens?

The product of probabilities includes a zero, ruling ham out regardless of every other word. Additive smoothing gives unseen word-class pairs a small nonzero probability.

You duplicate an informative feature three times and refit Gaussian naive Bayes. What typically happens?

Each copy adds the same log-ratio again, multiplying the log-odds; the sign, and so the decision, is unchanged.

Which is a generative classifier?

Gaussian naive Bayes models how each class generates its features, then applies Bayes' rule. The others model the decision directly.

According to Ng and Jordan's comparison, when is naive Bayes most likely to beat logistic regression?

Naive Bayes reaches its best error with fewer examples; logistic regression usually reaches a lower error once there is enough data.
