Dimensionality Reduction with PCA

Data often has more columns than it has independent ideas. The 64 pixels of a handwritten digit move together, a dozen sensor readings rise and fall with the same machine load, and survey answers cluster around a few attitudes. Principal component analysis (PCA) finds a small set of new axes, the directions in which the data varies most, and describes each row by its position along them.

Keeping the first few components compresses the data, removes some noise, speeds up later models and makes high-dimensional data possible to plot. It also throws information away, and the information it throws away is whatever varies least, which is not always what matters least. This chapter builds PCA from a single direction up, runs it in the page on real handwritten digits, and shows a case where it removes exactly the signal you needed.

Variance and directions

Centre the data by subtracting each column's mean. Pick a direction, a unit vector w, and project every point onto it: its coordinate along that direction is x·w. The first principal component is the direction along which those coordinates have the largest variance. Equivalently, it is the line that minimises the total squared distance from the points to their projections, the reconstruction error; the two are the same problem, because total variance is fixed and splits into the part along the line and the part perpendicular to it. The second component is the direction of largest remaining variance perpendicular to the first, and so on. The components are uncorrelated new features, ordered by how much variance they carry.

PCA via the SVD

The principal components are the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, and the variance along each is the matching eigenvalue. In practice PCA is computed with the singular value decomposition of the centred data matrix, X = U S Vᵀ, which is more numerically stable than forming the covariance matrix: the rows of Vᵀ are the components, the projected data is U S, and the variance of component j is sⱼ² / (n − 1). For very wide or very large data, randomised SVD computes just the first k components quickly, and scikit-learn chooses it automatically when that pays off.

from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
import numpy as np

X, y = load_digits(return_X_y=True)               # 1,797 images, 64 pixels each
pca = PCA().fit(X)
np.cumsum(pca.explained_variance_ratio_)            # 90% of the variance needs 21 components, 95% needs 29
Z = PCA(n_components=0.95).fit_transform(X)          # keep enough components for 95%: shape (1797, 29)
X_back = pca.inverse_transform(pca.transform(X))     # reconstruction from the kept components

Things to try.

  • Directions: start at 90°. The projections keep 29.8% of the variance and the red segments hold the other 70.2%. Turn to 30°: 91.7% kept, the most possible, because 30° is where PC1 lies. At 0° the split is 70.2% and 29.8%: kept and lost always add to 100%.
  • Digits, k = 1. One component keeps 14.8% of the variance, and the rebuilt 0 is a blur of the mean digit. At k = 10, 74.6% of the variance is kept and the 0 is clear, with 20.9% of its own variation still missing. 90% of the variance needs 20 components and 95% needs 28, close to the 21 and 29 on the full 1,797 images.
  • Compare digits at k = 10. The share of each image's variation left over is 15.3% for the 3, 24.8% for the 5, 24.7% for the 9 and 32.5% for the 7: a fixed k serves some shapes better than others.
  • Look at the eigendigits. The first components are patterns of light and dark that add strokes to, or remove them from, the mean image; they are not digits themselves.
  • When it hurts: choose PC1 only. It keeps 93.6% of the variance and gives 50.4% accuracy, chance. PC2 alone, 6.4% of the variance, gives 90.2%. A rule like "keep 90% of the variance" would have thrown away the whole signal.

How many components?

Plot the cumulative explained variance and keep enough components to reach a target, often 90% to 99%, or look for an elbow in the individual variances (a scree plot). When PCA feeds a model, treat k as a hyperparameter and choose it by cross-validation. On the full digits data with logistic regression, 2 components gave 53.5% accuracy, 10 gave 84.0%, 20 gave 89.9% and 40 gave 91.4%, against 92.0% with all 64 pixels: most of the accuracy comes back well before all the variance does.

Scaling and whitening

PCA chases variance, so it is at the mercy of units. On the wine dataset, unscaled, the first component explains 99.8% of the variance, and it is almost entirely one column, proline, whose values run into the hundreds while most others are below 10. After standardising, the first component explains 36.2% and blends many features. Standardise first unless all features share a unit and their raw variances are meaningful, as with pixel intensities.

Whitening (PCA(whiten=True)) divides each component by its standard deviation, so every kept component has unit variance. Some downstream methods, such as k-means or an RBF kernel, then treat all directions equally. It also amplifies low-variance components, which are often mostly noise, so use it deliberately.

When PCA hurts

PCA is unsupervised: it never looks at the labels. If the difference between classes lies along a direction of small variance, PCA ranks it last and a truncated projection drops it. The simulator's third view is an extreme case, but milder versions are common: a small but decisive measurement swamped by large, irrelevant ones, or faces whose pixel variance is dominated by lighting rather than identity. If you need a projection that preserves class differences, use a supervised method such as linear discriminant analysis, or put PCA in a pipeline and let cross-validation decide whether, and how much, it helps.

Use case: eigenfaces

In 1987 Lawrence Sirovich and Michael Kirby showed that photographs of faces, treated as long vectors of pixel values, could be rebuilt surprisingly well from a few dozen principal components. In 1991 Matthew Turk and Alex Pentland at the MIT Media Lab turned this into a face recognition system. They computed the principal components of a set of face images, which, displayed as images, look like ghostly faces and became known as eigenfaces; each new face was described by its coordinates along them, and recognised by finding the nearest known face in that small space. It was fast enough to run on the hardware of the time, and in their experiments on 16 people it identified faces correctly 96% of the time across lighting changes, 85% across changes in head orientation and 64% across changes in size. The weakness was the one this chapter warns about: PCA keeps whatever varies most, and across photographs that is often the lighting, not the person. In 1997 Peter Belhumeur, João Hespanha and David Kriegman's "Fisherfaces" replaced PCA with linear discriminant analysis, which looks for directions that separate people rather than directions of variance, and handled lighting much better.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Running PCA on unscaled features, so the column with the largest units becomes the first component.
  • Fitting PCA on the full dataset before splitting, which leaks test-set structure into training.
  • Assuming high-variance directions are the important ones for a supervised task.
  • Reading components as real-world factors without checking their loadings.
  • Expecting PCA to unfold curved structure; it only finds linear directions.
  • Choosing k by a fixed variance threshold when downstream accuracy is what matters.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What does PCA do?

How a strong answer sounds
PCA finds new axes for the data, ordered by how much the data varies along them. The first axis is the direction where the points are most spread out, the second is the most spread-out direction at right angles to the first, and so on. If you keep only the first few axes and describe each point by its position along them, you get a much shorter description that keeps most of the variation. That is useful for compressing data, plotting it in two dimensions, removing noise or speeding up models. The catch is that it only keeps what varies most, which is not necessarily what matters for your problem.

Deeper: You added PCA before a classifier and accuracy dropped. Why might that happen, and what would you do?

How a strong answer sounds
PCA is unsupervised, so it keeps the directions of largest variance without regard to the labels. If the information that separates classes lies in low-variance directions, truncating removes it. Other causes are unscaled features, so one large-unit column dominates the components, too few components, or nonlinear structure that a linear projection cannot preserve. I would check the scaling, then treat the number of components as a hyperparameter inside a pipeline with cross-validation, including the option of no PCA at all. If I need a reduced representation that keeps class information, I would try a supervised projection such as linear discriminant analysis, feature selection, or a model with built-in regularisation instead of PCA.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Principal components are orthogonal directions of maximum variance, equivalently of minimum reconstruction error.
  • Compute them with the SVD of the centred data; variances come from the singular values.
  • Choose k from cumulative explained variance, or better by cross-validating the downstream model.
  • Standardise first unless features share units; whitening equalises component variances.
  • PCA ignores labels: small-variance directions can carry the signal.
  • Fit PCA inside a pipeline, on training data only.

Check your understanding

What does the first principal component maximise?

It is the direction of largest variance, which is the same as the line with the smallest total squared distance from the points.

You run PCA on unscaled data where one column is in the thousands and the rest are below 10. What happens?

Variance scales with the square of the units, so the large column dominates. On the wine data, unscaled PC1 explains 99.8% of the variance and is almost all proline.

How are principal components usually computed in practice?

The right singular vectors are the components, and the squared singular values give their variances. Sorting original features by variance is not PCA: components are combinations of features.

Two classes differ only along a direction with very small variance. You keep the first principal component and train a classifier. What do you expect?

PCA does not see labels. It keeps the high-variance direction and drops the one that separates the classes.

What does whitening do to the kept components?

Each component is divided by its standard deviation, so all directions have equal variance; small, noisy components are amplified too.
