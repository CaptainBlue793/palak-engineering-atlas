Evaluating Multi-Agent Systems

A multi-agent system can be right for the wrong reasons, wrong for one agent's reasons, and right on one run and wrong on the next. Evaluating it needs more than one success rate. You need the end-to-end outcome, a check on each agent's part, a strong single-agent baseline run the same way, the spread across repeated runs, and the cost of each correct result. This chapter does all five on one task, and the answer differs from the one Chapter 4 gave.

The task is Chapter 4's release-readiness check on its 8 estates. Chapter 4 ran each design once at temperature 0. Here each ran 4 times at temperature 0.7, with seeds 1 to 4, as a production system sampling its answers would. There were three designs: one agent; a lead writing free-form briefs for workers; and code that plans and combines while agents only check. All ran on qwen3:14b, 96 runs in all, with every worker's finding kept for checking.

  • Score end-to-end outcomes strictly
  • Check each agent's contribution separately
  • Run a single-agent baseline under the same conditions
  • Measure variance: pass@k and pass^k
  • Compare designs by cost per correct result

End-to-end outcomes

Score the whole system on what the user gets, strictly. Here a run is right only if the go/no-go decision is right and the list of blockers is exact. The looser score is flattering. The code-planned design got go/no-go right in all 32 runs, while one agent got it right in 24 and the lead design in 21. On the strict score: code-planned 29 of 32, one agent 14 of 32, lead with free-form briefs 11 of 32. The lead design, which looked best in Chapter 4 at 7 of 8, came last.

Per-agent checks

An end-to-end score says the lead design failed 21 times. Per-agent checks say why. Every worker's finding was compared with the truth for its service, and every combined verdict with the findings it was built from.

  • Workers under free-form briefs: 55 of 187 findings were wrong (29%). At temperature 0.7, a brief that leaves out the 2.0% threshold or the 13:00 cut-off lets each worker decide for itself, and they decided differently.
  • Workers with code-written template briefs: 3 of 168 wrong.
  • The lead's combine step: in 8 of the lead design's 21 failures it did not match its own workers' findings, adding or dropping a service.

The fix list follows directly: briefs that carry the rules, and combining in code. Without per-agent checks you would be guessing.

for brief, finding in zip(run.briefs, run.findings):           # per-agent check: each worker against the truth
    wrong_findings += finding["blocking"] != (brief["service"] in truth.blocking)
flagged = {b["service"] for b, f in zip(run.briefs, run.findings) if f["blocking"]}
merge_faithful = set(run.verdict["blocking"]) == flagged             # per-agent check: the combine step

The single-agent baseline

The baseline has to run under the same conditions as the team: same model, same tools, same temperature, same repeats. Here one agent got 14 of 32, between the two team designs. Against it, the code-planned team is a clear improvement, and the free-form lead team is worse than not having a team at all. A team compared with a weakened baseline, or with a single lucky run, proves nothing. The fair comparison is repeated runs, scored the same way.

Variance

Repeats show what one run hides. pass@4 is the share of estates solved at least once in 4 runs; pass^4 is the share solved in all 4.

  • Lead with free-form briefs: pass@4 0.88 and pass^4 0.13. It could solve almost every estate sometimes, and solved almost none reliably.
  • One agent: 0.63 and 0.38.
  • Code-planned: 1.0 and 0.75. It solved every estate at least once and six of the eight every time.

For a system that acts on its answer, such as releasing or not, pass^k is the number that matters. Chapter 4's 7 of 8 came from one run per estate at temperature 0, which is one draw from a wide distribution.

pass_at_k  = mean(any(right[e]) for e in estates)       # solved at least once:  lead 0.88, code-planned 1.0
pass_hat_k = mean(all(right[e]) for e in estates)       # solved every time:     lead 0.13, code-planned 0.75

Cost-adjusted results

Divide the tokens by the correct results. One agent used about 5,100 tokens per run and 11,600 per correct result. The code-planned team used 14,000 per run and 15,400 per correct result. The free-form lead team used 8,200 per run and 23,900 per correct result, the most expensive per right answer despite being the cheaper team per run. Cost per correct result, alongside pass^k, is the honest summary. The code-planned team costs about a third more per correct answer than one agent and is right twice as reliably. Whether that trade is worth it depends on what a wrong release costs.

Use case: how agent benchmarks are run

Agent benchmarks increasingly report reliability as well as success. τ-bench introduced pass^k to show how quickly an agent's success falls when the same task must succeed several times. Multi-agent research systems are evaluated end to end with rubric-based judges, and teams check them against human labels. Failure analyses such as the MAST study in Chapter 14 label which agent and which step failed. In production the same layers appear as end-to-end success metrics on live traffic, per-agent assertions in traces (Chapter 22) and regression suites of past failures re-run on every change.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • One run per case: Chapter 4's 7 of 8 became 11 of 32 with repeats.
  • A loose score: go/no-go was right 32 of 32 for a design that was strictly right 29 times, and 21 of 32 for one right 11 times.
  • No per-agent checks, so you cannot tell bad briefs from a bad merge.
  • A baseline run under easier conditions than the team.
  • Reporting pass@k for a system that must be right every time.
  • Cost per run instead of per correct result: the cheaper team was the dearest per right answer.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How would you evaluate a multi-agent system?

How a strong answer sounds
Five layers: a strict end-to-end score; per-agent checks on each worker's output and on the combine step; a single-agent baseline run under the same conditions; repeated runs reported as pass@k and pass^k; and cost per correct result. In a test, the lead-and-workers design that looked best on one run was the least reliable over four (pass^4 of 0.13), and per-agent checks showed why: 29% of its workers' findings were wrong.

Deeper: A multi-agent system passes most cases in testing but fails often in production. What do you look for?

How a strong answer sounds
Variance first: testing at temperature 0 with one run per case measures one draw. Re-run each case several times at production settings and look at pass^k. In a test, a design went from 7 of 8 on one run to 11 of 32 over four repeats. Then per-agent checks, to find whether the plan, the workers or the merge fails. Then fix the component, often by moving the deterministic part into code, and keep the failing cases as a regression suite.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Score strictly, end to end: the loose score hid most of the failures.
  • Per-agent checks explain failures: 29% of workers' findings were wrong under free-form briefs, 2% with templates.
  • Run the single-agent baseline the same way: here it beat one of the two team designs.
  • Repeat runs: pass^4 was 0.13, 0.38 and 0.75 for designs that each "worked" on some run.
  • Compare cost per correct result: 11,600, 23,900 and 15,400 tokens.
