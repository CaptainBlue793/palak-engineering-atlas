Simulations & Agent Societies

Agents can also stand in for people. A model told to be "a terse customer" can test a support agent a thousand times overnight. A few models told to be sellers or players can show what a system of incentives does before anyone real is exposed to it. Simulation is one of the most useful things to do with many agents and one of the easiest to over-trust. A simulated person behaves like a model's idea of that person, and a simulated market like a model's idea of a market.

There were two experiments on qwen3:14b. First, a billing agent with a refund tool talked to simulated customers holding hidden facts, an account, an invoice and an amount, in four personas: cooperative, terse, confused, and withholding until told why. That made 20 conversations. Second, three seller agents with a cost of 10 set prices for 10 rounds in a market where all 100 buyers go to the cheapest seller. They ran with and without a public message board, three times each.

  • Build simulated users with hidden facts and a persona
  • Watch behaviour emerge from several agents with incentives
  • Use simulated users to find real bugs
  • Know what changes at scale
  • Know where simulation misleads

Simulated users

A simulated user is an agent with three things: private facts, a persona, and a goal. The support agent sees only what the simulated user says. Code knows the facts, so it can grade the outcome exactly. Here the refund was right, meaning the right account, invoice and amount, in 5 of 5 conversations with cooperative customers. With terse customers it was 0 of 5, with confused customers 0 of 5, and with withholding customers 3 of 5. The personas did their job: they found failures the cooperative case hid.

CUSTOMER = ("You are role-playing a customer contacting billing support. Facts only you know: account {account}; "
            "you were charged twice for invoice {invoice}, two payments of £{amount}. {persona}")
PERSONA["terse"] = "Give only the single detail the agent asked for, one per message, never volunteer anything."

Testing with simulated users

Each failing persona exposed a specific agent bug that a scripted test with all the facts up front would never show:

  • Terse: the agent asked for the account and the amount in one message, got only the account, and issued the refund for 0.00 instead of asking again, despite "never guess" in its prompt.
  • Confused: the customer gave a wrong invoice first. The agent never asked anyone to confirm anything, in 0 of 20 conversations, so it refunded the wrong invoice every time.
  • Withholding: twice the agent put the invoice number in the account field rather than wait for the account.

These are the bugs that real, imperfect customers trigger. The fixes are ordinary: ask one thing at a time, read back before acting, and validate arguments in the tool. Each fix can be checked by running the same personas again.

Limits of simulation

The simulated users were not real users. Cooperative customers put all three facts in their first message in 15 of 15 cases. Real customers rarely do, so the 5-of-5 for that persona is optimistic. Personas were mostly followed, but not always. One "confused" customer never gave the wrong invoice, and one "withholding" customer gave the account before being told why. Simulated users are also a model's guess at people: polite, articulate, consistent and quick to give up their goal. Use them to find failure modes cheaply and in bulk. Do not use them to estimate how often real users hit those failures; that needs real logs.

Emergent behaviour

In the market, the textbook outcome for identical sellers competing on price is a price close to cost, because each seller can win every buyer by undercutting the others by a little. The agents did not get there. Every seller in every run opened at exactly 15.00, and prices drifted down only slowly. Without a message board the 10-round average was 13.7 to 14.5, ending at 13.4 to 14.5, far above the cost of 10. With a board, the sellers wrote "Let's keep prices fair and competitive. If you're willing, let's discuss a collusive strategy to avoid undercutting each other," and "I agree with the collaborative approach." One run held 15.00 for all 10 rounds. Another drifted down to 11. Nobody told them to collude. The behaviour emerged from three agents, a goal and a channel. Research on LLM pricing agents has reported the same tendency at larger scale.

Scale

Simulations grow in two directions. One is more runs: the 20 conversations here took a few minutes, and a nightly suite of thousands is cheap next to real users. The other is more agents interacting. The Generative Agents study, in this course's case studies, ran 25 agents with memories in a simulated town. Costs grow with the number of agents times the number of interactions, and every agent reads its own context each step, as Chapter 16 measured. Emergent effects need enough agents and rounds to appear, and enough repeated runs to tell a pattern from one run's luck. These market numbers come from three runs per arm, which is enough to see the direction, not to measure the size.

Use case: simulation in practice

Simulated users are now a standard part of testing conversational agents. τ-bench pairs an agent with an LLM-simulated customer and checks the final database state. Teams build persona libraries to cover the awkward cases real users bring. Agent-based simulations with LLMs are used in research on social behaviour, markets and policy, from the 25-agent town of the Generative Agents study to studies of pricing agents that found them settling above competitive prices. Two practices recur. Simulations are used to find failure modes and compare designs, and validated against real behaviour before anyone quotes their rates. And incentive designs such as markets, auctions and negotiations are stress-tested with agents before people are exposed to them.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Testing only with cooperative simulated users: 5 of 5, while terse and confused were 0 of 5.
  • Quoting simulated failure rates as real ones: simulated users front-load facts real users hold back.
  • Not checking persona adherence before trusting a result.
  • Letting agents with shared incentives talk freely without expecting coordination against the goal.
  • Reading a pattern from one run: one board run held 15.00, another fell to 11.
  • No ground truth: simulated users need hidden facts that code can grade against.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How would you test a support agent with simulated users?

How a strong answer sounds
Give each simulated user private facts and a persona, and grade the agent's actions against the facts in code. Cover awkward personas, not just cooperative ones. In a test, cooperative customers got the right refund 5 of 5 times, while terse customers got 0 of 5, because the agent refunded 0.00 when the amount was not volunteered. Confused customers got 0 of 5, because the agent never asked anyone to confirm an invoice.

Deeper: What can multi-agent simulations tell you, and what can they not?

How a strong answer sounds
They find failure modes and show directions of behaviour cheaply. Three pricing agents never competed down to cost and, given a message board, openly proposed colluding. They do not give real-world rates: simulated customers put all their facts in the first message 15 of 15 times, which real customers do not, and personas slipped in 2 of 20 conversations. I would use simulation to find and fix behaviours, then validate the important ones against real data.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Simulated users need private facts, a persona and a code-graded outcome.
  • Awkward personas found real bugs: a refund of 0.00, a wrong invoice never questioned.
  • Simulated users are too helpful: cooperative ones volunteered every fact at once.
  • Three pricing agents stayed far above cost and, with a board, talked about colluding.
  • Use simulation to find failure modes and directions, not to estimate real rates.
