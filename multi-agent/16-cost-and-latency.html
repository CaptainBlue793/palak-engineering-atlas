Cost & Latency of Many Agents

Every agent you add reads its own instructions, its own tool schemas and its own context, and writes its own output. Tokens multiply. Time does not simply divide: agents running "in parallel" still queue for the same model, and waiting on tools is the only part that overlaps for free. This chapter measures both on one GPU, and then puts the token multipliers measured in this course next to what each one bought.

Sixteen qwen3:14b workers each checked one service of a synthetic estate. They used two tools, and every tool call waited 1 second, like a real API. They ran at concurrency limits of 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16, on one GPU served by Ollama one request at a time. Then the same 16 checks ran with a small context window and an output cap, and with thinking switched on.

  • Count tokens per agent and per team
  • Tell wall-clock time from total model time
  • Set concurrency limits from where the bottleneck is
  • Choose cheaper settings for workers, and check they are cheaper
  • Decide when more agents pay for themselves

Tokens per agent

A worker's cost is roughly its fixed overhead plus its share of the work. The overhead is the system prompt, the tool schemas and the final report, and every worker pays it. The 16 health-check workers used about 960 tokens each, 15,400 in all, across 48 model calls. The same overhead runs through this whole course. Every chapter that compared a team with one agent measured a multiplier, and the right question about each is what it bought. Sometimes it was a lot: three times the tokens took a 40-service check from 36 to 119 rows right. Sometimes nothing: 15 votes cost 15 times one sample and moved the score from 24.7 to 25. Sometimes the team was worse: 8.7 times the tokens for 4 of 6 against 6 of 6.

Wall-clock vs total time

With a concurrency limit of 1, the 16 workers ran one after another: 63.5 s, which is exactly the GPU's 31.5 s of model time plus 32 s of tool waits. Raising the limit let the tool waits overlap: 42.3 s at a limit of 2, then 31.2 s at 4. After that it flattened: 30.9 s at 8, 30.4 s at 16. The floor is the GPU's own work, about 31 s, because Ollama serves one request at a time. No amount of concurrency moves that floor. Only more model capacity does, such as a second GPU, a server that batches requests, or an API.

sem = asyncio.Semaphore(limit)                    # 1 → 63.5 s, 4 → 31.2 s, 16 → 30.4 s
async def worker(svc):
    async with sem:
        return await check(svc)                      # model calls queue on one GPU; tool waits overlap

Concurrency limits

Past the floor, more concurrency only moves the waiting around. Each worker's own latency, from start to report, went from a median of 3.9 s at a limit of 1 to 7.7 s at 4, 14.7 s at 8 and 26 s at 16. Every worker started at once and then queued for the GPU. The batch finished no sooner, and every individual result arrived later. Measured another way, the time workers spent waiting on a model call added up to 375 s at a limit of 16, against the GPU's 31 s of real work. Set the limit just high enough to keep the bottleneck busy, here about 4, and no higher. That keeps per-task latency low and leaves room for other work.

Cheaper and dearer settings for workers

Production teams often give workers a smaller or cheaper model. This course uses one model throughout, so it changed settings instead:

  • Small context window and output cap (2,048 context, 256 output tokens): the same 16 of 16 right and the same 15,500 tokens, because these prompts were short anyway. It was slightly slower, 35.2 s against 30.9 s, and changing the context size makes Ollama reload the model. A cheaper setting that does not match where the tokens go saves nothing.
  • Thinking on: also 16 of 16, at 2.6 times the tokens (39,900) and 10.7 times the time, 331 s, with each worker taking a median of 164 s. For a lookup task, thinking bought nothing.

Measure a cheaper worker against the task's real failure cases before you switch, as Chapter 10 did for thinking mode.

When it pays

Across the course the pattern is consistent. Extra agents paid when one agent was failing at something they fix: a list too long to work through carefully (Chapter 1's 40 services), more to look at than one pass covers (Chapter 3's modules), checks that can be grounded (Chapter 9's testing critic). They did not pay when one agent was already right (Chapter 8's labelling), when the work was coupled (Chapter 1's incident), or when the extra agents shared the same mistakes (Chapter 10's votes). Price a team as tokens per correct result, and on one GPU price its time as model time, because the model is the queue.

cost_per_correct = total_tokens / correct_results
# Ch1, 40 services: one agent 13,000 tokens / 36 rows = 361 per correct row; team 39,800 / 119 = 334
# Ch10, votes:     one sample 174 / 0.82 = 212 per correct answer;   15 votes 2,610 / 0.83 = 3,130

Use case: the bill for multi-agent systems

Anthropic reported that its multi-agent research system used about 15 times the tokens of a chat, and that token usage explained most of the variance in its results. The cost was worth paying for broad, valuable research questions and not for simple ones. Production teams manage it with the levers measured here. They cap concurrency at what the model backend can serve, route routine sub-tasks to smaller or cheaper models after testing them, cut repeated context such as long tool schemas, and track cost per successful task rather than per call. Hosted APIs add a lever this machine does not have: they serve requests concurrently, so wall-clock time falls with concurrency, but the token bill does not.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Expecting concurrency to beat the model's own floor: 31 s at every limit from 4 to 16.
  • Raising concurrency past the bottleneck: each worker's latency rose from 3.9 s to 26 s.
  • Assuming a setting is cheaper: a small window saved nothing and reloaded the model.
  • Turning on thinking for lookups: 10.7 times the time for the same result.
  • Pricing per call instead of per correct result.
  • Paying a multiplier for a gain the single agent already had.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Why doesn't running agents in parallel make the job proportionally faster?

How a strong answer sounds
Because only the waiting overlaps; the model work queues for the same capacity. Sixteen workers with 1-second tool calls took 63.5 s one at a time, 31.2 s with four at once, and 30.4 s with all sixteen. About 31 s was the GPU's own work, and no concurrency gets under that. Past the bottleneck, each worker's own latency just grew, from 3.9 s to 26 s.

Deeper: How do you decide whether a multi-agent design is worth its cost?

How a strong answer sounds
Compare cost per correct result against a strong single agent, on the failure cases that matter. In this course, three times the tokens took a 40-service check from 36 to 119 correct rows, a clear win per correct row, while 15 votes cost 15 times one sample for no real gain, and a team on a coupled incident used 8.7 times the tokens and did worse. I would also set concurrency from the bottleneck and test cheaper worker settings rather than assume they help.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Each worker pays its own overhead: about 960 tokens per health check here.
  • Concurrency overlaps waiting, not model work: 63.5 s → 31 s, then a floor at the GPU's time.
  • Past the bottleneck, each task waits longer: median worker latency 3.9 s → 26 s.
  • Test "cheaper" settings: a small window saved nothing; thinking cost 10.7 times the time for nothing.
  • Price a team per correct result: the course's multipliers ran from 1.4 to 34, and the gains did not follow the cost.
