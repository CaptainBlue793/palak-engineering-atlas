Context Isolation & Information Loss

Giving each agent its own small context is the main reason to use several agents (Chapter 1). Its price is that each agent knows only what reached it. When one agent learns something the others need, such as a decision, a constraint or a requirement from a stakeholder, nothing carries it across unless you build something that does. The others then make reasonable choices that conflict, and nobody notices until the parts are put together.

Five qwen3:14b agents each designed the REST API for one resource of the same shop: users, orders, invoices, products and webhooks. Every design fixes five conventions: id format, pagination, timestamps, error shape and field naming. First, how consistent are the five parts when nothing special is required? Then a harder case. Three designers each heard one requirement from a stakeholder, meant for the whole API: ids are prefixed strings, timestamps are Unix milliseconds, pagination is offset and limit. How far did each spread? Each set-up ran 6 times, with designers at temperature 0.7, as separate agents would differ.

  • Weigh what isolation buys against what it hides
  • Find what workers never see
  • Recognise conflicting decisions between isolated agents
  • Know what summaries between agents lose
  • Share decisions and requirements so they actually propagate

Why isolate

Isolation keeps each agent focused, keeps its prompt small and stops one agent's mistakes leaking into another's context. It also costs less than you might fear when the right answer is the obvious one. With no special requirements, the five isolated designers chose the same value for all five conventions in 4 of 6 runs, and 28 of 30 conventions matched. They chose UUIDs, offset-and-limit pagination, ISO 8601 timestamps, an error object and snake_case almost every time. The model has strong defaults, and isolated agents share them. A lead who decided the conventions first and put them in every brief, or a log of the earlier designers' decisions, made it 6 of 6. That was cheap, at about 1,200 to 1,400 tokens against 870 for isolated designers.

What workers never see

The trouble starts when the right answer is not the default and only one agent knows it. In the second experiment, the users designer was told that ids must be prefixed strings "across the whole API". The orders designer heard that "every timestamp in the API must be Unix milliseconds". The invoices designer heard that only offset-and-limit pagination is supported. Isolated, each requirement stayed with the designer who heard it. The prefixed ids appeared in 6 of 30 designs and Unix milliseconds in 7 of 30. Offset pagination appeared in 28 of 30, but only because it is what the model picks anyway. Not one of the 30 designs followed all three.

Conflicting decisions

A shared decision log ought to fix this: designers ran in turn, and each read the earlier designers' typed decisions with their reasons. It spread the first decision, not the requirements. The users designer went first, so its prefixed ids reached 30 of 30 designs. The orders designer went second. Its Unix milliseconds stayed at 8 of 30, because later designers saw two earlier conventions and followed the first one, the users designer's ISO timestamps. Pagination got worse than isolated, 23 of 30, as later designers copied an earlier choice instead of the invoices requirement. A decision log records what agents chose, and later agents conform to it. A requirement that arrives after a conflicting decision has already been made does not win.

- users: {"id_format": "prefixed string like usr_123", "timestamps": "ISO 8601 strings in UTC", ...}
         (reasons: ids must be prefixed strings like usr_123 ... across the whole API)
- orders: {"id_format": "prefixed string like usr_123", "timestamps": "Unix milliseconds", ...}
          (reasons: Orders are a core financial resource and must align with the finance requirement ...)

Summaries between agents

A model-written summary of the earlier designs, passed to each next designer, did no better: Unix milliseconds in 6 of 30, 4 of 30 designs fully compliant, at 3,560 tokens a run. A summary is a lossy hand-off, the same failure as Chapter 5's notes, which kept the problem and dropped the account number. A reconciliation pass did nothing at all. After all five were done, each designer saw the full log and was told to revise anything that conflicted with a requirement anyone had been given. Not one design changed in any of the 6 runs, and the pass nearly tripled the cost. Seeing a requirement inside someone else's reasons is not the same as being told it applies to you.

Shared decision logs that work

What worked was separating requirements from decisions and collecting them first. Before anyone designed anything, each designer copied word for word every requirement it had been given into a requirements register. Then all five designed against the full register, introduced as applying to the whole API. All three requirements reached 30 of 30 designs, every design followed all three in all 6 runs, and it cost about 2,140 tokens a run, less than the summaries. The lessons:

  • Gather constraints before design starts, not alongside it.
  • Pass them verbatim, with their scope, not paraphrased inside someone's reasons.
  • Keep "what must be true" (requirements) apart from "what someone chose" (decisions).
register = []
for res in RESOURCES:                                      # phase 1: collect, word for word
    register += ask(designer[res], "Copy every requirement you were given, word for word.", schema=REQUIREMENTS)
for res in RESOURCES:                                      # phase 2: design against all of them
    design[res] = ask(designer[res], brief(res) + "\nRequirements register (applies to the whole API):\n" + "\n".join(register))

Use case: design docs, ADRs and agent specs

Engineering teams solved this for people long ago. A design document or a set of architecture decision records (ADRs) captures constraints and decisions in one shared place before the parts are built, and a review checks each part against them. Agent systems that build software in parts follow the same pattern. Spec-first coding agents write the requirements and interface down before generating code. The multi-agent "software company" systems in this course's case studies pass a product requirements document and an architecture document between roles. What carries the information is a written, shared artefact with the requirements in it, not a summary of what the last agent did.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Testing isolation only on default choices: 4 of 6 runs consistent, which hides the real risk.
  • A requirement only one agent hears: isolated, 0 of 30 designs followed all three.
  • Expecting a decision log to spread requirements: it spread the first decision instead.
  • Summaries as the hand-off between agents, which drop the scope and the details.
  • A "revise if anything conflicts" pass: it changed 0 designs in 6 runs.
  • Paraphrased requirements: "every timestamp in the API" became "the finance requirement".

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is lost when agents have separate contexts?

How a strong answer sounds
Anything one agent learns that the others need. When the right choice is the model's default, it costs little: five isolated API designers matched on every convention in 4 of 6 runs. But when three designers each heard one API-wide requirement, isolated designs followed all three in 0 of 30. Separate contexts keep agents focused, so you have to build the channel for what must cross: a shared register of requirements, not hope.

Deeper: Agents share a decision log but still produce inconsistent parts. Why, and what do you change?

How a strong answer sounds
A decision log spreads whatever was decided first. In a test, the first designer's id format reached 30 of 30 designs while a later designer's timestamp requirement reached 8 of 30. Later agents conformed to the earlier choice, and a pass asking everyone to revise changed nothing. I would collect requirements verbatim, with their scope, before any design, give everyone the register, keep decisions in a separate log, and check the parts against the register in code. The register got 30 of 30.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Isolation is cheap when the right answer is the default: 4 of 6 runs consistent with no help.
  • A requirement only one agent hears stays there: 0 of 30 designs followed all three, isolated.
  • A decision log spreads the first decision, not the requirement: 30 of 30 for one, 8 of 30 for another.
  • Summaries lost the scope; a "revise if it conflicts" pass changed nothing.
  • A requirements register, collected first and shared verbatim, reached 30 of 30.
