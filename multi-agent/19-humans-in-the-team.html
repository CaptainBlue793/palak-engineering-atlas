Humans in a Team of Agents

Most multi-agent systems that act on the world keep a person somewhere in the loop: approving an action, checking a finding, steering a run that has gone off course. Where you put that person decides two things: which errors get caught, and how much of the person's day the system uses up. A reviewer asked to check everything catches everything and soon stops reading carefully. A reviewer asked at the wrong points catches nothing.

This chapter replays four approval policies on Chapter 18's recorded release checks: 16 cases for each of two designs, where each case is one run plus a second independent run of the same estate. The reviewer is simulated by code that always knows the truth, so a review asked for is a review done right. That isolates the question this chapter is about: given a perfect reviewer, how many errors does each policy catch, and for how many reviews?

  • Place checkpoints where a person adds the most
  • Choose an approval policy and count its workload
  • Steer a running team so the change reaches every agent
  • Explain a run to a person in a few lines
  • Keep the reviewer's workload sustainable

Checkpoints for people

A checkpoint is a point where the run stops and waits for a person: before an irreversible action, after a plan and before the work, or when the agents are unsure. In a multi-agent run there are more candidate points than people can staff: every plan, every worker's finding, every hand-off, every final decision. The useful checkpoints are where errors are both likely and costly, and where the person has what they need to judge. Checking a worker's finding takes a look at two numbers. Checking a whole plan takes knowing the system.

Approvals

The four policies, replayed on the free-form lead design, which was strictly right in only 6 of 16 cases unaided:

  • Check every finding: 16 of 16 right, for 94 reviews, nearly six per release.
  • Approve every "go": 8 of 16, for 5 reviews. It caught the dangerous kind of error, releasing past a blocker, but left the wrong no-go lists alone.
  • Check only where two runs disagree: each worker check was run twice and the person saw only the services where the two findings differed. 10 of 16, for 36 reviews.

On the code-planned design, which was right in 15 of 16 unaided, the same policies cost 84, 6 and 1 reviews. Its one wrong case stayed wrong under every policy except checking everything: both runs made the same mistake, so no disagreement flagged it, and it was a no-go, so no "go" approval caught it. The best way to cut a reviewer's workload is a system that makes fewer mistakes. The next best is to ask only where mistakes show.

def needs_review(service: str, first: dict, second: dict) -> bool:
    """Ask a person only where two independent worker checks disagree."""
    return first[service]["blocking"] != second[service]["blocking"]
# lead design: 36 reviews, 6 -> 10 of 16 right; code-planned design: 1 review, 15 of 16

Steering mid-run

Sometimes the person's job is not to approve but to change course: "also check the cache", "the threshold is 1.5% today". Chapter 15 measured what happens to a requirement that arrives partway through. Dropped into a decision log, it reached only the agents that had not yet decided, and later agents followed the earlier choice. A "please revise" pass changed nothing. Steering lands when it goes where requirements go: into the register that every agent reads, with its scope, and with the affected work re-run. A message to one agent is a hint that the rest never see.

Explaining a run

A person can only check what they can see. A lead-design release check produced about 8,200 tokens of model traffic, too much to read for each approval. What the reviewer needs fits in about ten lines: the verdict, each service with its finding and the numbers behind it, and which findings the agents disagreed on. The simulator below shows each case that way. Build that digest from typed findings, not by asking a model to summarise its own run (Chapter 5 showed what summaries drop), and link each line to the trace for anyone who wants to dig (Chapter 22).

Workload

Reviews are a budget. Spend it where the errors are. Checking every finding fixed everything at nearly 6 reviews per release, which no on-call engineer would keep doing carefully for long. Checking disagreements spent 36 reviews to fix 4 of the lead design's 10 errors, but only 1 review on the better design. Approving every "go" spent 5 or 6 reviews to remove the costly direction of error. In practice teams combine them: always approve irreversible or high-impact actions, and add review only for the specific uncertain parts. And measure the reviewer like any other component: reviews per day, time per review, and how often they overturn the agents.

Use case: human-in-the-loop in agent platforms

Agent frameworks build the checkpoint in. LangGraph can interrupt a graph before a node and resume it with a person's input. The OpenAI Agents SDK and others support approval on tool calls. Coding agents ask before running commands or writing outside the workspace. Operations teams add approval steps to automated remediation, so a person confirms before a restart or rollback. The design questions are the ones measured here. Which actions need approval? What does the person see? How do you keep the volume low enough that each approval is a real decision rather than a click?

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Asking a person to approve everything: 94 reviews for 16 releases, and attention runs out.
  • Relying on disagreement alone: mistakes both runs share are never flagged.
  • Approving only one direction and treating the other as safe without deciding so.
  • Showing the person the transcript instead of a digest of typed findings.
  • Steering one agent mid-run and expecting the rest to follow.
  • Not measuring the reviewer: overturn rate and time per review are system metrics.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Where would you put a human in a team of agents?

How a strong answer sounds
Before irreversible or high-impact actions always, and otherwise only where the agents show uncertainty, for example where two independent checks disagree. On recorded release checks, a person checking every finding fixed every case but took 94 reviews for 16 releases. Checking only disagreements took 36 on a weak design and 1 on a strong one. The best way to cut human workload is a design that makes fewer mistakes.

Deeper: What can disagreement-triggered review not catch, and what do you add?

How a strong answer sounds
Mistakes the agents share. On the strong design, the one wrong case was the same in both runs, so nothing flagged it, and it was a no-go, so a go-approval did not see it either. I would add an approval on the high-impact direction regardless of agreement, spot-check a sample of agreed cases to measure the shared-error rate, and vary prompts or methods between the two checks so their mistakes are less correlated (Chapter 10).

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Where you put the person decides what is caught and how much of their day it takes.
  • Checking everything fixed all 16 cases for 94 reviews; nobody keeps that up carefully.
  • Disagreement-triggered review cost 36 reviews on a weak design and 1 on a strong one.
  • Shared mistakes are never flagged by disagreement: add approvals on high-impact actions.
  • Steer through the shared register, and show reviewers a digest, not a transcript.
