Failure in Distributed Work

One agent fails as a whole: it stops, and you see it stop. A team of agents fails in pieces. One worker times out, another returns, a third is retried and does its work twice. The orchestrator has to decide what to do with half an answer. This primer covers partial failure, timeouts, retries, idempotency and cascading failure, the ideas every later chapter assumes.

The numbers come from seeded simulations, so they repeat exactly. Fan-outs of 3, 10 and 30 workers were run 2,000 times each at failure rates of 2%, 5% and 10%. Thirty clients retried against a backend that can serve five at once. A hundred jobs were retried after replies that arrived late. These are properties of the plumbing, so no model was needed.

  • Expect partial failure, and design the result for gaps
  • See why more workers means more failed runs
  • Retry with backoff and jitter, within a limit
  • Use idempotency keys so a retry does not repeat an effect
  • Stop one failure from spreading with timeouts and circuit breakers

Partial failure

When an orchestrator sends work to ten workers, the question is not whether one will fail but how often all ten succeed. If each fails independently with probability p, all n succeed with probability (1 − p)n. In 2,000 simulated runs, ten workers at a 5% failure rate all succeeded in 59% of runs. Thirty workers at 10% all succeeded in 4%. A plain gather raises on the first failure, so that is how often the orchestrator got an answer at all. With return_exceptions=True it got something back in every run, with gaps. The lesson is to design the final answer for gaps: say which parts are missing, rather than failing or inventing them.

results = await asyncio.gather(*(run_worker(b) for b in briefs), return_exceptions=True)
done    = [r for r in results if not isinstance(r, Exception)]
missing = [b.topic for b, r in zip(briefs, results) if isinstance(r, Exception)]
return synthesise(done, missing=missing)            # the answer names what it could not cover

Timeouts

A worker that never answers is worse than one that fails, because nothing tells you to stop waiting. Every call across a boundary needs a timeout: a model call, a tool call, a message to another agent. The timeout also creates a new problem. When it fires, you do not know whether the work happened. The worker may have finished and its reply may be late. In the simulation, 20% of replies were late, and the orchestrator retried each late job once. Of 100 jobs, 15 were applied twice, because the first write had already succeeded.

Retries, backoff and jitter

A retry turns a transient failure into a success. In the fan-out simulation, one retry per failed worker lifted ten workers at 5% from 59% to 97% of runs fully complete, and thirty at 10% from 4% to 75%. Retries also add load, and load is often why the call failed. Thirty clients called a backend that serves five at once and rejects the rest, each client allowed eight attempts:

  • Retry immediately: 205 attempts in 0.1 s, 200 rejected, and only 5 of 30 clients succeeded. The other 25 used up their attempts while the first five were still being served.
  • Exponential backoff (wait 0.05 s, then 0.1, 0.2, …): all 30 succeeded, with 130 attempts in 3.3 s. The clients waited the same amounts, so they kept colliding in waves.
  • Backoff with jitter (each wait multiplied by a random 0.5 to 1.5): all 30 succeeded, with 106 attempts in 1.1 s. Spreading the retries out filled the backend's free slots instead of hitting them all at once.
async def with_retry(call, attempts=4, base=0.5, retry_on=(TimeoutError, ConnectionError)):
    for k in range(attempts):
        try:
            return await asyncio.wait_for(call(), timeout=30)
        except retry_on:                              # transient only: never retry a bad request
            if k == attempts - 1: raise
            await asyncio.sleep(base * 2 ** k * random.uniform(0.5, 1.5))   # backoff with jitter

Idempotency keys

A retry is safe only if doing the work twice has the same effect as doing it once. Reading is idempotent; sending an email, charging a card or closing a ticket is not. The fix is an idempotency key. The caller makes up one key per piece of work and sends it with every attempt. The receiver records the keys it has acted on, together with the effect, and a repeated key returns the stored result and does nothing new. In the timeout simulation, the same 100 jobs with keys gave 0 double applications instead of 15. It is the idempotent consumer from Primer 3, applied to calls instead of messages. For agents, the key belongs to the step, not the attempt. It should come from the orchestrator's plan rather than from the model, which may write a different call on retry.

def close_ticket(ticket_id: str, idempotency_key: str) -> dict:
    if (done := store.get(idempotency_key)) is not None:
        return done                                    # a retry: same answer, no second effect
    result = tickets.close(ticket_id)
    store.put(idempotency_key, result)               # in the same transaction as the effect
    return result

Cascading failure

A cascade starts when one slow part makes its callers slow. In an agent team, a tool that hangs holds a worker; the orchestrator waits for the worker; the user waits for the orchestrator. Retries from every level multiply the load on the tool that was already struggling. Three defences stop the spread:

  • Timeouts at every level, each shorter than the level above, so the inner call gives up first.
  • Retry budgets, so only one level retries, or retries are capped as a share of traffic. Otherwise three levels of three attempts make up to 27 calls to the failing part.
  • Circuit breakers: after several failures in a row, stop calling the part for a while and fail fast, then let one test call through to see whether it has recovered.

These defences are not measured here. The Distributed Systems course covers them in depth, and Chapters 14 and 21 apply them to agents.

💳
Use case: the patterns are already in the APIs agents call

Payment APIs such as Stripe accept an Idempotency-Key header and return the stored result when a key repeats, which is exactly what an agent's retried refund needs. Cloud SDKs, including AWS's, retry throttled calls with exponential backoff and jitter by default. Model APIs return "overloaded" or rate-limit errors that their client libraries retry with backoff. Workflow engines such as Temporal record each step's result, so a retried workflow skips steps that already happened. When an agent calls these through tools, pass a key derived from the step, and leave retries to one layer instead of stacking another on top.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • A fan-out that fails if any worker fails, so more workers means fewer answers.
  • Hiding the gaps, so the final answer covers missing parts by inventing them.
  • No timeout on a call to a tool, a model or another agent.
  • Retrying immediately, or at every level, multiplying the load on the part that is failing.
  • Retrying calls with side effects without a key, so a late reply becomes a second charge.
  • Retrying errors that will not change, such as a bad request or a refused permission.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Why do more workers make a fan-out less reliable, and what do you do about it?

How a strong answer sounds
If each worker fails independently, all of them succeed with probability (1 − p)n, which falls fast as n grows. In a simulation, 10 workers at 5% all succeeded in 59% of runs and 30 at 10% in 4%. I would gather with return_exceptions so one failure does not sink the run, retry transient failures once with backoff and jitter (that lifted 59% to 97%), and have the final answer say which parts are missing.

Deeper: An agent's tool call to issue a refund timed out. Should it retry?

How a strong answer sounds
Only with an idempotency key. A timeout does not tell you whether the refund happened; the reply may just be late. In a simulation with 20% late replies, retrying 100 jobs without keys applied 15 of them twice; with keys, none. The key should come from the orchestrator's step, not the model, so every attempt carries the same one. I would retry with backoff and jitter, cap the attempts, and have only one layer retry.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • All n workers succeed with probability (1 − p)^n: 30 workers at 10% completed 4% of runs.
  • Gather with return_exceptions and let the answer name its gaps.
  • One retry lifted 10 workers at 5% from 59% to 97% of runs complete.
  • Immediate retries served 5 of 30 clients; backoff with jitter served all 30 in 1.1 s.
  • A timeout does not mean the work failed: idempotency keys cut double applications from 15 to 0.
