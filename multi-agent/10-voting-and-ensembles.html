Voting & Ensembles

The simplest way to use many agents is to ask the same question several times and take the most common answer. It needs no coordination at all: no messages, no roles, no hand-offs. Whether it works depends on one thing, how independent the mistakes are. If each sample is wrong in its own way, the majority is usually right. If the samples share a mistake, the vote only repeats it with more confidence.

This chapter uses Chapter 9's 30 computing questions. qwen3:14b answered each one 15 times with a brief prompt at temperature 0.8, then 5 times each with a step-by-step prompt and with a "list every quantity with its unit first" prompt. It also gave one answer at temperature 0 and one with thinking switched on. Votes of every size were computed from those samples.

  • Use self-consistency: sample, then take the majority
  • Read how accuracy grows with the number of votes
  • Spot mistakes every sample shares
  • Mix prompts and settings instead of repeating one
  • Use agreement as a confidence signal

Self-consistency

Sampling at a temperature above 0 gives different reasoning paths, and often different answers. One sample with the brief prompt was right 82% of the time. Self-consistency means taking several samples and returning the most common answer. Votes need no protocol between agents, only a way to compare answers, which is easy for numbers and labels and hard for free text. Each sample costs the same, about 174 tokens here, so 15 votes cost 15 times one answer.

from collections import Counter
answers = [ask(question, temperature=0.8, seed=s) for s in range(15)]
(best, n), = Counter(a for a in answers if a is not None).most_common(1)
agreement = n / len(answers)                     # 0.8 or more was right 22 of 22 times here

Majority vote and diminishing returns

More votes barely helped. Averaged over many random draws from the 15 samples, one sample got 24.7 of 30 questions right, 5 votes 25.1, 9 votes 25.2, and all 15 votes 25. On 25 questions the model was already mostly right, so the vote changed nothing. On the other 5 the majority was wrong, and adding samples only made the wrong majority bigger. A vote cannot get past the share of samples that are right. When that share is below a half, more votes make it worse.

Correlated mistakes

The five questions the vote lost were not hard in an exotic way. On four of them the model made the same slip again and again (the fifth, a birthday-bound estimate, scattered across many wrong answers):

  • Error budget (0.1% of 30 days at 100 requests per second): 9 of 15 samples said 25,920, ten times too small.
  • Errors per hour (0.5% of 2,000 per second): 11 of 15 said 3,600, again ten times too small.
  • Link transfer (1 GiB over 1 Gbit/s): 11 of 15 said 8 seconds, treating GiB as GB.
  • Write quorum (5 replicas, R = 2): 9 of 15 said 3.

Samples from one model at one prompt share its habits. Asking it again is not a second opinion. These are the mistakes Chapter 9's debate also failed to fix when both agents started wrong.

Mixing prompts and settings

Changing how the question was asked did more than asking again. The step-by-step prompt alone was right 86% of the time per sample, and its 5 votes got 29 of 30. It fixed the error-budget, GiB, quorum and birthday-bound questions; only errors per hour stayed wrong, 3 of its 5 samples still saying 3,600. Mixing all three styles, 5 votes each, got 28. The lesson has two parts. A better prompt beats a bigger vote, so test prompts before adding samples. And a mix of methods beats repeating one method, because different methods make different mistakes. This course runs every agent on qwen3:14b, so it mixes prompts and settings rather than models. Thinking mode, the most expensive setting, did not help here: one thinking answer at temperature 0 got 23 of 30, against 26 for the plain answer, at about 10 times the tokens (1,770 against 170) and 22 seconds per question.

Agreement as confidence

The most useful thing a vote produced was not the answer but the agreement. When 80% or more of the 15 samples agreed, the majority answer was right in 22 of 22 questions. When agreement was lower, it was right in only 3 of 8. Chapter 7's router never chose "unsure" when asked to. Agreement between samples is a confidence signal the model cannot talk its way out of. Use it to route the low-agreement cases to a stronger check, a better prompt or a person, and let the high-agreement ones through.

Use case: self-consistency and ensembles in practice

Self-consistency was introduced in research on chain-of-thought reasoning, where sampling several reasoning paths and voting raised accuracy on maths word problems. Evaluation teams use the same idea when they run a model-graded check several times and keep only verdicts that agree. Production systems more often use agreement than the vote itself. They sample twice and compare, and send disagreements to a slower path such as a larger model, a tool call or a human review queue. That is cheaper than voting on everything and catches the cases where the model is unsure.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Paying for 15 votes when one sample was already 82% right and the vote reached 25 of 30.
  • Treating repeated samples as independent opinions: 11 of 15 shared one wrong answer.
  • Voting before fixing the prompt: a step-by-step prompt with 5 votes got 29.
  • Assuming the expensive setting helps: thinking mode got 23 of 30 at 10 times the tokens.
  • Throwing away the agreement, the cheapest confidence signal you have.
  • Voting on free text without a way to decide when two answers are the same.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: When does majority voting over several model answers help?

How a strong answer sounds
When each sample is right more often than not and the mistakes are independent. In a test on 30 questions, one sample was right 82% of the time and 15 votes reached only 25 of 30, because on 5 questions most samples made the same mistake, such as an error budget ten times too small. Changing the prompt to step-by-step and voting 5 times got 29.

Deeper: How would you use several samples to decide when to escalate to a person?

How a strong answer sounds
Measure agreement. With 15 samples, when at least 80% agreed the answer was right 22 of 22 times; below that, 3 of 8. So I would take a few samples, preferably with different prompts, let high-agreement answers through, and send low-agreement ones to a stronger check or a person. That uses the samples as a confidence signal, which a model will not give reliably when asked directly.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • More votes barely helped: 24.7 of 30 with one sample, 25 with fifteen.
  • Samples share mistakes: on 5 questions the majority was wrong and grew with more votes.
  • A better prompt beat a bigger vote: 5 step-by-step votes got 29 of 30.
  • Thinking mode got 23 of 30 at about 10 times the tokens; test settings, don't assume.
  • Agreement of 80% or more was right 22 of 22 times; route low agreement for a second check.
