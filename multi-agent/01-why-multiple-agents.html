Why More Than One Agent (and When Not)

A team of agents promises three things one agent lacks: a fresh, small context for each piece of work, pieces running at the same time, and specialists. It also costs something on every run: more model calls, more tokens, and the work of splitting the task and putting the answers back together. This chapter measures both sides on the same model and tools, so you can tell which tasks are worth a team.

Two tasks, each run by one agent and by a team of qwen3:14b agents. The broad task is a health check: report the error rate, latency and newest deploy of every service, for 10, 20 and 40 services. The parts are independent. The coupled task is the incident from Primer 1: follow the errors to the service where the failure starts. Each step depends on the last. Every tool call waits 0.5 s, like a real API, in both arms.

  • Recognise when one agent is overloaded, and how it fails
  • See what a separate context per agent buys
  • Know what runs in parallel and what does not
  • Measure the coordination tax in calls and tokens
  • Recognise the tasks where one agent wins

What one agent runs out of

On the 10-service health check, one agent did well. It batched its tool calls, about ten per step, finished in 4 model calls, and got 57 of 60 rows right across six estates. The three misses were the wrong deploy, an older one listed first. Then the estate grew. On estates with each service's deploys listed out of time order, one agent got 25 of 30 rows right at 10 services and 59 of 60 at 20. At 40 services, two of three runs fetched every service's metrics and then wrote the report without ever calling get_deploys. Every metric was right (80 of 80) and every deploy was made up: "v1.2.3", "v2.1.0" and so on down the list. It was not the context window: the longest prompt in those runs was about 3,000 of 16,384 tokens. A long list of similar work is where one agent cuts corners, and it fills the gaps with plausible values instead of saying they are missing.

Separate contexts

The team gave each service to its own worker: a fresh conversation with one instruction, two tool calls and a typed report. No worker read more than about 500 tokens, however many services there were, while the single agent's prompt grew to about 1,800 at 10 services, 3,500 at 20 and up to 6,900 at 40. A worker cannot skip another service's work, because it never sees it. At 40 services the team got 119 of 120 rows right, against 36 for one agent. This is the main reason teams exist: each agent's context stays small and on topic, however large the whole job gets.

async def health_check(services: list[str]) -> list[dict]:
    # one worker per service: its own messages, its own two tool calls, a typed row back
    rows = await asyncio.gather(*(agent(CHECK_SYS, f"Report the error rate, p99 and newest deploy for {s}.",
                                       tools, schema=ROW, max_steps=8) for s in services))
    return rows                                    # merged by code: no lead agent needed

Parallel work

The team was also faster on 10 services: 21 s against 33 s. That was not the model. One GPU serves one request at a time (Primer 2), so the team's 30 model calls still ran one after another. What overlapped was the waiting. The single agent's loop ran its 21 tool calls in turn at 0.5 s each, while the workers waited on theirs together. At 20 services the gap was 41 s against 61 s. At 40, the team's 120 model calls took about as long as everything else saved: 83 s, against 74 to 127 s for one agent. Parallel agents save the time spent waiting on tools and people. They do not create model capacity you do not have.

The coordination tax

Every agent needs its own instructions, its own tool schemas and its own final report, and a lead may then read all the reports. On the 10-service check the team used 2.2 times the tokens of one agent (12,400 against 5,600) and 30 model calls against 4. At 40 services it was 3.1 times (39,800 against 13,000). That price bought the 119 against 36. On a task where one agent was already right, the same price buys nothing. The tax also includes the code you write to split the task and merge the answers, and the new ways for the team to fail (Chapter 14).

When one agent wins

On the coupled incident, one agent with the method from Primer 1 followed the errors and found the cause in 6 of 6 incidents, using 7 model calls, 4,100 tokens and 5 s on average. A team cannot split this task well, because nobody knows which services matter until the evidence is read. So the lead sent a worker to all 10 services and read their findings. It found the cause in 4 of 6, with 66 model calls, 35,400 tokens and 31 s: 8.7 times the tokens for a worse result. In both misses the cause was the email service, and the worker sent to email reported it healthy. Its brief was the same as every other worker's, "Check email.", and the model read it as a request to check an inbox. It replied "I cannot check your email" without calling a tool, and the required report format turned that refusal into "status: healthy". The brief "Check the email service." worked. The lead then had "orders: errors from email" and "email: healthy" in front of it, did not question the contradiction, and blamed orders. When each step depends on the last, one agent following the thread beats a team guessing in advance, and every hand-off is a place for a wrong finding to be believed.

Use case: breadth-first research

Anthropic has described its multi-agent research feature: a lead agent plans, then starts subagents that search different parts of the question at the same time in their own context windows. They reported it beat a single agent on broad research questions and used roughly 15 times the tokens of a chat. They also noted that coding tasks, where steps depend closely on each other, suit it less. Both halves match this chapter's numbers: a team for broad, separable work where one agent cuts corners, and one agent for a thread that has to be followed step by step. The case study at the end of the course covers that system.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Adding agents before measuring one, so there is no baseline to beat.
  • Splitting a coupled task, so the lead guesses which parts matter and pays for all of them.
  • Expecting parallel agents to be faster on one model server: only the waiting overlaps.
  • Trusting every worker's finding, even when two findings contradict each other.
  • Terse briefs: "Check email." was read as "check your inbox", and a forced format hid the refusal.
  • Letting one agent grind through a long list and accepting a report with invented values.
  • Counting the team's accuracy and not its tokens: 2 to 9 times here.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: When would you use several agents instead of one?

How a strong answer sounds
When the work splits into independent parts and one agent, measured, does badly on the whole. In a test, one agent asked for 40 services' health read every metric and then invented every deploy version; a team with one small worker per service got 119 of 120 rows. It cost about 3 times the tokens, and it was faster only because tool waits overlapped. If one agent already does the job, a team just costs more.

Deeper: Someone proposes a team of agents for incident root-cause analysis. What do you say?

How a strong answer sounds
That it is a coupled task: each step depends on the last result, so the team cannot know in advance which services matter. In a test, one agent following the errors found the cause in 6 of 6 incidents with about 4,100 tokens. A lead with one worker per service found it in 4 of 6 with 35,400. In both misses, one worker misread its brief ("Check email.") as a request to check an inbox, the report format turned its refusal into "healthy", and the lead believed it despite another worker pointing at email. I would keep one investigating agent and, if needed, add parallel workers only for independent checks it asks for.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • One agent did well on 10 and 20 services; at 40 it skipped the deploys and invented them in 2 of 3 runs.
  • Separate contexts kept every worker near 500 tokens or less; the team got 119 of 120 rows at 40 services.
  • The team was faster only by overlapping tool waits: 21 s against 33 s at 10 services, no faster at 40.
  • The coordination tax was 2.2 to 3.1 times the tokens on the broad task and 8.7 times on the coupled one.
  • On a coupled task one agent won: 6 of 6 against 4 of 6, at an eighth of the tokens.
