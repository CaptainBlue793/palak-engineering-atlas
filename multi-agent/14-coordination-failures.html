Coordination Failures

One agent fails in ways you can see in its trace: a wrong tool call, a loop, a confident wrong answer. A team of agents adds failures that live between agents. A brief one agent misreads, a result another ignores, a promise to act that nobody keeps, two agents who agree on something neither should accept. A published taxonomy, MAST, sorts these failures into three groups, and every one of them has already happened in this course.

This chapter does two things. First, it counts the coordination failures recorded in Chapters 1 to 9, each mapped to a MAST failure mode, with the counts recomputed from the stored runs. Then it measures a failure the earlier chapters did not cover. Two qwen3:14b agents negotiate a one-hour deploy window, each knowing only its own constraints, in 12 scenarios. In 6 exactly one window works, and in 6 none does. They tried five ways of reaching agreement.

  • Name the three groups of multi-agent failure
  • Recognise misalignment between agents in a trace
  • Spot failed verification: agreement that checks nothing
  • Detect loops and deadlocks, and design them out
  • Measure failure rates by mode, not just success

Specification failures

MAST, the Multi-Agent System failure Taxonomy, comes from a study by Cemri and colleagues (2025), "Why Do Multi-Agent LLM Systems Fail?", which labelled traces from several popular multi-agent frameworks. Its first group is failures of specification: an agent breaks the task or its role, does not know when to stop, or loses earlier context. This course recorded several. In Chapter 2, a reviewer ended 14 reviews with "APPROVED" after its reasoning, and the loop missed 11 of them. That is an unclear termination condition. In Chapter 4, a lead asked for the target's dependencies listed the target itself in 6 of 8 runs. In Chapter 5, a billing agent with no account number refunded an invented one in 3 of 10. The fix in each case was not a better prompt but a sharper interface: a typed verdict, a validated plan, a tool that rejects placeholders.

Misalignment between agents

The second group lives in the gaps. One agent fails to ask for clarification, ignores another's input, withholds information, drifts off task, or says one thing and does another. In Chapter 1 a worker read "Check email." as checking an inbox and reported "healthy" instead of asking. In Chapter 5 a front agent wrote "I will transfer your case" and did not, in 15 of 30 conversations. That is a reasoning-action mismatch. Its hand-off notes left out the account number 10 times in 10. In Chapter 6 a deployer in a group chat answered the chat instead of calling its tool. The common thread is that each agent did something reasonable from inside its own context. The failure only exists in the space between contexts.

Verification failures

The third group is failing to check: stopping too early, checking too little, or checking wrongly. In Chapter 4 a lead stopped after one or two briefs in 7 of 8 runs. In Chapter 7 an analysis stage invented statistics from empty input, and nothing downstream noticed. In Chapter 9 eight debates ended with both agents agreeing on a wrong answer. Verification failures are the most dangerous, because they turn the other two groups into confident output. A team that fails loudly can be fixed. A team whose checker approves the failure cannot be fixed until someone notices.

Loops and deadlocks

The negotiation shows how two agents with all the information still fail to coordinate. Every run was capped at 8 turns:

  • Free chat: 2 of 12 correct, and 10 hit the cap, every infeasible case among them. By turn 2 both agents had listed every hour they could not accept, enough to see that no window worked. They went on proposing one hour at a time, each time an hour the other had already ruled out.
  • Typed proposals, with a "no feasible window" exit: 0 of 12 correct. Turns ended, but often by agreeing on a window that broke a rule. That happened 5 times, twice in cases where no valid window existed at all.
  • The same with a code arbiter that rejects any accepted window breaking a constraint: no false agreements, but back to 10 deadlocks and 1 of 12 correct.

The protocol was not the problem. Negotiation was the wrong mechanism for a problem that is an intersection of two sets.

forbidden = {who: ask(agent, "List the start hours you cannot accept, as integers.", schema=FORBIDDEN) for who, agent in team}
allowed   = set(HOURS) - forbidden["lead"] - forbidden["dba"]        # code does the logic
window    = min(allowed) if allowed else None                    # 12 of 12, ~400 tokens

Designing the failure out

Two more set-ups took negotiation away. Asking each agent to list the hours it can accept, so that code could intersect them, got only 4 of 12. The model had to invert "you cannot accept 08:00, 10:00…" into a list of allowed hours and got the inversion wrong in 10 of 12 cases, which produced 8 false agreements. Asking each agent to copy the hours it was told it cannot accept, which is the form its rules were in, and letting code compute the rest got 12 of 12. It took two calls and about 400 tokens, against 3,700 to 4,700 for negotiating. The general rule: let agents report what they know in the form they know it, and let code do the logic. Coordination failures shrink when there is less to coordinate.

Measuring failure rates

"The team got it right 7 times in 12" does not say what to fix. Count failures by mode, as the catalogue below does. Each mode has a different fix, and the counts show where to start. Making the counts cheap means storing what each agent sent and received, which this course has done throughout. Chapter 18 builds evaluation around these counts, and Chapter 22 uses traces to find the first bad step.

Use case: MAST and failure analysis in practice

The MAST study labelled traces from open-source frameworks, among them ChatDev, MetaGPT and AG2, on coding and maths tasks. It found failures spread across all three groups, with no single fix covering most of them. Its authors also showed that a better prompt or role description recovered only part of the lost performance, and argued that structural changes were needed: verification steps, clearer termination and better communication protocols. Teams running agent systems in production report the same practice this chapter uses: store every inter-agent message, label failures by mode on a sample of runs, and fix the largest mode first.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Measuring only success, so you cannot tell a misread brief from a missing check.
  • Letting agents negotiate a problem code can solve: 2 of 12 against 12 of 12.
  • No turn cap, or a cap with no outcome: 10 of 12 free chats would have run for ever.
  • Typed messages without checks: they ended runs, but in 5 agreements that broke a rule.
  • Making a model transform data (invert a list) instead of copying it and letting code transform it.
  • Fixing coordination with prompts when the interface or the mechanism is wrong.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: How do multi-agent systems fail?

How a strong answer sounds
In three ways, following the MAST taxonomy: specification failures (an agent breaks the task, its role or the stop condition), misalignment between agents (a misread brief, a promised action not taken, information left out of a hand-off) and failed verification (stopping early, checking nothing, agreeing on a wrong answer). In the runs behind this course, a front agent announced transfers it never made in 15 of 30 conversations, and hand-off notes dropped the account number 10 of 10 times.

Deeper: Two agents keep looping without agreeing. What do you do?

How a strong answer sounds
First ask whether they should be negotiating at all. In a test, two agents choosing a deploy window under private constraints got 2 of 12 right in free chat, with 10 runs hitting the turn cap, and typed proposals with a "no window" exit got 0 of 12 with 5 agreements that broke a rule. Having each agent copy its constraints as data and letting code intersect them got 12 of 12 for a tenth of the tokens. Where negotiation is needed, cap the turns, define what happens at the cap, and check every agreement in code.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • MAST sorts multi-agent failures into specification, misalignment between agents, and verification.
  • Every group occurred in this course's own runs; count failures by mode to know what to fix.
  • Free negotiation deadlocked in 10 of 12; typed proposals ended with 5 rule-breaking agreements.
  • A code arbiter stopped bad agreements but not deadlocks; changing the mechanism solved 12 of 12.
  • Let agents report facts in the form they have them; let code do the logic.
