The Multi-Agent Interview Playbook

Multi-agent questions are now common in interviews for AI engineering and platform roles. They come as design rounds ("design a research assistant with several agents"), concept questions ("what is a hand-off?"), the judgement call ("one agent or many?") and debugging scenarios ("the agents keep looping"). Strong answers have the same shape: a clear structure, a single-agent baseline, the specific ways the design can fail, and numbers.

This chapter gathers the course into that shape. It gives a framework for design rounds in eight steps, model answers for five common prompts, concept questions, the decision about one agent or many, and debugging scenarios. Every answer cites a measurement from this course, all on qwen3:14b, so you can say "I measured this" rather than "I read that".

Design rounds

A design round gives you a product and 45 minutes. Interviewers listen for structure, and for whether you know where these systems break. Start from the single-agent version and earn every extra agent. For each split, say what crosses between agents and in what shape. Say what happens when a part fails, who holds which privilege, and how you would know it works. The panel below walks five common prompts through the framework. Try each step before opening its answer.

Concept questions

Expect short definitions followed by "and what goes wrong?". A few with the answers this course measured:

  • Hand-off: passing control and context to another agent. It should be a tool call with typed fields. Free-text notes dropped the account number 10 of 10 times, and front agents announced transfers they never made in 15 of 30 conversations (Chapter 5).
  • Orchestrator–worker: a lead splits the task, briefs workers and combines. The split is the weak point. A lead included the target in its own dependencies in 6 of 8 runs (Chapter 4).
  • Blackboard: shared state that agents read and write. Whole-document writes kept 11 of 48 findings and per-key writes kept 48 (Chapter 11).
  • MCP and A2A: tools versus agents. A planted instruction in a remote agent's result was obeyed 5 of 5 times unless it was taken in as typed fields (Chapter 12).
  • Self-consistency: sample and vote. Fifteen votes moved 24.7 to 25 of 30, a better prompt reached 29, and agreement of 80% or more was right 22 of 22 times (Chapter 10).

One agent or many

This is the question behind every design round, and the honest answer is "measure". A team helped where one agent was failing at something a team fixes. A 40-service check went from 36 to 119 rows right (Ch 1), and modules with several kinds of issue from 10 to 12 found (Ch 3). It did not help where one agent was already right, such as labelling 60 items (Ch 8). It hurt on coupled work: 4 of 6 against 6 of 6 on an incident chain, at 8.7 times the tokens (Ch 1). Teams cost 1.4 to 34 times the tokens across this course (Ch 16). Give the interviewer the rule — separable work, a measured failure of one agent, waiting that overlaps — and one number on each side.

Debugging scenarios

"Our agents …" questions test whether you go to the evidence. The pattern is to read the trace, find the first bad step, replace that component's output with the right one and re-run, then fix the component and add the case as a test (Chapter 22). Common scenarios and where to look:

  • "They loop and never agree." Check whether negotiation is the right mechanism at all. Two agents choosing a time window deadlocked in 10 of 12 runs, and stating constraints as data for code to intersect solved 12 of 12 (Ch 14).
  • "The answer is confident and wrong." Look for an upstream stage that failed quietly. An analysis stage invented statistics from empty input, and the report repeated them (Ch 7).
  • "It worked in testing." Look at variance. One temperature-0 run showed 7 of 8; four repeats at 0.7 showed pass^4 of 0.13 (Ch 18).
  • "The framework's agent never uses its tools." Read the raw requests. A typed-output setting forced JSON and stopped every tool call (Ch 13).

A design framework

The eight steps, in the order an interviewer wants to hear them: clarify the job and build the single-agent baseline; decide one agent or many; split the work and write briefs; decide how agents share information; set limits and handle failure; assign trust and privilege; evaluate; and operate, with people, durability and traces. Each step has a measured default from this course. The defaults are a typed message over prose, code over a model for deterministic parts, a register for requirements, least privilege, pass^k over one run, and cost per correct result.

Use case: what these rounds look like

Companies building AI products now ask candidates to design an agent system end to end: a research assistant, a customer-support system, a coding assistant, an operations agent. They often add a follow-up about failure, cost or security. Some ask for a take-home in which you build a small multi-agent pipeline and report how you evaluated it. The candidates who stand out bring their own measurements. Build a small version of one of these five designs, run it on a dozen cases several times, and you will have numbers of your own to cite.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Starting with five agents instead of one agent and a reason to add more.
  • Naming roles instead of interfaces: what crosses between agents, in what shape?
  • Leaving out failure: timeouts, partial results, retries, idempotency.
  • No evaluation plan, or a single run at temperature 0.
  • Ignoring cost, or quoting it per call instead of per correct result.
  • Treating other agents' output as trusted.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: Should this be one agent or many?

How a strong answer sounds
I would build one agent first and measure it. If the work splits into independent parts and the single agent is failing on the whole, such as a long list it cuts corners on, a team can help: one worker per service took a 40-service check from 36 to 119 correct rows. If the steps depend on each other, one agent following the thread wins: 6 of 6 against 4 of 6 for a team, at an eighth of the tokens.

Deeper: Walk me through how you would make a multi-agent system you have built reliable enough to ship.

How a strong answer sounds
Typed interfaces everywhere: briefs from templates, hand-offs with required fields, findings keyed by the dispatched task, requirements in a register. Code does what is deterministic: planning a graph walk, merging, privileged actions. Limits on every loop and call, idempotency keys on side effects, checkpoints so a crash resumes. Then evaluation with repeats and per-agent checks. On one task this course measured, that design reached pass^4 of 0.75 against 0.13 for a free-form lead and workers.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Start every design from a measured single agent, and earn each extra agent.
  • Answer in eight steps: baseline, one or many, split, sharing, limits, trust, evaluation, operation.
  • Name interfaces and their shapes, not just roles.
  • Bring numbers for both sides of "one agent or many".
  • Debug from the trace: first bad step, counterfactual replay, a regression test.
