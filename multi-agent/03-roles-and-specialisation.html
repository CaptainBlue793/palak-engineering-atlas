Roles & Specialisation

Multi-agent systems are usually described by their roles: a planner, a researcher, a security reviewer, a tester. A role is a few sentences of system prompt, sometimes with its own tools. It is fair to ask what those sentences change. Is a "security reviewer" better at security than the same model asked to review for security? Does it stay in its lane? Is it worth an extra model call?

To find out, qwen3:14b reviewed code with planted problems in four set-ups. The first was a plain generalist. The second was three specialists (security, performance, correctness) given only their role. The third was a generalist given a checklist of what to look for. The fourth was the three specialists, each given its own part of that same checklist. There were two sets. 16 short snippets each had one planted issue or none. 4 longer modules of about 30 lines each had three issues, one per kind. A problem counted as found if a finding pointed at it.

  • Separate what a role adds from what its instructions add
  • See where specialists beat a generalist, and where they do not
  • Give each role only the tools it needs
  • Recognise role drift
  • Measure specialisation against a fair baseline

Role prompts

A role prompt does two things at once. It says who the agent is ("You are a security reviewer"), and usually what to look for. To separate the two, compare a role on its own with a generalist given the same list of things to look for. On the short snippets the role barely mattered. Every set-up found 11 or 12 of the 12 issues, so the task was too easy to tell them apart. The differences were in the noise. The plain generalist raised 4 false alarms on the 4 clean snippets, such as warning that returning a database row "may expose sensitive information". The checklist generalist raised 1, calling a parameterised query vulnerable to SQL injection, and both specialist set-ups raised 0. The one issue most set-ups missed was a secret compared with ==, a timing leak. Only the checklist generalist found it.

CHECK = {
 "security":    "untrusted input reaching SQL, shell commands or file paths; secrets compared or handled unsafely",
 "performance": "queries or other slow calls inside loops, work that grows quadratically, loading far more data than needed",
 "correctness": "off-by-one errors, shared mutable defaults, wrong arithmetic, errors swallowed so failures look like success",
}
generalist  = "You are a code reviewer. Report real problems. Check for " + "; ".join(CHECK.values())
specialists = {r: f"You are a {r} reviewer. Report only {r} problems. Check for {c}." for r, c in CHECK.items()}

Where specialists win

The longer modules separated the set-ups. Each had three issues among four functions, so a single reviewer had to keep several kinds of problem in mind at once. The plain generalist found 7 of 12. Role-only specialists found 9. The generalist with the checklist found 10. The specialists with their part of the checklist found all 12. Two effects add up. The checklist told the model what to look for (7 → 10), and splitting it into three focused passes caught the rest (10 → 12). That cost 2.4 times the tokens (5,600 against 2,400) and nearly twice the time, because the three passes share one GPU. Specialisation pays when there is more to look at than one pass reliably covers. It is the same lesson as Chapter 1's 40-service health check.

Specialised tools

A role can also mean a smaller set of tools. A worker that only reads metrics gets one tool schema, not twenty. That keeps its prompt short, leaves fewer wrong tools to choose and limits what it can damage. This chapter's reviewers needed no tools, so it does not measure this. Chapter 1's workers had two tools each, Chapter 6 splits tools between agents, and Chapter 17 treats a role's tools as its privileges.

Different models per role

Production teams often give each role a different model: a large one for the lead, small fast ones for routine workers, sometimes a different family for the reviewer so that its mistakes differ from the writer's. This course runs every agent on qwen3:14b, so the effect of the role is not mixed up with the effect of the model. Chapter 16 measures what changes when workers run with cheaper settings.

Role drift

Specialists did not stay in their lanes. Of the role-only specialists' findings, 32 of 58 were about an issue from another lane. A "security reviewer" reported the off-by-one error, and a "performance reviewer" reported the SQL injection. Often they relabelled it to fit, as in "security: off-by-one error in the loop range". With the checklist it fell to 19 of 46, still four in ten. So part of the specialists' gain is not expertise. It is three differently angled looks at the same code, and duplicates the merge step has to remove. If lanes matter, for privileges or for a clean report, enforce them in code: drop or re-route findings outside the role instead of trusting the prompt.

Use case: role-based agent frameworks

Frameworks such as CrewAI and MetaGPT are built around roles. Each agent gets a role, a goal and a backstory, and the software-company systems in the case studies at the end of this course give agents job titles such as product manager, architect, engineer and tester. MetaGPT's own authors credit much of its result to standard operating procedures and the structured documents passed between roles, not to the titles alone. That matches this chapter. A role's checklist did more than its name, and the name alone did not keep the agent in its lane.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • Comparing specialists with a plain generalist instead of one given the same checklist.
  • Trusting the role to keep agents in their lanes: 32 of 58 role-only findings were out of lane.
  • Paying for three passes on small inputs where one pass already finds everything.
  • Merging specialists' findings without deduplicating: the same issue arrives under three labels.
  • Giving every role every tool, so a role is only a name.
  • Changing the role and the model at once, so you cannot tell which helped.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What does giving an agent a role actually change?

How a strong answer sounds
Mostly what it pays attention to, not what it knows. In a code-review test, a generalist given a checklist of what to look for went from 7 to 10 of 12 planted issues; splitting that checklist into three specialist agents took it to 12, at 2.4 times the tokens. On short snippets every set-up found 11 or 12, so the split bought nothing there. And roles leak: about half the specialists' findings were from other lanes.

Deeper: Your team has a security agent that must only touch security issues. How do you make sure?

How a strong answer sounds
Not with the prompt alone: in a test, 19 of 46 findings from specialists with a checklist, and 32 of 58 with only a role, were about other kinds of issue, often relabelled to fit. I would enforce the lane in code: give the agent only security tools, require a typed category, and filter or route anything outside it in the merge step. I would also check the lane in evaluation, not just the total found.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • A role prompt mixes identity with instructions; test it against a generalist with the same instructions.
  • On short snippets every set-up found 11 or 12 of 12; roles mainly cut false alarms (4 → 0).
  • On longer modules: generalist 7, with checklist 10, specialists with the checklist 12 of 12.
  • The split cost 2.4 times the tokens and nearly twice the time on one GPU.
  • Roles leak: 32 of 58 role-only findings were from another lane. Enforce lanes in code.
