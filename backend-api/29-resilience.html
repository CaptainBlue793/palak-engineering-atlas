Timeouts, Retries & Circuit Breakers

Every service depends on others, and every one of them will, at some point, be slow or down. The question is what your service does then. Without protection, a dependency that hangs makes your requests hang, your workers and connections fill up, and its outage becomes yours. With the wrong protection, retries multiply the load on the struggling dependency and keep it down. Resilience is a small set of patterns, applied together, so that a failing dependency degrades one feature instead of taking down the whole service.

This chapter covers timeouts, retries with backoff and jitter, retry budgets, circuit breakers and graceful degradation. The lab runs a product page that calls a recommendations service, which hangs for three seconds in the middle of the test, under four policies.

  • Put a timeout on every network call, within an end-to-end deadline
  • Retry only safe failures, with exponential backoff and jitter
  • Cap retries with a budget so they cannot multiply load
  • Fail fast with a circuit breaker while a dependency recovers
  • Degrade gracefully with fallbacks and bulkheads

Timeouts everywhere

A call without a timeout can wait for ever, and a dependency that hangs, rather than failing quickly, is the most dangerous failure because nothing reports an error: requests just pile up, each holding a worker slot, a connection and memory. Every network call needs a timeout: connect timeouts of about a second, read timeouts set from the dependency's normal latency (a few times its p99, not thirty seconds), and an overall deadline for the request that each downstream call fits within, so a caller that will give up after 2 seconds never waits 5 for a sub-call. Set them explicitly: httpx.AsyncClient(timeout=0.3); many clients, including Python's requests, have no timeout by default.

Retries with jitter

Retry failures that are likely to be transient, timeouts, connection errors, 502, 503, 504, 429 with Retry-After, and only for operations that are safe to repeat: reads, and writes made idempotent (Chapter 15). Space retries with exponential backoff and random jitter (Chapter 20), and keep the total within the caller's deadline. Never retry at every layer: three layers each trying three times turn one failure into 27 calls to the bottom service. Retry in one place, usually closest to the caller that knows the deadline.

Retry budgets

Retries help when failures are rare and random, and harm when a dependency is overloaded, since every retry is extra load at the worst moment. A retry budget caps retries as a fraction of normal traffic, for example "retries may add at most 10% to the calls this client makes", tracked with a token bucket that ordinary successful calls refill. When most calls are failing, the budget runs out and the client stops retrying, which is exactly the behaviour a struggling service needs from its callers. gRPC's retry throttling and several service meshes implement this.

Circuit breakers

A circuit breaker wraps calls to one dependency and watches their outcomes. Closed: calls go through. After a run of failures, it opens: calls fail immediately, without touching the network, for a cool-down period, so callers stop waiting and the dependency stops receiving traffic it cannot handle. Then it goes half-open: one trial call is allowed; success closes the breaker, failure opens it again. Breakers turn slow failure into fast failure and give a sick service room to recover. Keep one breaker per dependency (and per operation if they fail independently), and pair it with a fallback. The lab's breaker is a few lines:

class CircuitBreaker:
    """Closed: calls go through. Five failures in a row: open for 1 s, failing fast. Then half-open: one trial call."""
    def __init__(self, threshold=5, cooldown=1.0):
        self.threshold, self.cooldown, self.failures, self.opened_at, self.state = threshold, cooldown, 0, 0.0, "closed"

    def allow(self):
        if self.state == "open" and time.time() - self.opened_at >= self.cooldown:
            self.state = "half-open"
            return True
        return self.state != "open"

    def record(self, ok):
        if ok:
            self.failures, self.state = 0, "closed"
        else:
            self.failures += 1
            if self.state == "half-open" or self.failures >= self.threshold:
                self.state, self.opened_at = "open", time.time()

Simulator: a dependency hangs for three seconds

A product page calls a recommendations service. A client loads the page 20 times a second for 8 seconds; from second 2 to second 5, the recommendations service hangs for 5 seconds on every call. The page runs one of four policies: no timeout; a 300 ms timeout with a fallback list of bestsellers; that plus 3 attempts with jittered backoff; and that plus a circuit breaker that opens after five failures in a row for one second. Each dot is one page load: when it was sent, how long it took, and what the user got.

Both services are FastAPI on uvicorn; load from an httpx async client, 160 page loads per run. "Calls to recommendations during the outage" counts requests the dependency received between seconds 2 and 5. The fallback is a static bestseller list marked "degraded": true.

Things to try.

  • no timeout: every page during the outage took 5 seconds, because the page waited as long as the dependency did. Every request "succeeded", and every user waited.
  • timeout 300 ms + fallback: the same outage costs each user a third of a second and shows bestsellers instead of personal recommendations.
  • + 3 attempts: now each failing page makes up to three calls, so the hanging service received about two and a half times as many calls during the outage, and users waited around a second for the same fallback. Retries made things worse for everyone.
  • + circuit breaker: after five failures the breaker opened and pages got the fallback in a few milliseconds; the dependency received about half as many calls as with retries alone, mostly the failures before the breaker opened and one trial each second, and live recommendations returned as soon as it recovered.

Graceful degradation

Decide in advance what each feature does when its dependency is unavailable. Recommendations fall back to bestsellers; a price comes from a cache that may be a few minutes old; a non-essential widget is simply left out; writes are queued for later (Chapter 20). Mark degraded responses so clients and dashboards can tell. Isolate dependencies from each other with bulkheads, separate connection pools and concurrency limits per dependency (Chapter 28), so a slow recommendations service cannot use up the connections that checkout needs. Some dependencies have no fallback, such as the payment provider for a payment; there, fail fast with a clear error and let the user retry.

Use case: the EBS re-mirroring storm, April 2011

On 21 April 2011 a routine network change in one availability zone of AWS's US East region sent traffic for the Elastic Block Store, the service that provides disk volumes to virtual machines, onto a network that could not carry it. Many storage nodes lost contact with the replicas of their data, and each one, following its design, immediately searched for a new place to copy its data and began re-mirroring. Thousands of nodes did this at once, exhausting the free capacity in the cluster; nodes that could not find space kept retrying aggressively, adding more load. AWS's post-mortem called it a "re-mirroring storm". The cluster was stuck, volumes in that zone were unavailable for many hours, and some recovery took days, affecting the many companies running there. AWS's fixes included changing the retry logic to back off and making nodes stop searching when capacity was unavailable. It is the lab's retry policy at the scale of a cloud region: retries that are reasonable one at a time become an attack on the system when everyone makes them at once.

⚠️
Common mistakes
  • HTTP clients without timeouts, including requests' default.
  • Timeouts longer than the caller is willing to wait.
  • Retrying non-idempotent calls, or 4xx errors that will fail again.
  • Retries at every layer, multiplying load during outages.
  • No jitter, so failed clients retry in synchronised waves.
  • Fallback code that has never been run until the outage.
  • One shared pool for every dependency, letting one slow service starve the rest.

Interview drill

Explain it simply: What is a circuit breaker and why is it useful?

How a strong answer sounds
It is a wrapper around calls to a dependency that tracks failures. While things are normal it is closed and calls go through. If too many fail, it opens: for a cool-down period every call fails immediately, without waiting for a timeout and without sending traffic to the struggling service. Then it lets a trial call through; if that works it closes again, otherwise it reopens. That turns slow failure into fast failure for users, usually with a fallback such as cached or generic data, and it stops callers from piling load onto a service that is trying to recover. In an experiment where a dependency hung for three seconds, pages went from waiting five seconds, or about a second with retries, to a few milliseconds with the breaker.

Deeper: A checkout page calls pricing, inventory, recommendations and payment. Design its resilience.

How a strong answer sounds
Start from a deadline for the whole request, say 2 seconds, and give each call a timeout that fits inside it. Classify the dependencies: payment and inventory are essential, pricing is essential but cacheable, recommendations are optional. Recommendations get a short timeout, no retries, a circuit breaker and a fallback, or are simply omitted. Pricing gets a timeout, one retry with jitter if time allows, and a fallback to a recently cached price with a policy for honouring it. Inventory gets a timeout and a breaker; if it is down, we either accept the order and reconcile later or fail clearly, a business decision. Payment calls carry an idempotency key, have a timeout and at most a carefully bounded retry; on timeout the order goes into a pending state and a background job confirms the result rather than charging again. Separate connection pools and concurrency limits per dependency act as bulkheads, retries are budgeted and happen only at this layer, and every fallback is exercised regularly, for example with fault injection in staging.

🧠 Key takeaways

  • Every network call has a timeout that fits the caller's deadline.
  • Retry only transient failures of idempotent operations, with backoff, jitter and a budget, in one layer.
  • Circuit breakers turn slow failure into fast failure and let dependencies recover.
  • Plan and test a fallback for every optional dependency; mark degraded responses.
  • Bulkheads keep one slow dependency from starving the others.
